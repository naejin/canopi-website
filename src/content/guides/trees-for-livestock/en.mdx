Animals and trees on the same ground is one of the oldest ways to farm. Eric Toensmeier counts it among the systems that store the most carbon on grazing land (see [Carbon farming](/guides/carbon-farming/)); he cites estimates that pasture with trees can hold up to about three times the carbon of open pasture. This guide covers the main ways trees feed animals, and the dangers that come with them.

<QuickAnswer>
Trees feed animals three ways: shade over pasture, leaves and pods the animals reach or you knock down, and shrubs you cut and carry to them. Plan them so something is ready in the lean season, keep stock off young trees, and know which fodder plants are poisonous.
</QuickAnswer>

## Trees in pasture

Trees and grazing managed together on one piece of ground is <Term id="silvopasture">silvopasture</Term>. The trees are spaced much wider than in a wood, like a savanna. Some farms plant double rows of trees with wide, sunny grazing alleys between; dense plantings can be grazed only while young. Sow grasses and legumes that cope with some shade.

Dense shade starves a pasture, but moderate shade often makes it grow more than full sun, the author reports, especially in the tropics. Shade spares animals the worst heat, and trees even out the temperature below them, so the grazing season runs longer.

The best-known example is the oak pasture of Spain and Portugal (dehesa, montado): holm and cork oaks spread over grass where sheep, pigs and cattle graze. Grazing under coconut palms is common across Asia and the Pacific.

## Fodder trees

Some trees feed the animals directly. Their leaves, nuts, acorns or pods drop when ripe or are knocked down with poles, and the animals do the harvesting.

Leaves carry stock through a dry season. Branches are lopped and thrown down to the animals, usually from trees kept as <Term id="pollard">pollards</Term>, so the regrowth stays out of reach. [Trees with edible leaves](/guides/trees-with-edible-leaves/#keep-them-low) shows how the cuts work.

A <Term id="nitrogen-fixer">nitrogen fixer</Term> feeds the pasture as well as the herd. In Africa, the apple-ring acacia (*Faidherbia albida*) drops its leaves in the rainy season, when crops need the light, and gives pods and leaves in the dry season. The author reports that farmers who add it can keep more animals. See [Trees among field crops](/guides/trees-in-the-field/) for this tree in fields.

## Cut and carry: fodder banks

A <Term id="fodder-bank">fodder bank</Term> is a dense planting of fodder shrubs, kept low by <Term id="coppice">coppicing</Term>, whose branches you cut and carry to animals in a pen or stall. The manure stays where you can gather it for crops, and no hooves trample the planting. It suits a small farm: the book shows Rose Koech, a Kenyan dairy farmer, cutting legume shrubs from her fodder bank for her cows. Among the plants the book names for fodder banks are perennial pigeon pea and moringa <Invasive /> (more on moringa in [Trees with edible leaves](/guides/trees-with-edible-leaves/)).

Trees also give feed you can store. <Term id="tree-hay">Tree hay</Term>, leafy branches cut and dried for when pasture is short, was once common across Europe. Dried pods and nuts, such as carob, mesquite, acorns and chestnuts ([Nuts as a staple](/guides/nut-staples/)), keep too. Hay fields, the author notes, often run down when they are cut year after year and nothing goes back. Fodder trees give you a second store that does not depend on them. Bamboo, willow and poplar leaves are fodder as well: see [Coppice, willow and bamboo](/guides/coppice-and-bamboo/).

## Insects as feed

Some trees feed insects that feed animals. Silkworms eat only mulberry leaves and their close kin, and the worms can go to poultry.

## Keeping young trees alive

Animals among trees can do harm. They damage young trees and crops and dig holes; sheep have wrecked drip irrigation in nut groves, the book notes. Its remedies: short grazing spells, movable electric fencing, guards around young trees, and the right animal for the planting, such as poultry under older trees. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Follow the maker's safety instructions for electric fences, especially near children.

A <Term id="green-corral">green corral</Term>, a hedge of spiny or unpalatable plants, keeps stock out while young trees establish: see [Thorny and poisonous](/guides/windbreaks-and-living-fences/#thorny-and-poisonous). A <Term id="living-barn">living barn</Term> of windbreaks and small woods shelters animals from cold wind.

## Poisonous and invasive fodder plants

A badly planned system can poison the animals it feeds. Three warnings from the book:

<Safety title="Leucaena">
Leucaena <Invasive /> leaves contain mimosine, a toxin. The book treats leucaena as feed mainly for cattle, sheep and goats, and only as part of their diet: too much harms them. It also appears on international lists of the most harmful invasive plants. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Ask a local livestock adviser before you feed it.
</Safety>

<Safety title="Mesquite pods">
Animals fed only on mesquite <Invasive /> pods for long periods can fall ill, and some have died. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Ask a vet or livestock adviser before making the pods a main feed.
</Safety>

Jatropha leaves are toxic to stock, and cattle have choked on Osage orange <Invasive /> fruit: both are covered under [Thorny and poisonous](/guides/windbreaks-and-living-fences/#thorny-and-poisonous).

The fast nitrogen-fixing fodder trees are the ones most likely to escape. Start with fodder trees native to your region, and check every newcomer against your local invasive list.

## Grazing and carbon: what is known

Toensmeier sets out both sides of a heated debate. Planned rotational grazing has passionate advocates, yet he finds little peer-reviewed support for its biggest carbon claims: reviews range from gains to losses, with little effect in dry grasslands, and results vary with soil and grass.

His own reading:

- The manager's skill matters more than the rotation scheme.
- Feeding pasture with nitrogen fertiliser always added to emissions in the study he cites.
- Methane from the animals can cancel the carbon the soil gains. In one Colombian study, better grass alone came out no better than degraded pasture once the extra cattle's emissions were counted; adding leucaena turned the pasture into a net store.
- Stop feeding animals grain from annual crops, and feed them from perennials instead.

## For your climate

The principles hold everywhere; the trees do not. A few the book names:

<ForClimate zones="tropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
- **Guásimo** (*Guazuma ulmifolia*): a pasture tree whose fruit feeds stock.
- **Leucaena** <Invasive />: the classic shrub of intensive silvopasture in Australia and Colombia, only with the warning above.
- **Perennial pigeon pea**: a nitrogen-fixing shrub for a cut-and-carry fodder bank.
- **Coconut**: grazing under the palms keeps the ground clear.
- **Breadfruit**: its fruit is fed to pigs and poultry.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="subtropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Mild winters with light frost.

- **Carob**: sweet pods that animals have eaten for millennia.
- **Bamboo**: its leaves feed cattle, sheep and goats.

**For the tropical highlands:** chachafruto, a nitrogen-fixing pod tree grown from live stakes as a fodder bank.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="mediterranean" source="carbon-farming-solution">
- **Holm oak and cork oak**: the acorn pastures of Spain and Portugal.
- **Carob**: pods for stock, stored dry.

Sow grasses that take some shade beneath them.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="arid" source="carbon-farming-solution">
For dry places: the book's arid and dry semi-arid lands.

- **Apple-ring acacia**: pods and leaves in the dry season.
- **Gum arabic** (*Acacia senegal*): a nitrogen fixer whose pods make excellent fodder.
- **Mesquite** <Invasive />: in India and parts of Africa, wild stands are thinned to scattered trees over pasture, with saltbush (*Atriplex*) beside them. Heed the mesquite warning.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="temperate" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "warm temperate", which includes cool-summer places such as southern Britain.

- **Honey locust** <Invasive />: pods drop into the pasture; choose grafted sweet-podded varieties.
- **Oaks and chestnuts**: acorns and nuts for pigs.
- **Poplars and willows**: pollarded for summer leaf fodder, as in Europe for centuries.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="continental" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "cold temperate".

- **Honey locust** <Invasive />: several sweet-podded varieties take hard winters.
- **Poplars and willows**: pollards for tree hay.
- **Siberian pea shrub**: fodder and windbreak in one.

In Wisconsin, Mark Shepard grazes animals among rows of nut and fruit trees, a system the book calls restoration agriculture.
</ForClimate>
