In parts of the humid tropics, the day's starch comes from a tree or a clump rather than a field: in central Africa, the book notes, bananas are the main staple. This guide follows Eric Toensmeier's *The Carbon Farming Solution* and treats them as everyday food, not dessert.

<QuickAnswer>
In the warm tropics, perennials can supply the starch that grain or potatoes give elsewhere. Breadfruit, bananas and plantains, and peach palm bear starchy fruit; sago, enset and agave store it in their trunks; air potato <Invasive /> hangs tubers on its vines. Plant several varieties, harvest clumps a stem at a time so the plant lives on, and grow air potato only as a proven edible clone: the wild plant is poisonous and one of the world's worst invaders. Deserts have agave; cooler places should look to [nuts](/guides/nut-staples/) instead.
</QuickAnswer>

## Which plants give starch?

<Points>
- **Starchy fruit you pick**: breadfruit, unripe jakfruit, bananas and plantains, peach palm.
- **Starch stored in a trunk**: palms, banana relatives and agaves that build up starch for years before they flower.
- **Tubers on a vine**: air potato <Invasive /> grows its tubers in the air, a root crop that needs no digging.
</Points>

All of them can be a <Term id="staple-crop">staple crop</Term>, eaten day in, day out. Start with natives or plants long grown in your region, and check your local invasive list before planting anything from elsewhere, as the book urges. Starchy nuts such as chestnut and ramon are in [Nuts as a staple](/guides/nut-staples/).

## Trees you pick from

**Breadfruit** comes in hundreds of varieties. Choose several that ripen at different times and you can pick through much of the year, the book says, though only a handful of varieties have ever left the Pacific. Trees start bearing three to six years after planting, the author reports. The fruit bruises easily and keeps poorly, so pick by hand and use it soon.

In a <Term id="homegarden">homegarden</Term>, a breadfruit tree carries yam vines as a living trellis and shades taro, ginger and cassava beneath it, while spare fruit goes to pigs and poultry. See [Food forests](/guides/food-forest/). Its relative jakfruit is eaten unripe as a starchy vegetable; the seeds carry a warning in [Nuts as a staple](/guides/nut-staples/#nuts-that-need-care).

**Peach palm** is a clumping palm of the American tropics whose fruit was once as important there as maize and cassava, the book reports. The fruit spoils fast, so it is traditionally dried into flour or otherwise preserved. Its tall trunks are covered in spines (see below), but breeders have dwarf, spineless forms that are far easier to pick. It also shades cacao, holds up pepper vines and breaks the wind.

## Clumps you cut stem by stem

Bananas and plantains are one <Term id="hybrid-swarm">hybrid swarm</Term> descended from two wild species. Kinds closer to the first tend to be sweet dessert bananas; kinds closer to the second tend to be firmer cooking plantains, which also stand wind and longer dry seasons better. They are quick: a first bunch some 12 to 18 months from planting, the book reports.

A banana is a <Term id="managed-multistem">managed multistem</Term> plant: the clump keeps sending up new stems, so you harvest stems and keep the clump. Each stem fruits once and is then cut, so that a younger one takes over. {/* general tip, not from the book: each stem fruits once */}

- **Shade**: they take a fair amount and grow under breadfruit, avocado, coconut and macadamia, and can shade coffee themselves.
- **Food and water**: hungry and thirsty; give rich soil and steady water. Leaves, stalks and fruit all feed livestock.
- **Frost**: it kills the top, but after a light, brief frost the plant can sprout again and fruit within the year, the book says.
- **Diversity**: the world's export banana was once a single variety, wiped out by a soil-borne disease; its replacement now faces a new strain. Most bananas set no viable seed, so home growers protect themselves with several varieties.

## Starch stored in trunks

Some palms, banana relatives and agaves store starch for years, then spend it all in one great flowering. In Mesoamerica, Africa and Oceania, people each learned that the store is fullest just before the plant flowers, and harvest then.

Cutting a single-trunked palm kills it. The book profiles only kinds that live on: clumps where you take one trunk and leave the others (sago), and plants that sprout again from the base (enset, agave). Before trunk starch is food, it has to be separated from the fibre: done by hand for sago and painstakingly by hand for enset. The book does not teach it, and neither do we.

- **Sago** is a clumping palm of hot freshwater swamps. It takes years to establish, then gives trunk after trunk.
- **Enset**, the Ethiopian banana relative, is a living larder: families harvest it when other crops fail, and a cut plant sprouts many new shoots. The ornamental enset sold in garden centres elsewhere is not the food crop, and it dies after flowering.
- **Agaves** are desert succulents whose starchy hearts were once a staple in the driest parts of Mesoamerica. Not every species is usable: some are bitter, some too small. Each plant flowers once, after about five to ten years, so growers plant a few every year to always have some ready, the book says. They cut when the plant swells or as the flower stalk is just starting, and offsets at the base take its place.

<Safety title="Spines">
Sago and peach palms have very spiny trunks, and agave leaves end in hard spines. Wear thick gloves and eye protection when you work near them, and keep them away from paths and play areas. The same spines make them good barrier hedges: see [Windbreaks and living fences](/guides/windbreaks-and-living-fences/).
</Safety>

## Air potato: edible clones only

Air potato <Invasive /> is a yam that bears its tubers on the vine, as <Term id="bulbil">bulbils</Term>. They ripen over several months, drop when ready, need no digging, and keep for months. It needs a strong trellis or sturdy living trees: at a Mexican farm the book follows, edible clones grow well on established <Term id="nitrogen-fixer">nitrogen fixers</Term>. The vines wither when winter or the dry months arrive.

**The warning.** Wild air potato <Invasive /> is usually poisonous to people, and it ranks on global lists of the most harmful invasive plants: it climbs into the canopy and smothers forests. The edible forms are far less aggressive and seldom survive in the wild, the author reports. In his view, the plant's native range, from Australia across Asia to Africa, is wide enough for it to feed many people without spreading any farther.

So plant only a named edible <Term id="clone">clone</Term> from a trusted source, never a tuber taken from a wild vine, and check your local invasive list first. Where the plant is banned, do not grow it.

## Pick starch plants for your climate

These crops need heat, or in the case of agave, drought. Elsewhere, look to [Nuts as a staple](/guides/nut-staples/) instead.

<ForClimate zones="tropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Hot all year: the book's "humid lowland tropics" unless noted.

- **Breadfruit**: only where it is hot all year; even mildly cool winters are too cold for it, the book says.
- **Plantains**: the hardier end of the banana family, for windy places or longer dry seasons.
- **Peach palm**: a long-lived clump for the humid tropics; near paths, plant the spineless dwarf kinds.
- **Sago**: for wet, freshwater ground where few staples grow, up to the landward edge of mangroves.
- **Air potato** <Invasive />: edible clones only, on sturdy trees, where it is native and not banned.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="subtropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Mild winters with light frost.

- **Air potato** <Invasive />: the book says it reaches well into the subtropics and survives light frost. Edible clones only.
- **Jakfruit**: the book gives it humid tropical and subtropical climates; its unripe fruit is the starch.

For the tropical highlands (the book's own category):

- **Enset**: for humid highlands; it rides out long droughts.
- **Highland bananas**: some varieties grow well up into the mountains.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="arid" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Deserts and dry steppe: the book's "arid to semi-arid" climates.

- ***Agave salmiana***: a large agave with a starchy heart, for dry country from warm temperate to tropical.
- **Parry's agave**: once grown in Mexico and the dry North American southwest; the book lists it even for climates with very hard winters.
- **Maguey** (*Agave americana*) <Invasive />: naturalised in drylands worldwide. Plant it only where it is native.

Agaves also make spiny living fences and, in Mexico, the living edges of terraces.
</ForClimate>
