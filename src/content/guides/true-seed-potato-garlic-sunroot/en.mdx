Can you grow potatoes, garlic and sunroots (Jerusalem artichokes) from seed instead of tubers and cloves? Yes, with two conditions: you need the few plants that still make seed, and you need a few years of patient selection. This guide shows how to find those plants, raise their seedlings and keep the best.

## Why grow them from seed

Planting a tuber or a clove works, season after season. Each one grows into a <Term id="clone">clone</Term>, a copy of its parent. That is the catch, in Joseph Lofthouse's view: every plant of a variety, in every garden, is the same plant, so a pest or disease that gets past one can get past them all.

A seed is a new mix of its two parents. Sow a bed of <Term id="true-seed">true seed</Term> and you get a bed of different plants: some fail where others hold. For Lofthouse, that is the reason to grow these crops from seed.

## Find plants that still make seed

Most potatoes, garlic and sunroots on sale never set seed, each for its own reason. Sunroots are <Term id="self-incompatible">self-incompatible</Term>: one variety cannot fertilise itself. Garlic has been grown from cloves for so long that most of it has lost the habit, and its flower head also fills with <Term id="bulbil">bulbils</Term>, small clone bulbs that can crush the flower stalks as they swell.

| Crop | Where to start |
|---|---|
| Sunroot | Plant several unrelated sunroots together; they pollinate each other and seed heavily |
| Potato | Buy true potato seed from a specialist supplier, or grow many varieties and keep the few that set berries with seed that grows (Lofthouse found his fertile potatoes this way) |
| Garlic | Find seed-setting stock; Lofthouse works with garlic from wild Central Asian ancestors |

{/* general tip, not from the book: this paragraph */}
Tubers sold under different names may all be one clone. Look for seed-grown or wild-derived stock to be sure your sunroots are truly unrelated.

Lofthouse also advises against using commercial potato hybrids as parents: some carry a form of male sterility that their offspring inherit.

## Collect the seed

**Potato.** In summer, mark the plants that form green berries where the flowers were, and keep replanting tubers of those varieties. Getting the seed out of the berries is not covered here; bought seed is the simpler start.

<FoodCaution risk={["potato-berries", "treated-seed"]} />

**Garlic.** A few plants set seed with their bulbils left in: mark those. On the rest, clear the bulbils so the flowers have room.

<Steps compact>
  <Step title="Wait">Let the head open until the flowers show.</Step>
  <Step title="Shake">Jostle the head, and favour plants whose bulbils drop off easily, as Lofthouse does.</Step>
  <Step title="Thin">Just after the flowers open, pick out the bulbils that are left and leave the flowers.</Step>

**Sunroot.** Birds eat the seed, so cut the heads soon after the petals drop, or tie a mesh bag over them and leave them longer.

## Sow and select

{/* general tip, not from the book: this paragraph */}
Expect most seedlings to be worse than the plant they came from. That is normal. Grow plenty, look hard, and keep only a few. The variety among them is the point: it is what lets you find plants that suit your garden, and it is what a clone lacks.

**Garlic.** Lofthouse has had most success sowing thickly outdoors over winter. Expect few seedlings at first (around 5% in his account), and more as you go on from seed. If a seedling comes up without a winter, mark it: he favours those. The best seedlings can be grown on from cloves straight away.

**Sunroot.** Let the seedlings cross among themselves, keep the best each generation, and grow the chosen ones on from tubers.

What to look for first, crop by crop:

- **Potato:** plants that set berries with seed that grows. Fertility comes before everything else.
- **Garlic:** bulbils that drop out easily, and seed that sprouts without a cold spell.
- **Sunroot:** large, smooth tubers on plants that thrive in your soil.

{/* general tip, not from the book: this paragraph */}
Judge seedlings by how they grow and what they yield, never by tasting them. A seedling potato is a new cross whose make-up nobody knows.
