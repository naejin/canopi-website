Can you grow potatoes, garlic and sunroots (Jerusalem artichokes) from seed instead of tubers and cloves? Yes, with two conditions: you need the few plants that still make seed, and you need a few years of patient selection. This guide shows how to find those plants, raise their seedlings and keep the best.

<CropCards crops={["potato", "garlic", "sunroot"]} />

## Why grow them from seed

Planting a tuber or a clove works, season after season. Each one grows into a <Term id="clone">clone</Term>, a copy of its parent. That is the catch, in Joseph Lofthouse's view: every plant of a variety, in every garden, is the same plant, so a pest or disease that gets past one can get past them all. He sees the same danger in clonal tree crops, and names the Cavendish banana as one.

A seed is a new mix of its two parents. Sow a bed of <Term id="true-seed">true seed</Term> and you get a bed of different plants: some fail where others hold. For Lofthouse, that is the reason to grow these crops from seed.

These are slow projects. The crop cards above give his ease ratings: hard for potato, very hard for garlic and sunroot. Start here if you enjoy breeding over several seasons.

## Potato

Can you grow potatoes from the seed in their green berries? Yes, if your plants make berries; growers call that seed "true potato seed". Most varieties on sale are sterile clones and never do, so the first job is a search.

- **Trial widely.** Lofthouse found his fertile potatoes by growing many varieties, looking for the few whose berries held seed that grew. The more you try, the better your odds.
- **Mark the plants that set berries.** Look for them in summer, where the flowers were.
- **Keep the fertile ones going.** Replant tubers of those varieties. He stopped growing the ones that never fruited.
- **Or start from bought seed.** Specialist suppliers sell true potato seed, a simpler start than cleaning seed from berries, which this guide does not cover.

<FoodCaution risk="potato-berries" />

Lofthouse also advises against using commercial potato hybrids as parents for a landrace: some carry a form of male sterility that their offspring inherit.

## Garlic

Why does your garlic flower but make no seed? Two reasons, in Lofthouse's account. Most garlic has been grown from cloves for so long that it has lost the knack. And the flower head is shared with <Term id="bulbil">bulbils</Term>, small clone bulbs that swell, press together and can crush the flower stalks before seed forms.

### Which garlic to start with

The seed-setting garlic he works with comes from wild ancestors gathered in the mountains of Central Asia. Among the garden types, he found purple-stripe garlic, which he describes as closest to those ancestors, the most useful.

### Sowing the seed

Expect few seedlings at first. Lofthouse puts first-generation germination at around 5%, rising each generation as you keep seed from the plants that seed most readily. Sown outdoors over winter, his garlic seed has sprouted most reliably. Some sprouts without a cold spell, and he favours those plants: his long-term aim is a garlic you sow in spring, like onions.

The best seedlings can be kept as new clones and grown from cloves straight away. Further off, Lofthouse hopes the seed-grown plants will become a garlic landrace that crosses freely.

## Sunroot (Jerusalem artichoke)

### Getting seed

A single sunroot variety will not set seed: it is <Term id="self-incompatible">self-incompatible</Term>. Plant several unrelated sunroots together and they pollinate each other and seed heavily.

In his garden, birds go for the seed. Cut the heads soon after the petals drop, or tie a mesh bag over them so you can leave them longer.

### Raising and selecting

Lofthouse's project ran like this: a garden sunroot crossed with a wild one, then about 50 seedlings a year, left to cross with each other, keeping about 15% each time.

What to keep:

- **Large, smooth tubers.** The garden strain he began with was knobby, and soil lodges between the knobs. Smooth tubers were his main goal.
- **Plants that thrive for you.** Every seedling is a new variety; keep the ones your soil and weather suit.

Once you have winners, grow them from tubers like any sunroot. The bed keeps flowering and crossing, so you can start a new round of breeding whenever you like. He finds the clones he bred now suit his garden better than shop-bought ones.

## The seedling years

{/* general tip, not from the book: the first paragraph of this section */}
Expect most seedlings to be worse than the plant they came from. That is normal. Grow plenty, look hard, and keep only a few. The variety among them is the point: it is what lets you find plants that suit your garden, and it is what a clone lacks.

What to look for first, crop by crop:

- **Potato:** plants that set berries with seed that grows. Fertility comes before everything else.
- **Garlic:** bulbils that drop out easily, and seed that sprouts without a cold spell.
- **Sunroot:** large, smooth tubers on plants that thrive in your soil.
