Leaves, clippings and kitchen scraps: how do they become good compost, how hot should the heap get, and what must stay out? This guide follows Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis, for whom compost is less a fertiliser than a starter culture that carries soil life into a bed.

<QuickAnswer>
Build a heap of about a cubic metre in thin, moist layers of fresh green and dry brown material, turn it when it cools or overheats, and use it once nothing is recognisable and it smells like fresh soil. In the authors' view, more brown makes a fungal compost for trees and shrubs, more green a bacterial one for vegetables. Keep faeces, treated materials and diseased plants out, and never treat home compost as germ-free.
</QuickAnswer>

## What compost does for soil life

In the authors' system, compost is first a way to move life. Spread on the surface, its organisms work down into the soil and settle there; who they are is the subject of [Living soil](/guides/living-soil/). By their count, one teaspoon of good compost holds up to about a billion bacteria and 150 to 300 m (400 to 900 ft) of fungal <Term id="hyphae">threads</Term>: a sample of a whole <Term id="soil-food-web">soil food web</Term>, not a bag of nutrients.

And compost is not one product. What goes in decides whether fungi or bacteria end up in charge, and they match that to the plant ([Trees like fungi, vegetables like bacteria](/guides/fungal-or-bacterial/)).

## What goes in

A pile needs food, water, air and microbes; the heat comes from the microbes at work, not from the sun. Food comes in two kinds. **Green** is fresh and moist: clippings, pulled weeds, kitchen scraps (but no meat or fat). **Brown** is old, dry and woody: autumn leaves, straw, chips, twigs. The fresher a green, the more nitrogen it brings.

The balance is the <Term id="carbon-nitrogen-ratio">carbon-to-nitrogen ratio</Term>. The authors aim for about 25 to 30 parts carbon to one of nitrogen. Too much brown and decay stalls; too much green and the surplus nitrogen is lost.

| Material | Rough carbon-to-nitrogen ratio (the authors' figures) |
|---|---|
| Grass clippings | about 19:1 |
| Fallen leaves | 40:1 to 80:1 |
| Paper | about 170:1 |
| Sawdust | about 500:1 |

Clippings and leaves together land near the target: a lawn and a few trees already give you the mix.

Mind the size of the pieces: too fine packs down and shuts out air, too coarse lets in so much that the pile rots unevenly. Find the middle by trying.

## Fungal or bacterial compost

The authors steer a pile with one rule: more brown, more fungi; more green, more bacteria.

| Ingredient, by volume (the authors' recipes) | Fungal compost | Bacterial compost |
|---|---|---|
| Alfalfa meal | 5–10 % | 25 % |
| Fresh grass clippings | 45–50 % | 50 % |
| Brown leaves, small chips or bark | 40–50 % | 25 % |

Fungal compost suits woody plants and most perennials; bacterial compost suits vegetables, annual flowers and lawns. The authors' "lazy" pile is just brown leaves layered with alfalfa meal, or with clippings if you have no meal; shredded leaves work faster. Cool pile: add grass. Overheating: use less.

{/* general tip, not from the book: the dust and mould warning below */}
<Safety title="Dusty meals and mouldy material">
Dry meals such as alfalfa meal, and dry or mouldy material when you turn a pile, give off dust and mould spores. Handle them outdoors, keep your face out of the cloud, and wear a dust mask if you have asthma or a weak immune system.
</Safety>

<Steps>
<Step title="Pick the size">

At least about 1 m (3.5 ft) each way, or it will not hold its heat; the authors find about 1.8 m (6 ft) the most one person can turn.

</Step>
<Step title="Give it a floor that breathes">

A pallet or mesh raised on blocks lets air in from below; sticks laid through the pile do the same.

</Step>
<Step title="Layer green and brown">

Layers of 10 to 15 cm (4 to 6 in), green on brown, each watered lightly.

</Step>
<Step title="Water to a squeeze">

A squeezed handful should give a few drops, no more. Too wet: mix in dry brown or turn it. In a dry climate, dish the top to catch rain; in a wet one, cover the heap. Closed bins and drums tend to stay too wet.

</Step>
<Step title="Wilt fresh clippings first">

The authors let fresh clippings dry a little before adding them, so they do not mat and smell.

</Step>
</Steps>

Why turn? The useful decomposers are <Term id="aerobic">aerobic</Term>: they need air. A pile without air still rots, but it goes <Term id="anaerobic">anaerobic</Term>, and the authors say it then makes alcohols that harm roots even in tiny traces.

## Heat: what the pile is doing

A hot pile passes through three phases (the authors' figures):

- **Warm-up.** <Term id="mesophilic">Mesophilic</Term> organisms, at about 20 to 40 °C (68 to 104 °F), start on the softer material; some form spores that sit out the heat.
- **Hot phase.** Above about 40 °C (104 °F), <Term id="thermophilic">thermophilic</Term> bacteria and fungi take over. A well-mixed pile reaches about 57 °C (135 °F) within one to three days, and about 65 °C (150 °F) in its centre.
- **Maturing.** The heat falls, the spore-formers return, thread-forming bacteria finish the woody remains, and springtails, worms and beetles move in.

Their targets: hold the centre at about 60 to 65 °C (140 to 150 °F) for several days at least to kill disease organisms, 65 °C (150 °F) also killing weed seeds. Never above about 68 °C (155 °F), where, they say, carbon starts to burn off. After the first peak, about 40 to 55 °C (104 to 131 °F) until it matures.

{/* The gloves, hand-washing and kitchen advice in the next box is a general tip, not from the book. */}
<Safety title="Home compost is never germ-free">
A home pile heats unevenly: the edges and corners stay cooler than the centre, so some disease organisms and weed seeds can survive. Treat finished compost as garden material, not as something clean: wear gloves if you have cuts, wash your hands after handling it, and keep it away from food preparation.
</Safety>

To check, push a long metal rod into the centre and pull it out: it feels warm when things are working; a compost thermometer gives the number. In the authors' Alaska winters, a frozen pile restarts by itself in spring, warmed by bacteria that work just above freezing.

<Safety title="Don't reach into a hot pile">
The centre of a working pile is hot enough to scald. The authors say it is fine to push a hand in to feel the heat; use a long metal rod or a compost thermometer instead, and open a hot pile with a fork, not your hands.
</Safety>

## Turn, cool, warm

<Points ruled="accent">

- **Not heating, or cooling too soon?** Turn it, outside and top to the middle. Still cool: add fresh green, nitrogen-rich material. Too wet: add dry brown.
- **Too hot?** Turning cools it for a while. If it keeps climbing, the authors add brown, which feeds slower fungi rather than the heat-making bacteria. Watering cools too, but it is the drastic option.
- **Outside in, every time.** So every part gets its hot spell. In the authors' experience, two or three turns finish a pile.

</Points>

## Is it ready?

Finished compost is dark, crumbly and coffee-coloured, nothing in it is recognisable, and it no longer heats when turned. Then use your nose, and only your nose. Fresh soil: good. Sour, rotten, vinegary or like vomit: it went without air, so do not use it. Ammonia: not finished. Either way, open it up, wait a few days and smell again.

Or grow something in it: if the plants look starved, the authors read it as too few grazers releasing nutrients. Half-done material is what they call "almost compost": not ready yet. A biological lab test is the precise check.

## Cold heaps and worm bins

A **cold heap** is the easy way: pile material in a corner and leave it for a year or more. It never reaches the authors' target heat, so keep weed seeds and diseased plants out. In return it holds more worms, beetles and millipedes, and the authors always keep one going for that variety.

A **worm bin** makes <Term id="vermicompost">vermicompost</Term> in a plain box of wood or plastic. Feed it scraps (no meat or fat), paper, cardboard, leaves and shredded browns. With no hot phase, the result is strongly bacterial in the authors' view; hot-compost weedy material first, or the bin will sprout.

## What stays out

<Safety title="Keep pet and human faeces out">
Disease organisms in dog, cat or human faeces can survive even a hot pile. The authors also leave all manure out of their compost, because of drug residues and E. coli.
</Safety>

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Many gardeners compost manure; the authors' rule is simply stricter.

- **Treated materials.** Nothing sprayed with weedkiller, pesticide or fungicide, or fed synthetic fertiliser, including clippings from a treated lawn: residues can outlast composting and kill the microbes. If you buy compost, ask what went in.
- **Diseased plants and hard-to-kill weeds.** Wait until you can bring a pile to full heat and finish it reliably: half-done compost can still carry disease and live seed.

## Spread it where it counts

The authors find a thin layer enough: 0.5 to 2.5 cm (¼ to 1 in) of the matching compost around plants. Fungal compost goes under trees, shrubs and most perennials, out to the <Term id="drip-line">drip line</Term> and kept off trunks and stems; bacterial compost on vegetable beds and annual flowers, and thinly on a lawn. For a fresh start they go deeper: 2.5 to 5 cm (1 to 2 in) of bacterial compost before planting a bed that had years of chemical feeding, and the same depth of fungal compost under trees and shrubs.

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
On vegetable beds, keep compost on the soil rather than on leaves you will eat, and wash what you harvest.

Their claim: new life shows in the top 15 to 38 cm (6 to 15 in) within about six months, and about 46 cm (18 in) down after a year.

Then cover it with [mulch](/guides/mulch/). The liquid version is [compost tea](/guides/compost-tea/); for whole beds, see [Vegetable beds without digging](/guides/no-dig-beds/).
