Trees and other perennials can feed you and pull carbon out of the air at the same time. Doing both on purpose is <Term id="carbon-farming">carbon farming</Term>. This guide follows Eric Toensmeier's *The Carbon Farming Solution*, a global survey of perennial crops and the farming practices built around them, and opens a section of guides on growing them.

<QuickAnswer>
Plants take carbon from the air, and some of it stays: in wood for as long as the plant lives, and in the soil as dark organic matter. In Toensmeier's survey, farms keep the most carbon when they look most like a forest: trees and other perennials, harvested without being killed, mixed with crops or animals. Any garden can start: plant perennial food crops that suit your climate, natives first, and stop the digging, burning and bare ground that send the carbon back.
</QuickAnswer>

## Where the carbon goes

Leaves turn carbon dioxide into sugars, and the plant builds itself from them. That carbon ends up in two places you can manage, and keeping it there is <Term id="carbon-sequestration">carbon sequestration</Term>.

<Figure>
<CarbonStores alt="A tree above the soil line with its roots below. Numbered marks show carbon dioxide entering the leaves, carbon held in the trunk and roots, carbon held as dark crumbs in the soil, and, on a dug patch to the right, carbon escaping back to the air." />

1. **In.** Leaves take carbon dioxide from the air.
2. **Woody store.** Trunk, branches and large roots hold carbon for as long as the plant lives.
3. **Soil store.** Dead roots, fallen leaves and root sugars feed soil life; part of it stays as dark organic matter.
4. **Out again.** Digging, bare ground, fire and clearing let it go back to the air.
</Figure>

Both stores can be lost. Cut and burn a tree and its wood returns to the air; turn and expose the soil and its life burns through the stored <Term id="soil-organic-matter">organic matter</Term>. Toensmeier estimates that in tree-based farming about a third of the carbon sits above ground and two-thirds below, so the soil is the bigger store, and the easier one to lose.

Neither store grows forever. After some decades of good care a soil reaches <Term id="saturation">saturation</Term>, then holds steady for as long as the care goes on. These practices were never invented for carbon, he points out: farmers took them up for fertility, for soil that stays put, and for a harvest that survives a bad year. [Living soil](/guides/living-soil/) shows how to feed the soil store.

## What stores the most

Toensmeier compares many studies and ranks farming systems by how much carbon they add each year. His rule of thumb: the more woody plants, the more carbon. Layered gardens of trees and shrubs, from tropical <Term id="homegarden">homegardens</Term> to the food forests of cooler places, come out on top.

The ranking is the author's reading of thin and uneven data, and he warns that studies measure in different ways. Take it as an order, not as amounts.

## Five ways to harvest a perennial without killing it

A plant that survives its harvest leaves its roots, and the soil around them, undisturbed. Toensmeier's book is about these crops. A tree cut to the ground to regrow is a <Term id="coppice">coppice</Term>; a clump that gives up a few stems at a time is a <Term id="managed-multistem">managed multistem</Term>. Decide which form each plant will take before you plant it.

Each one keeps the soil store. Only the two standard forms and the multistem keep a steady woody store too; coppice and hay give theirs up at each cut and rebuild it, in the author's account. For the cuts themselves, see [Trees with edible leaves](/guides/trees-with-edible-leaves/#keep-them-low).

Note each plant's harvest form in your Canopi design.

## Plant what exists now

Some perennial <Term id="staple-crop">staples</Term>, the crops that fill a plate every day, are ready today; others are still being bred. Toensmeier's advice is to start with what farmers already grow.

<Points ruled="accent">
- **Now** Crops already grown somewhere: chestnut, hazel, olive, breadfruit, bananas.
- **In some years** Better varieties of those crops, and a few new ones.
- **In decades** Perennial grains and most crops still in the lab.
</Points>

Start with plants native to your region: every region has its own <Term id="nitrogen-fixer">nitrogen fixers</Term>, windbreak and biomass plants, he notes. Before you plant anything from elsewhere, check your local list of invasive plants.

Some of the oldest tree staples, acorns and olives among them, are not eaten as picked; nor are the seeds of breadfruit and its relatives. [Staple foods from perennials](/guides/perennial-staples/) names the crops that need care.

## Where to start in your climate

The principles above hold everywhere; the species do not. Toensmeier sorts climates by their coldest winters, and he says his categories are only a rough start. A few first plantings for each:

<ForClimate zones="tropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The humid tropics are furthest ahead, in the author's view: several tree staples there already yield as much as grain.

- **Breadfruit**: a starchy-fruit tree for hot, wet lowlands, and a tall layer to grow smaller crops under. Its seeds, where it has any, are not eaten as picked: see the caution above.
- **Bananas and plantains**: grown as a clump; you cut stems and the plant lives on.
- **Tahitian chestnut**: a nitrogen-fixing nut tree for wet lowlands that grows well in shade.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="subtropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Mild winters with light frost.

- **Avocado**: the Mexican types are the hardiest; an oil-rich fruit for the middle layer.
- **Jackfruit**: a breadfruit relative with large starchy fruit, for humid places. Its seeds are not eaten as picked: see the caution above.
- **Chestnut**: the book's range for it reaches into the subtropics.

**For the tropical highlands:** enset is a staple in Ethiopia's highlands, but the author warns that the ornamental enset sold in garden centres is not the food crop.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="mediterranean" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Winter rain and a dry summer: water decides what you plant.

- **Carob**: pods for flour and livestock; it needs a fair share of winter rain to set them.
- **Olive**: an oil crop that also takes coppicing. The fruit is not eaten from the tree: see the caution above.
- **Italian stone pine**: the highest-yielding pine nut, by the author's account, and at home in dry summers.

The oak pastures of Spain and Portugal are an old example of trees with animals.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="temperate" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book calls this "warm temperate", a band that includes cool-summer places such as southern Britain.

- **Chestnut**: the one starchy nut grown and traded worldwide, in the book's account; Europe has long grown it as coppice too.
- **Hazel**: bears every year, unlike many nut trees, and can be grown as a coppiced hedge.
- **Walnut**: grown in France with grain between its rows. Black walnut releases a substance that holds some neighbours back.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="continental" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "cold temperate": long frosts and warm summers. Prefer trees that drop their leaves in winter: see [Honest limits](#honest-limits).

- **Hazels**: hybrids bred for cold and disease.
- **Chestnut**: hardy kinds at the milder end of this zone.
- **Honey locust** <Invasive />: sweet pods for livestock. Choose grafted sweet-podded varieties: the ornamental kinds mostly bear thin, papery pods, and wild trees carry long spines.
</ForClimate>

## Honest limits

Toensmeier is frank about the drawbacks. These are his points, and they shape a good planting.

<Points>
- **Support plants cost yield** Nitrogen fixers, hedges and mulch plants take space that could grow food. On slopes, where bare soil washes away, they often pay back over time.
- **Years before a harvest** Some tree staples take years to bear, a few take decades. Grow annuals between young trees while you wait: see [the waiting years](/guides/perennial-staples/#years-before-the-first-harvest).
- **Water in drylands** Trees drink. Where water is short, his guideline is to plant no more trees than the place once carried, or to choose thrifty perennials such as cacti and succulents.
- **Never clear forest** Clearing healthy forest to plant releases more carbon than the planting will store, worst of all on peat. Plant on farmland, worn-out or treeless ground.
- **Trees in snowy places** A dark evergreen canopy soaks up heat that snow would reflect, lowering the ground's <Term id="albedo">albedo</Term>. In the far north he expects it to warm more than it stores; in colder temperate places it eats into the gain.
</Points>

If you farm, expect a few lean years: in his account most of these practices raise yields and income only after a dip. Farmers often start where annual crops struggle most, on slopes and dry ground.
