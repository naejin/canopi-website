Will your sweet corn cross with the popcorn next to it? How many plants do you need to save seed? Which sweet corn comes up in a cold spring? This guide answers those questions, then follows corn as a <Term id="landrace">landrace</Term> from laying out the blocks to shelling the seed.

<QuickAnswer>
Corn is pollinated by the wind and crosses readily, so it adapts fast. It also weakens when grown from too few plants: Lofthouse never keeps a variety on fewer than 200. A cross shows in the kernels the same season, so sweet corn pollinated by flour or pop corn gives starchy kernels. Keep those types apart, by distance or by sowing them to flower at different times. Old-fashioned sweet corn comes up in colder soil than the extra-sweet kinds.
</QuickAnswer>

## How corn crosses

The tassel crowning the plant sheds pollen, and the silks on the young ears catch it. The wind does the carrying, and most kernels end up with a father from another plant: corn is an <Term id="outcrossing">outcrossing</Term> crop.

<FlowerDiagram variant="corn" />

That suits a landrace, because the genes reshuffle every year. The catch is <Term id="inbreeding-depression">inbreeding depression</Term>: corn has a name for losing vigour when it is bred from too few parents. Lofthouse's floor for keeping a variety is 200 plants, a number handed down among corn growers rather than one he worked out.

Corn pollen is heavy. In his fields, where the wind averages about 16 km/h (10 mph), it falls below silk height within about 8 m (25 ft), and most crossing happens within about 1 m (3 ft). A storm can carry stray grains much further, but a few from afar count for little against the pollen of your own patch.

You can see crossing right away. Let one coloured seed slip into a white corn patch, and the white cobs around it carry coloured kernels that same autumn.

## Keeping types apart

Sweet corn suffers most from a cross: pollen from flour, flint or pop corn gives starchy kernels on its cobs. That is why Lofthouse keeps sweet corn and flour corn from mixing. You have three ways to keep types distinct.

**Blocks.** Sow each type or colour as a block, side by side, rather than in single long rows. Because most pollen lands close by, each block mostly pollinates itself, and crosses show up only along the edges where two blocks meet. He grows purple, white and yellow corn this way and finds only a few stray kernels on the shared edges. Take seed cobs from the middle of each block.

<PlotDiagram variant="colour-blocks" />

**Timing.** Varieties that shed pollen at different times can't cross, even side by side. This is <Term id="isolation-by-time">isolation by time</Term>, and it is how he grows sweet and flour corn in one field: one early, one late.

**Separate pools.** He once crossed hundreds of corn varieties together, then sorted the types back out. Now each type has its own pool. A new flour corn joins only the flour corn, so the soft-kernelled flour type stays at the centre of that population.

Plan your blocks in Canopi before you sow.

To get the best of two kinds, he crossed them. His 'Paradise' sweet corn has an old-fashioned mother, which gives it a strong seed coat, and a sugary-enhanced father, which adds sweetness. Making a corn hybrid is simple: you pull the tassels off the mother plants before they shed pollen, which is called <Term id="detasseling">detasseling</Term>.

### Dry corn

- **Flint:** hard, glassy, beautiful kernels that hold up best against insects and animals, but are hard on a grain mill and make gritty meal.
- **Flour:** soft kernels that grind easily into fine, light flour. Its reputation is as the food-security corn.
- **Pop:** closely related to flint. If you cross popcorn, choose a flint partner: selecting good popping back in is easier than after a cross with flour corn.
- **Dent:** one of the types in his mixed grain corn.

One mixed population can feed all the others. Lofthouse drew several of his corns, among them his flour corn and 'Andean Sweet', out of 'Harmony', a <Term id="hybrid-swarm">hybrid swarm</Term> where dent, flint, sweet, flour and pop grow unsorted, and which adapts quickly when conditions change.

## Selecting cobs

At harvest, pick seed cobs for whatever matters in your garden. Three traits Lofthouse has chosen for:

- **Standing up to animals.** Where pheasants took the cobs of short plants, he kept seed from tall ones; where skunks and raccoons raided, he kept the cobs they had left. Over the years the stalks grew stiffer, the ears higher, and the losses stopped.
- **Popping.** Working on popcorn, he test-popped 20 kernels from every cob each winter and replanted the cobs that did best, on both count and size.
- **Easy shelling.** In dry corn, he ranks kernels that rub off the cob readily among his top traits.

You can choose in two ways. He uses <Term id="mass-selection">mass selection</Term>. <Term id="sibling-group-selection">Sibling-group selection</Term> suits traits that run in families, such as easy shelling.

## Hidden sweet kernels

Sweetness can hide in grain corn. It is a <Term id="recessive-trait">recessive trait</Term>: a kernel is sweet only when it gets the sweet gene from both parents. A single gene separates sweet corn from grain corn, so it sorts out cleanly.

To pull a sweet corn out of your grain corn:

1. Look for wrinkled kernels on your dry flour or flint cobs. If there are none, cross in a sweet corn: the first-generation kernels all look starchy, and on the next cobs, the <Term id="f2">F2</Term>, about one kernel in four is sweet and dries wrinkled.
2. Pick out the wrinkled kernels and sow them away from other corn.
3. Keep sowing only the wrinkled kernels. Once selected, sweetness stays.

Lofthouse reads a lone wrinkled kernel as a sign that at least half the kernels on that cob carry hidden sweetness, and he pulled his tall, animal-hardy 'Andean Sweet' out of 'Harmony' this way.

## Special populations

Two of his projects, as his reports rather than proven results:

- **Deep orange flint.** He selected a deep orange flint out of 'Harmony'. The colour comes from a South American race with deep orange kernels.
- **Air-rooted corn.** After scientists reported nitrogen-fixing microbes on the above-ground roots of some corn, he selected a population rich in them. It grows tall in his unfertilised fields. Whether air roots help in your garden is unknown.

## Save the seed

<Steps>
  <Step title="Dry on the plant">Leave seed cobs on tall, healthy, undamaged plants until they are fully dry.</Step>
  <Step title="Pick from many">Take seed cobs from many plants, not just a favourite few, so the population stays wide.</Step>
  <Step title="Shell">Rub the kernels off the cob by hand. Easy-shelling cobs make this quick.</Step>
  <Step title="Store dry">Keep the kernels dry and cool, labelled.</Step>
</Steps>
