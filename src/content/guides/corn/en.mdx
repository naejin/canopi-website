Will your sweet corn cross with the popcorn next to it? How many plants do you need to save seed? Which sweet corn comes up in a cold spring? This guide answers those questions, then follows corn as a <Term id="landrace">landrace</Term> from laying out the blocks to shelling the seed.

<QuickAnswer>
Corn is pollinated by the wind and crosses readily, so it adapts fast. It also weakens when grown from too few plants: Lofthouse keeps to the old rule of at least 200. A cross shows in the kernels the same season, so sweet corn pollinated by flour or pop corn gives starchy kernels. Keep those types apart, by distance or by sowing them to flower at different times. Old-fashioned sweet corn comes up in colder soil than the extra-sweet kinds.
</QuickAnswer>

## How corn crosses

The tassel crowning the plant sheds pollen, and the silks on the young ears catch it. The wind does the carrying, and most kernels end up with a father from another plant: corn is an <Term id="outcrossing">outcrossing</Term> crop.

<FlowerDiagram variant="corn" />

That suits a landrace, because the genes reshuffle every year. The catch: corn is known to lose vigour when it is bred from too few parents, which is called <Term id="inbreeding-depression">inbreeding depression</Term>. Lofthouse follows the traditional advice to grow at least 200 plants to keep a corn variety going. A smaller patch is fine to try a variety; save seed from a big one.

Corn pollen is heavy. In his fields, where the wind averages about 16 km/h (10 mph), it falls below silk height within about 8 m (25 ft), and most crossing happens within about 1 m (3 ft). A storm can carry stray grains much further, but a few from afar count for little against the pollen of your own patch.

You can see crossing right away. Let one coloured seed slip into a white corn patch, and the white cobs around it carry coloured kernels that same autumn.

## Keeping types apart

Sweet corn suffers most from a cross: pollen from flour, flint or pop corn gives starchy kernels on its cobs. That is why Lofthouse keeps sweet corn and flour corn from mixing. You have three ways to keep types distinct.

**Blocks.** Sow each type or colour as a block, side by side, rather than in single long rows. Because most pollen lands close by, each block mostly pollinates itself, and crosses show up only along the edges where two blocks meet. He grows purple, white and yellow corn this way and finds only a few stray kernels on the shared edges. Take seed cobs from the middle of each block.

<PlotDiagram variant="colour-blocks" />

**Timing.** Varieties that shed pollen at different times can't cross, even side by side. This is <Term id="isolation-by-time">isolation by time</Term>, and it is how he grows sweet and flour corn in one field: one early, one late.

**Separate pools.** He once crossed hundreds of corn varieties together, then sorted the types back out. Now each type has its own pool. A new flour corn joins only the flour corn, so the soft-kernelled flour type stays at the centre of that population.

Plan your blocks in Canopi before you sow.

## Corn types

### Sweet corn

There are three kinds of sweet corn, plus a blend of all three. In a cold spring, what matters is whether the seed comes up.

To get the best of two kinds, he crossed them. His 'Paradise' sweet corn has an old-fashioned mother, which gives it a strong seed coat, and a sugary-enhanced father, which adds sweetness. Corn hybrids are easy to make: you pull the tassels off the mother plants before they shed pollen: <Term id="detasseling">detasseling</Term>.

### Dry corn

- **Flint:** hard, glassy, beautiful kernels that hold up best against insects and animals, but are hard on a grain mill and make gritty meal.
- **Flour:** soft kernels that grind easily into fine, light flour. Its reputation is as the food-security corn.
- **Pop:** closely related to flint. If you cross popcorn, choose a flint partner: selecting good popping back in is easier than after a cross with flour corn.
- **Dent:** one of the types in his mixed grain corn.

His most varied corn, 'Harmony', grows flour, sweet, pop, dent and flint together with no sorting by type. It is a <Term id="hybrid-swarm">hybrid swarm</Term>, and of all his corn it adapts fastest when conditions change. He selected his other corns out of it.

## Selecting cobs

There are two ways to choose seed. Lofthouse uses the first.

  mass={{ title: "Mass selection", text: "Sow in bulk, save seed from the plants that thrive, and repeat every year.", alt: "A bed of corn plants with the best few ringed, all their seed going into one jar." }}
  sibling={{ title: "Sibling groups", text: "Sow a handful of seeds per cob as a small group, then keep or cull the whole group.", alt: "Four cobs, A to D, each sown as a small group of plants; groups B and D are ticked and go to the jar, A and C are crossed out." }}
/>

The glossary names them <Term id="mass-selection">mass selection</Term> and <Term id="sibling-group-selection">sibling-group selection</Term>. Whichever you use, select for:

- **Easy shelling.** In dry corn he wants kernels that come off the cob readily. It is one of his top traits, and it tends to run in families, which suits sibling groups.
- **Popping.** When he worked on popcorn, he popped 20 kernels from every cob each winter and replanted the cobs whose kernels popped best, judging both how many kernels popped and how much they swelled.
- **Standing up to animals.** Pheasants ate the cobs on his short plants, so he saved seed only from tall ones. Later, where skunks and raccoons raided, he saved from cobs they had left alone. In time the stalks grew stiffer, the ears rose higher, and the damage stopped.

Earliness shifts too. After a decade in his garden, his strain of the sweet corn 'Astronomy Domine' ripens about ten days sooner than the breeder's.

## Hidden sweet kernels

Sweetness is a <Term id="recessive-trait">recessive trait</Term>: a kernel is sweet only when it gets the sweet gene from both parents.

1. Cross a sweet corn with a starchy one. The first-generation kernels all look starchy.
2. Grow them out. On the next cobs, the <Term id="f2">F2</Term>, about one kernel in four is sweet, and it dries wrinkled.
3. Pick out the wrinkled kernels and sow them away from other corn. Once selected, sweetness stays.

A single wrinkled kernel on a flour cob means both its parents carried sweetness without showing it. Lofthouse reads it as a sign that at least half the kernels on that cob carry it too. That is how he found 'Andean Sweet' inside 'Harmony'. It came from plants already hardened against skunks, raccoons and birds, so it grows huge, with its cobs high on the stalk.

In his view the neat one-in-four rarely helps a landrace grower, because most traits depend on many genes. Sweetness is the exception: one gene separates sweet corn from grain corn.

## Special populations

Two of his projects, as his reports rather than proven results:

- **High-carotene flint.** He selected a deep orange flint out of 'Harmony'. The colour comes from a South American race said to carry far more beta-carotene than usual corn.
- **Air-rooted corn.** Some corn grows roots from the stalk above ground. Scientists found nitrogen-fixing microbes living on these roots, which give off a gel in damp weather. He has selected a population with plenty of air roots, and its plants stand out as some of the tallest in his fields, which he never fertilises. Whether air roots help in your garden is unknown.

## Save the seed

<Steps>
  <Step title="Dry on the plant">Leave seed cobs on tall, healthy, undamaged plants until they are fully dry.</Step>
  <Step title="Pick from many">Take seed cobs from many plants, not just a favourite few, so the population stays wide.</Step>
  <Step title="Shell">Rub the kernels off the cob by hand. Easy-shelling cobs make this quick.</Step>
  <Step title="Store dry">Keep the kernels dry and cool, labelled.</Step>
</Steps>
