Many trees and shrubs grow back from the stump when you cut them down. Cutting them again and again on purpose is <Term id="coppice">coppicing</Term>, and one planting can then give firewood, poles, fencing, basket rods and mulch for decades. Bamboo does the same job from a clump. Both are among the perennial harvests Eric Toensmeier surveys in his book (see [Carbon farming](/guides/carbon-farming/)).

<QuickAnswer>
Plant trees that regrow when cut, such as willow, hazel or nitrogen-fixing shrubs, and cut a different patch each year so there is always wood ready. Choose clumping bamboo where you can; if you plant a running bamboo, contain it. Burning the wood returns its carbon; the roots and soil keep theirs.
</QuickAnswer>

## Cut, regrow, repeat

Cut the stem near the ground. A crowd of new shoots springs from the living stump, the <Term id="stool">stool</Term>, and you cut them again once they reach the size you need. The same cut made higher up the trunk, out of animals' reach, is a <Term id="pollard">pollard</Term>. [Trees with edible leaves](/guides/trees-with-edible-leaves/#keep-them-low) shows both cuts step by step.

Regular cutting can make a tree live far longer than it would otherwise; the author reports coppice stools in Europe that are centuries old. What you want decides when you cut: thin rods for baskets are ready in a few years, poles and firewood take longer. Traditional European coppice was cut every several years to every couple of decades, depending on the size of wood wanted. The first cut from an old tree can give good timber; the fast regrowth after it rarely does.

## Plan a rotation

Divide the planting into as many patches as there are years between cuts. Each patch is a <Term id="coupe">coupe</Term>: cut one a year, leave the rest growing, and every year there is wood ready.

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Cut in the dormant season where winters are cold, and in the dry season in the tropics.

Commercial <Term id="short-rotation-coppice">short-rotation coppice</Term> packs willow or poplar together and cuts it by machine every few years, mostly for fuel. Grown as a single crop, the book notes, these plots draw pests and wear out within a few decades, where traditional stools last for centuries. At home, mix species.

## Firewood from nitrogen fixers

A fast <Term id="nitrogen-fixer">nitrogen fixer</Term> cut for firewood feeds its own soil: self-fertilising firewood, in the author's phrase. Toensmeier estimates that a dense block of such trees, about a sixth of a hectare, can meet a tropical family's cooking fuel, and less with an efficient stove. That is his estimate, not a promise.

The fastest nitrogen fixers are also the most likely to run wild beyond the planting. Look first at the trees native to your region that regrow when cut, as he advises, and check anything else against your local invasive list.

The leafy prunings make good mulch: chip them or lay them under your crops ([Which mulch](/guides/mulch/#which-mulch)).

<Safety title="Cutting and burning">
{/* general safety, not from the book */}Wear eye protection, gloves and sturdy boots to cut, and never use a chainsaw alone. Burn only dry wood, keep fires and charcoal making well away from buildings and dry vegetation, and follow local fire rules.
</Safety>

## Willow and poplar

Willow grows from cuttings pushed into moist ground, like a <Term id="live-stake">live stake</Term>. Its rods go into baskets, fences and natural building; it shelters fields as a windbreak or snow fence, binds streambanks, helps clean dirty water, and is easy to breed. In the far north it is one of the best choices, in the author's view. For basketry, see [Fibre, dye and soap](/guides/fibre-dye-and-soap/).

Poplar cuttings root easily too, and poplars grow fast. Willows like wet, even flooded ground; most poplars prefer humid places. Their leaves are good fodder: see [Feeding animals from trees](/guides/trees-for-livestock/).

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Plant both well away from drains and foundations.

## Bamboo: clumping or running

Bamboos come in two kinds, and the difference decides where you can plant them.

<Figure>
<BambooRhizomes clumping="Clumping: stays in a clump" running="Running: spreads by rhizomes" barrier="Barrier" alt="Two bamboos underground. A clumping bamboo has short rhizomes and its stems stay together. A running bamboo sends long rhizomes sideways and new stems come up far away, stopped here by a barrier." />
</Figure>

- A <Term id="clumping-bamboo">clumping bamboo</Term> stays in a tight clump. Most are tropical or subtropical, and they include the biggest bamboos and the best for building.
- A <Term id="running-bamboo">running bamboo</Term> <Invasive /> spreads by runners, sometimes aggressively, and most cold-hardy bamboos are of this kind. They are seen as invasive in places, though they spread by their roots, not by seed.

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Contain a runner from the day you plant it: a deep barrier or a strip you mow or trench all round, checked every year.

Bamboo needs a fairly humid climate; only a few kinds take semi-arid conditions.

## Harvesting bamboo

A bamboo clump is a <Term id="managed-multistem">managed multistem</Term>: you take a few canes at a time, never the whole clump. New canes come up every year and need several years to harden before they are strong enough to build with. Left uncut, each cane dies after about a decade while the clump lives on; cutting the mature ones spurs new growth, which the author says is why a managed stand stores more carbon than a wild one.

The canes serve for building and fencing, sections make seedling pots, and the rest goes to firewood and charcoal; the leaves are fodder. In China, crops grow between young bamboo plantings, goats and cattle graze beneath older stands, and bamboo shades mushrooms.

## Coppice and carbon, honestly

The book sorts perennial harvests into five kinds; coppice is one of the two that do not keep a steady store of carbon above ground, because the wood is cut back again and again. Burned wood returns its carbon to the air. The stools, roots and soil keep theirs.

Firewood from your own coppice can replace fuel brought from elsewhere, and poles or canes built into something hold their carbon for as long as they last. More in [Honest limits](/guides/carbon-farming/#honest-limits).

## For your climate

The principles hold everywhere; the species do not. Some trees that regrow when cut also cause trouble: eucalypts, for one, hold back nearby plants by <Term id="allelopathy">allelopathy</Term> and drink hard. A few the book names:

<ForClimate zones="tropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
- **Madre de cacao** (*Gliricidia sepium*): a nitrogen fixer that coppices strongly for firewood.
- **Red calliandra**: a fast nitrogen-fixing fuelwood shrub.
- **Clumping bamboos**: canes for building.
- **Leucaena** <Invasive />: good firewood, but a pest insect ravaged its large single-crop plantings, and it now ranks on lists of the worst invasive species worldwide.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="subtropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Mild winters with light frost.

- **Eucalypts** <Invasive />: coppiced for fuel and pulp; the author says their thirst and allelopathy are a serious problem only where rain is under 400 mm (16 in) a year.
- **Clumping bamboos**: canes for building in humid places.

**For the tropical highlands:** Andean alder (*Alnus acuminata*), a nitrogen fixer that coppices; coast redwood also regrows from the stump where it is cool enough.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="mediterranean" source="carbon-farming-solution">
- **Olive**: cut back on a ten-year cycle in some areas, which makes picking easier.
- **Eucalypts** <Invasive />: only where rain tops 400 mm (16 in) a year.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="arid" source="carbon-farming-solution">
For dry places: the book's arid and dry semi-arid lands.

- **Gum arabic** (*Acacia senegal*): a nitrogen fixer that coppices well and burns well.
- **Mopane**: a dryland firewood tree from Africa.
- **Mesquite** <Invasive />: grown for firewood in India, but spiny: see [Thorny and poisonous](/guides/windbreaks-and-living-fences/#thorny-and-poisonous).
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="temperate" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "warm temperate", which includes cool-summer places such as southern Britain.

- **Hazel**: the classic coppice for rods and poles.
- **Chestnut**: coppiced in Europe for centuries; the book shows a chestnut coppice in West Sussex, England.
- **Alder** (*Alnus glutinosa*): a nitrogen fixer for humid places.
- **Coast redwood**: one of the few conifers that regrows from the stump, and the rare coppice that gives building timber.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="continental" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "cold temperate".

- **Willows and poplars**: started from cuttings.
- **Siberian pea shrub**: a nitrogen-fixing shrub for small wood.
- **Running bamboos** <Invasive />: hardy here, but only behind a barrier.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="boreal" source="carbon-farming-solution">
- **Willows**: among the best options in the far north, in the author's view.
- **Poplars**: hardy into the boreal zone.
- **Dogwood** (*Cornus sericea*): a native North American shrub that coppices strongly.
</ForClimate>
