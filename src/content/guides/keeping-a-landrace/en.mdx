Is your saved seed losing vigour? Is your patch too small to keep a variety going? Can crossed seed go to a seed swap? This guide answers all three. A <Term id="landrace">landrace</Term> stays healthy when it stays large and varied, and the easiest way to keep it that way involves other gardeners.

## Start from your problem

<Points>

- **All your seed comes from last year.** Fill the sowing jar from several sources: see [Fill the sowing jar](#fill-the-sowing-jar).
- **Your patch is small.** Crowd the plants and pool seed with others: see [A small patch](#a-small-patch).
- **The crop looks tired.** Bring in a cross: see [When a line runs down](#when-a-line-runs-down).
- **You have crossed seed to give away.** Label it and take it to a swap: see [Seed swaps and seed libraries](#seed-swaps-and-seed-libraries).

</Points>

Which plants to keep seed from, and why natural crosses deserve a place, is the subject of [Choosing seed parents](/guides/choosing-seed-parents/).

## Fill the sowing jar

Sort the seed you could sow by how well you know it, then decide how much of each goes in.

<PlantingMixBar title="One way to mix a year's sowing, from his figures" parts={[{ label: "Last year's seed", value: 70, tone: "own" }, { label: "Earlier years", value: 20, tone: "old" }, { label: "New seed", value: 10, tone: "new" }]} />

- **Your own seed, last year's harvest.** The bulk of the jar.
- **Your own seed, older jars.** A share from two or three earlier harvests; Lofthouse puts it at 10 to 30% of his planting. Each year's seed carries the mark of its summer, and blending years keeps a hot or wet one from tipping the whole crop.
- **Seed from a grower you know.** Someone whose garden and habits you trust can supply a large share, the way Lofthouse sows freely from long-time partners.
- **Seed from a stranger or from far away.** Keep it small (he stays under about 10%) or sow it a little apart. It earns its place by what it adds.

For a <Term id="self-pollinating">self-pollinating</Term> crop such as beans or tomatoes, give new seed time: in his garden most newcomers struggle at first.

## A small patch

Plant numbers matter less than you may fear. In his view, a diverse landrace has little to fear from <Term id="inbreeding-depression">inbreeding depression</Term>; the real risk is an <Term id="outcrossing">outcrossing</Term> crop that has already been narrowed down to a few lines.

- **Keep seed from many plants**, as many as you comfortably can, and never let one plant become the whole of next year's sowing.
- **Crowd the bed.** Closer spacing means more parents in the same space. Lofthouse grows tomatoes in tight clumps or close rows.
- **Pool with other gardens.** Your neighbour's plants count as part of the same population (see [Your landrace lives in many gardens](#your-landrace-lives-in-many-gardens)).
- **Corn is the exception.** It is the one crop where he sets a floor: 200 plants or more, a figure long used for corn.

## When a line runs down

If a crop grows weaker year after year, let it cross with another variety of the same crop. Lofthouse's remedy for a worn-out line is fresh genes.

## Your landrace lives in many gardens

Pool seed with other gardens and with your own older jars, and your landrace's population becomes every plant in all of them, which keeps its <Term id="effective-population-size">effective population size</Term> up.

- **Pick partners whose conditions match yours.** In his experience, seed grown in your valley, your soil and your kind of summer is a year ahead already.
- **Make it a routine.** Set aside a time each year to swap with the same few growers and hear how last year's seed did; Lofthouse does his in winter.
- **Leave copies with friends.** Seed you gave away is your backup: more than once, a friend has sent him back a variety he had lost. See [Storing seed](/guides/storing-seed/).

## Seed swaps and seed libraries

Bring crossed landrace seed back to a library or swap without apology: where a library may worry about crossing, Lofthouse sees seed that crossed in a local garden as adapted to it.

Label it honestly. Write that it varies and may change from year to year, and that it was grown near here.

<SeedPacketLabel label="A label for shared landrace seed" stamp="Landrace: may be crossed" fields={[{ name: "Crop", value: "Winter squash" }, { name: "Grown in", value: "your town or valley" }, { name: "Years grown", value: "4" }, { name: "Grower", value: "your name" }, { name: "Note", value: "early, mixed colours" }]} />

Swaps are also the cheapest diversity you'll find. Look for:

- home-grown seed marked "may be crossed": Lofthouse is always glad to get it;
- leftovers from someone's trials, since a trial needs only a few seeds and the rest of the packet goes spare.

Also cheap: the mixed packets some seed companies sell, several varieties of one crop together.

<FoodCaution risk="treated-seed" />

A swap that takes only locally grown seed is worth seeking out; Lofthouse counts one in his region as his most useful.

If you sell seed rather than give it, check the rules where you live.

## Spread the risk

Don't rely on one crop. Some crop families thrive in hot, dry summers, others in cool, damp ones; grow several, and one bad season rarely takes everything.

Crops kept as <Term id="clone">clones</Term>, or bred to be uniform, can fail all at once when a pest breaks through. A diverse crop that crosses freely, he argues, can re-sort its genes to meet the change.

## Do this now

<Checklist id="keeping-a-landrace">
  <Item id="old-seed" when="winter">Take out seed from two or three past harvests and set aside a share for this year.</Item>
  <Item id="arrange-swap" when="winter">Arrange one swap with a nearby grower, with packets labelled "landrace: may be crossed".</Item>
  <Item id="mix-before-sowing" when="sowing">Mix last year's seed, older seed and a little new seed before you sow.</Item>
  <Item id="backup-packet" when="after-harvest">Send a backup packet to a friend.</Item>
