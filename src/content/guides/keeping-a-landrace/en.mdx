Is your saved seed losing vigour? Is your patch too small to keep a variety going? Can crossed seed go to a seed swap? This guide answers all three. A <Term id="landrace">landrace</Term> stays healthy through a few yearly habits, and the most useful of them involve other gardeners.

## Six habits

Lofthouse keeps his landraces with six habits. Here they are in the order you meet them in the year.

<Points>

- **At sowing, mix in older seed.** A share from earlier harvests keeps one odd year from steering the crop.
- **At sowing, add a little new seed.** Now and then, a small share from elsewhere brings in genes you lack.
- **In the bed, grow plenty of plants.** Crowd them if space is short.
- **At harvest, choose many parents.** More seed from the best, some from the good.
- **At harvest, favour natural crosses.** Give their seed a place of its own next year.
- **All year, share.** Swap with neighbours and leave copies with friends.

</Points>

Choosing parents and favouring crosses are covered in [Choosing seed parents](/guides/choosing-seed-parents/). The rest follows.

## This year's sowing mix

Before you sow, put the jar together from three kinds of seed. Here is one way, from the shares Lofthouse uses.

<PlantingMixBar title="One way to mix a year's sowing, from his figures" parts={[{ label: "Last year's seed", value: 70, tone: "own" }, { label: "Earlier years", value: 20, tone: "old" }, { label: "New seed", value: 10, tone: "new" }]} />

- **Last year's harvest** is the bulk of it.
- **Seed from several earlier harvests:** about 10 to 30% of his planting. A summer that is unusually hot or wet only shapes one year's seed, so plants that suit cooler or drier years stay in the crop.
- **New seed from outside your area:** up to about 10% of his planting. Even a newcomer that does poorly may leave its pollen in your crop; one that does well earns a place in the jar.

Where new seed comes from decides how much you sow. Seed from a garden you know nothing about deserves a small share, or a spot a little apart. Lofthouse keeps big sowings for seed from growers he has worked with for years.

New seed of a <Term id="self-pollinating">self-pollinating</Term> crop needs patience. In his garden most new bean and tomato varieties fail in their first year, and the few that make it settle in later.

## How many plants?

It depends on how varied your seed already is. In his view, a diverse landrace has little to fear from <Term id="inbreeding-depression">inbreeding depression</Term>. Plant numbers matter most for <Term id="outcrossing">outcrossing</Term> varieties that are already inbred.

Lofthouse won't give a minimum. His one rule is never to resow from a single seed, year after year. Keep seed from as many plants as you comfortably can. Corn is the crop where he follows the traditional advice: at least 200 plants to keep a variety going.

### In a small space

Crowd your plants. More plants in the bed means more parents. Lofthouse fits 10 to 25 tomatoes into one clump, or spaces them 15 cm (6 in) apart along a row.

### If your seed loses vigour

Let the tired line cross with another variety of the same crop. That is his remedy: fresh genes into a worn-out line.

## Your landrace lives in many gardens

Pool seed with other gardens and with your own older jars, and your landrace's population becomes every plant in all of them, which keeps its <Term id="effective-population-size">effective population size</Term> up.

- **Pick partners whose conditions match yours.** In his experience, seed grown in your valley, your soil and your kind of summer is a year ahead already. Seed from far away is still welcome, for the diversity it brings rather than for a reliable crop.
- **Make it a routine.** Set aside a time each year to swap with the same few growers and hear how last year's seed did; Lofthouse does his in winter.
- **Leave copies with friends.** Seed you gave away is your backup: more than once, a friend has sent him back a variety he had lost. See [Storing seed](/guides/storing-seed/).

## Seed swaps and seed libraries

Bring crossed landrace seed back to a library or swap without apology: where a library may worry about crossing, Lofthouse sees seed that crossed in a local garden as adapted to it.

Label it honestly. Write that it varies and may change from year to year, and that it was grown near here.

<SeedPacketLabel label="A label for shared landrace seed" stamp="Landrace: may be crossed" fields={[{ name: "Crop", value: "Winter squash" }, { name: "Grown in", value: "your town or valley" }, { name: "Years grown", value: "4" }, { name: "Grower", value: "your name" }, { name: "Note", value: "early, mixed colours" }]} />

Swaps are also the cheapest diversity you'll find. Look for:

- home-grown seed marked "may be crossed": Lofthouse is always glad to get it;
- leftovers from someone's trials, since a trial needs only a few seeds and the rest of the packet goes spare.

Also cheap: the mixed packets some seed companies sell, several varieties of one crop together.

<FoodCaution risk="treated-seed" />

Swaps limited to local seed are especially valuable. His most important one is a seed exchange in his region that takes only locally grown seed.

If you sell seed rather than give it, check the rules where you live.

## Spread the risk

Don't rely on one crop. Some crop families thrive in hot, dry summers, others in cool, damp ones; grow several, and one bad season rarely takes everything.

Crops kept as <Term id="clone">clones</Term>, or bred to be uniform, can fail all at once when a pest breaks through. A diverse crop that crosses freely, he argues, can re-sort its genes to meet the change.
