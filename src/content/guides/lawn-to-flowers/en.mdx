A mown lawn gives pollinators almost nothing: few flowers, nowhere to nest, no cover. Kim Eierman, who designs gardens in the northeastern United States, holds that most gardens can give some of it back, and a balcony counts too. Keep the lawn you actually use, never spray what's left, and start with one small bed you can see from the house. A meadow is planted, then cut once a year; it is not a lawn left unmown.

## Keep the lawn you use

Mark out the grass you really use, for play, pets or sitting; the rest can go. In the part you keep, let clover, dandelions and other flowering "weeds" bloom, and never spray it: Eierman notes that pesticides drifting off lawns have killed many pollinators.

What goes in place of the rest depends on what you need to see over. Where you want to keep a clear view, plant low flowering ground covers in large groups; where height does no harm, a tall meadow with paths through it. Whichever you choose, plant many kinds: a single ground cover in place of grass is still a monoculture.

## Clearing turf without weedkiller

Clear a little at a time; no part of this needs doing all at once. Eierman avoids herbicides and gives two ways:

<Points>
- **Slow, with no machine** Lay cardboard or newspaper straight on the grass, then a layer of compost and a layer of mulch on top. Leave it a few months: the grass beneath dies in the dark and rots, and you plant straight into the layers.
- **Fast, with a turf cutter** Set the machine deep enough to take the grass roots with the turf, lift it off, and plant the same day.
</Points>

The slow way is close to building a bed without digging: [Vegetable beds without digging](/guides/no-dig-beds/) explains how to plant and feed a bed without turning the soil.

## Your first bed: an island or a deeper edge

Before buying plants, note a few things about the spot. None of it needs the garden in flower.

<Steps compact>
<Step title="Sun">Count the hours of direct sun each spot gets, and check again in another season. Photos at a few times of day help.</Step>
<Step title="Wet spots">Find where water still stands a day after rain.</Step>
<Step title="Wind and warmth">Mark the windy corners, and the sheltered ones that stay warmer.</Step>
<Step title="Soil">Have it tested, or read it yourself first ([What's in your soil?](/guides/reading-your-soil/)).</Step>
<Step title="What is already there">List the trees, shrubs and perennials you have, and any on your local invasive list, which come out first.</Step>
<Step title="Gaps in bloom">Note the seasons when nothing flowers ([Something in flower all season](/guides/flowers-for-pollinators/#bloom-succession)).</Step>
</Steps>

Where Eierman looks up US hardiness and heat zones, use your local climate data.

**An island** is a small bed cut out of the lawn, anywhere you will see it every day; passers-by may copy it. Size it to hold several kinds of plant, each in a group ([Enough of each](/guides/flowers-for-pollinators/#enough-of-each)), and give it a clear, flowing edge: a crisp outline shows the bed is cared for, Eierman finds, so the stems you leave standing through winter look intended.

Arrange it by the size each plant will reach, not the size it is in the pot; young shrubs and perennials grow far bigger. The tallest plants go where they hide nothing: in the middle of a bed seen from all round, at the back of one set against a wall or hedge. One or two flowering shrubs give the bed its shape and plenty of flowers.

For a hot, dry spot, pick plants that thrive in heat and drought. Eierman suggests a prickly pear <Invasive /> native to her own region; choose your own region's drought-loving flowers. {/* general tip, not from the book */}If you do plant a cactus, its spines and fine bristles lodge in the skin: keep it back from paths and handle it with thick gloves.

**A deeper edge** works with what you already have. If your house has a narrow strip of evergreen shrubs along its walls, common in North American gardens and the book's example, it most likely feeds no insect. Where a shrub there is dead or struggling, put in a flowering one that fits at its full size: a shrub you must keep clipping loses its flower buds. Then widen the bed outward, stepping down from the shrubs to smaller flowering plants and grasses, with a low flowering ground cover at the front. Where you need year-round screening, choose evergreens that flower.

Do the same where trees meet the lawn: instead of a wood that stops dead at mown grass, build steps down from the trees, with host plants for caterpillars among them. [A layered homegarden](/guides/food-forest/) explains planting in layers, and [Windbreaks, living fences and hedges](/guides/windbreaks-and-living-fences/) covers hedges.

## A meadow, not an unmown lawn

A <Term id="meadowscape">meadowscape</Term> is planted and managed. Stop mowing a lawn and you get tall lawn grass and weeds, not a meadow.

<Points>
- **The mix** Flowers and wild grasses together. Eierman's rule of thumb is about six parts flowers to four parts grasses, with at least three kinds in flower at any time, scattered through rather than in blocks. The grasses knit the soil and feed caterpillars, and a scattered planting gives deer no obvious patch to graze. If you want a tidier look, set the meadow plants in bold, clear-cut drifts instead.
- **The site** Full sun, fairly level ground, no trees or shrubs, and lean soil. Rich soil and shade favour other plants over meadow grasses, so test the soil first.
- **A bank** On a slope, keep to low flowers and grasses planted thickly: they hold the bank and end the chore of mowing it ([Keep rain and soil on your land](/guides/water-and-slopes/)).
- **Seed or young plants** Seed costs least and looks most natural, but takes several years to fill in and needs the most weeding early on. Young plants cost more but fill in within a season or two.
</Points>

Once it has filled in, cut the meadow once a year at the end of the cold or dormant season, never in autumn: standing, it gives shelter and seed, and the cut keeps woody plants out. Cut a large one in patches over several weeks, so insects resting in it can leave. Where winters are cold, see when ([Cold winters](/guides/homes-for-pollinators/#cold-winters)).

## Pots on a balcony

Any balcony, roof or front step can feed pollinators. Use the biggest pots that fit, frost-proof where winters freeze. Leave perennials in them all year and add compost every year; water more often than in the ground, because pots dry out fast.

Choose long-flowering plants and pollinator favourites first: there is room for only a few. Compact forms suit pots, Eierman says, as long as they still have nectar and pollen.

In one large pot, combine a tall plant, a mid-height one and a trailing one that flower in different seasons: one pot, three seasons of food. Group the pots together. [Flowers all season](/guides/flowers-for-pollinators/) helps you choose.

## Bring the neighbours in

Small insects fly only short distances, so the roads and buildings that cut gardens apart leave them stranded: <Term id="habitat-fragmentation">habitat fragmentation</Term>. A <Term id="pollinator-pathway">pollinator pathway</Term> joins unsprayed, flowering gardens along a street or across a town.

Start with the house next to yours: ask its owners to turn part of their lawn into flowers, and share seedlings and host plants. A small sign saying the garden is for pollinators and never sprayed explains the winter stems. Invite people round; a local conservation group may already run a pathway you can join.
