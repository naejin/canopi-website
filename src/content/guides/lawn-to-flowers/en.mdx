A mown lawn gives pollinators almost nothing: few flowers, nowhere to nest, no cover. Kim Eierman, who designs gardens in the northeastern United States, holds that most gardens can give some of it back, and a balcony counts too. Start with one small bed you can see from the house, keep the lawn you actually use, and never spray what's left. A meadow is planted and cut once a year, not just left unmown.

## Look before you buy

Watch your garden before you buy, and fewer plants will struggle. Most of this can be done while the garden is dormant.

<Steps compact>
<Step title="Sun">Note how many hours of direct sun each spot gets, and check again in another season. Photos at a few times of day help.</Step>
<Step title="Wet spots">Find where water still stands a day after rain.</Step>
<Step title="Wind and warmth">Mark the windy corners, and the sheltered ones that stay warmer.</Step>
<Step title="Soil">Have it tested, or read it yourself first ([What's in your soil?](/guides/reading-your-soil/)).</Step>
<Step title="What is already there">List the trees, shrubs and perennials you have, and any on your local invasive list, which come out first.</Step>
<Step title="Gaps in bloom">Note the seasons when nothing flowers ([Something in flower all season](/guides/flowers-for-pollinators/#bloom-succession)).</Step>
</Steps>

## Start with an island

An island is a small bed cut out of the lawn. Put your first one where you see it every day; passers-by may copy it.

Make it big enough to hold several kinds of plant, each in a group ([Enough of each](/guides/flowers-for-pollinators/#enough-of-each)), and give it a clear, flowing edge. Eierman finds that a crisp outline tells everyone the bed is looked after, so the dead stems you leave standing through winter look intended, not neglected.

Plant for each plant's full-grown size: a small pot can hide a large shrub. Seen from all sides, the bed takes its tallest plants in the middle; backed by a wall or hedge, at the back. A flowering shrub or two gives it a frame and many flowers.

In a hot, dry spot, choose plants that thrive there. Eierman suggests prickly pears <Invasive /> for a dry island. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Their spines and fine bristles lodge in the skin: plant them back from paths and handle them with thick gloves.

## Deeper beds and richer edges

Many houses have a narrow strip of evergreen shrubs along the walls that feeds no insect. Replace any dead or struggling ones with flowering shrubs that fit at their full size: a shrub you must keep clipping loses its flower buds. Then widen the bed outward, stepping down from the shrubs to smaller flowering plants and grasses, with a low flowering ground cover at the front. Where you need year-round screening, choose evergreens that flower.

Do the same where trees meet the lawn: instead of a wood that stops dead at mown grass, build steps down from the trees, with host plants for caterpillars among them. [A layered homegarden](/guides/food-forest/) explains planting in layers, and [Windbreaks, living fences and hedges](/guides/windbreaks-and-living-fences/) covers hedges.

## Less lawn, kept better

Keep the grass you use for play, pets or sitting, and replace the rest. In the grass you keep, let clover, dandelions and other flowering "weeds" bloom, and never spray it: Eierman notes that pesticides drifting off lawns have killed many pollinators.

<TryConsortium plant="Trifolium repens" name="White clover" />

Choose what replaces it by what you need to see over: low flowering ground covers in large groups where you want a clear view, a tall meadow with paths where height does no harm. Either way, plant many kinds: replacing a lawn with a single ground cover swaps one monoculture for another.

To remove turf without herbicide, smother it. Cover the grass with cardboard or newspaper, then compost, then mulch, and plant through once the turf below has rotted, a few months later. For speed, lift the turf with a turf cutter that cuts deep enough to take the roots. [Vegetable beds without digging](/guides/no-dig-beds/) explains how to plant and feed a bed without turning the soil.

## A meadow, not an unmown lawn

A <Term id="meadowscape">meadowscape</Term> is planted and managed. Stop mowing a lawn and you get tall lawn grass and weeds, not a meadow.

<Points>
- **Where** Full sun, fairly level ground (on a slope, use low plants), no trees or shrubs, and lean soil. Rich soil and shade suit other plants better than meadow grasses, so test the soil first.
- **What** Flowers and wild grasses together. Eierman's rule of thumb is about six parts flowers to four parts grasses, with at least three kinds in flower at any time, scattered through rather than in blocks. The grasses knit the soil and feed caterpillars, and a scattered planting gives deer no obvious patch to graze.
- **Seed or young plants** Seed costs least and looks most natural, but takes several years to fill in and needs the most weeding early on. Young plants cost more but fill in within a season or two. For a tidier look, plant meadow species in deliberate drifts.
- **On a slope** Low flowers and grasses planted thickly hold a bank and end the chore of mowing it ([Keep rain and soil on your land](/guides/water-and-slopes/)).
</Points>

Once it has filled in, cut the meadow once a year at the end of the cold or dormant season, never in autumn: standing, it gives shelter and seed, and the cut keeps woody plants out. Cut a large one in patches over several weeks, so insects resting in it can leave. Where winters are cold, see when ([Cold winters](/guides/homes-for-pollinators/#cold-winters)).

## Pots on a balcony

Any balcony, roof or front step can feed pollinators. Use the biggest pots that fit, frost-proof where winters freeze. Leave perennials in them all year and add compost every year; water more often than in the ground, because pots dry out fast.

Choose long-flowering plants and pollinator favourites first: there is room for only a few. Compact forms suit pots, Eierman says, as long as they still have nectar and pollen.

In one large pot, combine a tall plant, a mid-height one and a trailing one that flower in different seasons: one pot, three seasons of food. Group the pots together. [Flowers all season](/guides/flowers-for-pollinators/) helps you choose.

## Bring the neighbours in

Small insects fly only short distances, so the roads and buildings that cut gardens apart leave them stranded: <Term id="habitat-fragmentation">habitat fragmentation</Term>. A <Term id="pollinator-pathway">pollinator pathway</Term> joins unsprayed, flowering gardens along a street or across a town.

Start next door: ask a neighbour to turn part of their lawn into flowers, and share seedlings and host plants. A small sign saying the garden is for pollinators and never sprayed explains the winter stems. Invite people round; a local conservation group may already run a pathway you can join.
