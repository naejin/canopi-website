Mulch is anything you lay on the soil to keep water in, keep weeds down and soften heat and cold. This guide is about organic mulch only: leaves, chips, bark, straw and clippings. Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis add one more job: mulch feeds and shelters soil life, a slow, cold compost that never heats.

Short answer: yes, a woody mulch can hold nitrogen, but mostly in a thin layer where it touches the soil, not down among the roots. Lay 5–7.5 cm (2–3 in), keep it off stems and trunks, and pick the kind by the plant: brown and coarse under trees and shrubs, green and fine on vegetable beds.

## What mulch does

<Points>

- **Keeps water in.** Less evaporates, and heavy rain no longer packs the surface.
- **Shades out weeds.** Buried seed stays in the dark and seedlings get no light.
- **Buffers the soil.** It stays cooler in heat and insulated in cold. Where soil freezes and thaws, mulch keeps it frozen, so plants do not start too early.
- **Feeds soil life.** Worms pull it down and leave castings and tunnels; springtails and mites live in it.

</Points>

The authors grant that mulch brings less life than compost, because it has not rotted yet. Their fix: lay compost first and mulch on top, and the compost's organisms move up into the mulch ([Compost that feeds soil life](/guides/compost-for-soil-life/)).

## Does it steal nitrogen?

Microbes rotting a carbon-rich mulch need nitrogen. When the mulch has too little, they take it from the soil it touches, and plants cannot use it for a while: <Term id="nitrogen-tie-up">nitrogen tie-up</Term>. The authors put the threshold at about 30 parts carbon to one of nitrogen or more (the <Term id="carbon-nitrogen-ratio">carbon-to-nitrogen ratio</Term>).

Their answer to the worry: it happens mostly in a thin band where mulch meets soil, and usually does not reach the roots below. Where enough grazers such as protozoa and nematodes eat those microbes, the nitrogen comes back.

They even use it against weeds. A weed seedling's shallow roots sit in that hungry band, while crop roots deeper down are untouched.

To lessen it under wood chips, experience taught the authors to use chips of about 1 cm (⅜ in) or larger, which bacteria colonise more slowly. You can also age chips before spreading, or mix in something green such as fresh clippings or alfalfa meal. On vegetable beds, they spread alfalfa meal, straw or clippings at leaf-fall: any tie-up is over by spring planting.

## Fungal or bacterial mulch

The authors steer soil life with three dials, all their rules:

- **What it is.** Aged, brown material favours fungi; fresh, green material favours bacteria.
- **Where it lies.** Left on top, it favours fungi; mixed into the top few centimetres, bacteria.
- **What state it is in.** Coarse and dry favours fungi; fine and wet, bacteria.

So the same leaves can go either way: whole on top under trees, chopped fine and worked in on a vegetable bed. Clippings and straw count as green even once they have dried and paled, the authors say, because they keep their sugars. They also say some bacteria in fine, wet mulch make substances that hold fungi back.

## Which mulch

| Mulch | Feeds mostly | Notes (the authors') |
|---|---|---|
| Autumn leaves | Fungi on top; bacteria if chopped fine | Their favourite brown mulch. In their experience it grows fungi faster than chips, and can rot away within about six months. |
| Bark and wood chips | Fungi | Slow: about 5 cm (2 in) of bark lasts three or four years. Age them or add greens. |
| Straw | Bacteria (counts as green) | A green mulch for vegetable beds. |
| Fresh grass clippings | Bacteria | Thin layers only, and only clean ones (see the box below). |
| Pine needles | Fungi | Only once aged: fresh needles carry terpenes that harm many plants. |
| Peat | — | Lifeless, in their view: mix it with other material. |

The authors also avoid cedar chips, for their terpenes.

## Lay it right

<Steps compact>
<Step title="Compost first">

If you have some, a thin layer of compost under the mulch.

</Step>
<Step title="No deeper than 5–7.5 cm (2–3 in)">

Thicker, the authors say, blocks air and water and smothers the root fungi (<Term id="mycorrhiza">mycorrhizae</Term>). See [Mycorrhizal fungi](/guides/mycorrhizal-fungi/).

</Step>
<Step title="Leave a gap at stems and trunks">

Mulch pressed against bark lets microbes start rotting the plant itself.

</Step>
<Step title="Clippings thin">

Thick clippings mat, heat up, go sour and smell. Any green mulch that smells bad is too wet and has gone <Term id="anaerobic">anaerobic</Term>: open it up and water less.

</Step>
<Step title="Before the weeds">

On vegetable beds, lay a bacteria-friendly mulch before weeds come up, with a little bare soil round each stem.

</Step>
</Steps>

## Under trees and on the lawn

Leave fallen leaves where they drop under trees and shrubs, and run a mower over them to break them open. Top up with any brown mulch out to the <Term id="drip-line">drip line</Term>, a few centimetres deep. The authors mulch even where they have no compost, and they advise giving up on grass under trees.

On the lawn, they leave clippings where they fall all season, as a light green mulch, and mow autumn leaves and storm twigs in place instead of raking them up.

At leaf-fall, mulch vegetable beds too: in the authors' view, soil life keeps working through winter under it.

<Safety title="Only clean clippings">
Never mulch with clippings from a lawn treated with weedkiller or pesticide, or from a lawn where dogs go: the residues and the faeces end up on your beds.
</Safety>

## Brews poured on mulch

The authors also pour an aerated brew of fresh plant material onto mulch, to add protozoa. This guide gives no recipe: it is a homemade brew with the same risks as compost tea, and it can turn foul without air. Read [Compost tea: what it is and what can go wrong](/guides/compost-tea/) first.
