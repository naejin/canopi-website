Which vegetables can stay in the ground over winter? How do you keep squash and roots without a cellar? And what is there to eat at the end of winter, when the stores run low and nothing new is ready? The answer is to grow crops that are ready, or that keep, at different times of year. The wheel shows where the lean weeks fall and what fills them in Joseph Lofthouse's garden.

## Leave it in the ground

The garden itself is the simplest store. A row of carrots or turnips under a layer of straw can stay where it grew, and you dig what you need on any day the soil is not frozen. That is how Lofthouse keeps his, along with sunroot (Jerusalem artichoke). The straw, spread in autumn, keeps the ground from freezing as hard.

**Sunroot** is the one he relies on most. In his garden it can be dug at any point from autumn to spring, whenever the ground allows, and most of the patch stays in the soil. It comes back every year, even in a year you never harvest it. Give it a corner it can keep for good: he says it spreads like a weed.

Sunroots are usually grown as <Term id="clone">clones</Term>, every plant the same. He bred his own sunroots from seed and now keeps the best as his own clones: see [Potato, garlic and sunroot from true seed](/guides/true-seed-potato-garlic-sunroot/).

## Keeping crops without a cellar

Each kind of crop wants its own kind of place. In Lofthouse's experience:

<Points>

- **Spring greens stay in the ground.** Pick them as you need them; there is nothing to store.
- **Winter squash goes indoors.** A dry shelf in the living space is enough, with no cooling.
- **Roots want a cool corner.** Find them a spot that is moist and dark as well as cool.

</Points>

A root cellar need not be a room: a covered hole in the ground, big enough for some potatoes, was his grandfather's cellar.

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Look over your stores now and then through the winter. Use first whatever keeps least well, and throw out anything that has gone soft or mouldy.

## The early-spring gap

The leanest weeks often come at the turn of winter and spring: stores are running down and the new garden is barely sown. Three crops fill them in Lofthouse's garden.

- **Spinach sown at the end of summer.** See [What to sow in autumn](/guides/season-shifting/#what-to-sow-in-autumn).
- **Kale, cabbage and Brussels sprouts that came through winter.** To his taste, their first leaves of spring are at their best.
- **Egyptian (walking) onions.** He picks them as green onions from about two weeks after the snow has gone, and goes on picking all season.
- **Skirret**, a perennial he grows, gives his earliest greens. He dislikes them later in the year, but after a greenless winter they are a treat.

## Spread the risk

A garden that feeds you all year cannot hang on one crop. Lofthouse spreads the risk in three ways.

- **Several crop families.** In his view some families do best in hot, dry summers and others in cool, damp ones. Grow a few of each, and an odd year rarely takes everything.
- **Several kinds of legume.** Alongside common beans he grows lentils, teparies, favas and many more, his list running as far as lupini, grasspea, fenugreek and alfalfa. Some like heat, some take frost, and he finds a single disease, insect or summer seldom beats them all. See [Beans and other legumes](/guides/beans-and-legumes/).
- **Varied seed.** Plants that differ from each other and cross can shift as conditions change. Fields of identical plants can fail together; his example is the corn blight that swept the United States in 1970. More in [Pests and diseases](/guides/pests-without-sprays/), and on keeping a varied line going in [Keeping a landrace healthy](/guides/keeping-a-landrace/).

<Safety title="Not every legume is an everyday pulse">
Lupini and grasspea carry natural toxins and are unsafe without proper preparation. Eat no unfamiliar legume without your food-safety authority's guidance.
</Safety>

## Your own seasonal cues

Dates move from year to year; what you see in the garden moves with the weather. Lofthouse keeps short reminders that tie a job to a sign, such as picking his green onions two weeks after the snow has gone, or checking a friend's apricot trees on that friend's birthday. Make your own for sowing and harvesting: note what flowers, leafs out or turns up when each crop is ready, and add a line every year.

## Wild greens and mushrooms

Lofthouse counts weeds among his food: in his view they suit his valley better than anything shipped in. Wet spells, too muddy for garden work, are his mushroom time.

He grows mushrooms only outdoors, never in a sterile indoor setup. He blends mushroom trimmings into their washing water, pours it over a material the mushroom grows on, and leaves the patch to itself, checking it during or just after damp weather.

<Safety title="Wild food is his practice: identify before you eat">
Lofthouse eats wild lamb's quarters more often than lettuce, and wild oyster mushrooms more often than shop mushrooms. He picks dandelions only from shady plants, before the warm weather, and keeps cues for his forays: morels once the spring grass is up, oyster mushrooms soon after rain. Wild plants and mushrooms have poisonous look-alikes, one wrong mushroom can kill, and an outdoor patch can raise wild mushrooms beside the one you seeded. Eat nothing wild, and no mushroom, you have not identified with certainty.
</Safety>

## Seed is not food

Saving seed leaves you with far more than you will sow. Seed kept for sowing is not food by default, and seed treated with chemicals never is. Dry beans and other pulses also need proper preparation before they are eaten.

<Safety title="Eating surplus seed is his practice, not a rule">
Lofthouse eats some surplus seed, whole in bread, stir-fries and soups, or ground into flour, makes fresh mustard from his own, and counts fig-leaved gourd seeds as food. Some seeds are poisonous, and seed for sowing may be treated or mixed. Eat seed only from a crop grown and kept as food.
</Safety>
