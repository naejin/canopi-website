Will your zucchini cross with your pumpkin? Can you save seed from a shop-bought butternut? Why did one squash come out bitter? This guide answers those questions, then shows how to grow squash, melons and cucumbers as a <Term id="landrace">landrace</Term>: a mixed population that suits your garden a little better every year.

<QuickAnswer>
Bees move pollen between plants, so varieties of one species cross freely, and that is why these crops adapt so fast. The three main squash species, pepo, moschata and maxima, almost never cross with each other. In Lofthouse's experience, hybrid squash, melons and cucumbers are fine to save seed from. Rinse squash and melon seed; ferment cucumber seed. A bitter fruit is neither food nor a seed parent: stop eating it and throw its seed out.
</QuickAnswer>

## Why start here

Each plant carries two kinds of flower. The male ones only shed pollen; the female ones have a small fruit at their base. A plant built this way is <Term id="monoecious">monoecious</Term>.

Bees carry pollen from plant to plant, so most seeds have a different father from their mother plant. These are <Term id="outcrossing">outcrossing</Term> crops: their genes are reshuffled every summer, and the plants that suit your soil, weather and pests show up quickly.

Lofthouse's muskmelon landrace grew mostly from the two plants that thrived best when he first sowed a wide mix of varieties together.

You don't need special seed. Some hybrid crops can leave their offspring without pollen, but in his experience squash, melon, cucumber and watermelon hybrids don't. A supermarket butternut or an F1 courgette can join your mix.

## Know your species

Check the species before the variety. Varieties of one species cross freely; different species almost never do. Lofthouse grows thousands of squash a year and found a single natural cross between species in twelve years.

So your zucchini will cross with your carving pumpkin, since both are pepo, but not with your butternut, which is moschata. Seed catalogues usually give the Latin name.

Many pepo are picked young as summer squash. A zucchini left on the vine until its seed is ripe becomes a marrow, which you can use as a winter squash.

### Melons

Muskmelon (cantaloupe) and honeydew are one species, so they cross. Lofthouse wants his muskmelons netted and orange inside, so his green-fleshed melons grow in another field. Cucumbers and watermelons are separate species, and in his experience their hybrids are fine to save from too.

His watermelons are bred for yellow flesh. Lofthouse believes the pigment that turns watermelons and tomatoes red has a bitter edge, so a yellow melon can taste sweet with less sugar. That is his view of flavour; it is not the toxic bitterness described under [Bitter fruit](#bitter-fruit).

## Starting in a short season

If your summers are too short for most varieties, don't hunt for the one that ripens. Sow many and let the season choose.

By its third year, Lofthouse's moschata landrace gave plenty of ripe fruit in a season of only 84 days. It began badly: frost came 88 days after the first sowing, three varieties in four set no fruit, and the seed he kept came from green fruit left to finish indoors.

You can use the same trick anywhere. Seed can grow long before its fruit is ripe, and it keeps maturing inside a picked fruit. Before the first frost, bring in every fruit you want seed from, ripe or green. Let the green ones finish in the house before you open them. Their seedlings may be a little less vigorous, but they grow.

His watermelons started from about 700 seeds, the offspring of crosses among hundreds of varieties. He harvested five fruits that first year and counts that as a good start. The most dependable seed came from growers nearby, with his climate, soil and pests, so ask your neighbours first.

## Keep what defines it, let the rest vary

A landrace doesn't have to look like a jumble. First decide how much of the look you want to keep, then sow to match.

| You want | Sow | For example |
|---|---|---|
| Any look, as long as it grows well | All the types mixed, as one <Term id="hybrid-swarm">hybrid swarm</Term> | Squash grown for the table, where shape and skin don't matter |
| One look, kept steady | A mix, then keep selecting for the one or two traits that make the crop yours | Zucchini: long, slim fruit; the skin colour can be anything |
| Two looks, both kept | Each type at its own end of the row, as <Term id="sister-lines">sister lines</Term> | Acorn at the head of the row, Delicata at the foot |
| Mostly one look, the others not lost | More seed of the favourite than of the rest | Lofthouse sows about nine long-neck butternuts to one round |

With sister lines, each end mostly pollinates itself and the crosses gather in the middle. With an uneven mix, a few fruit of the other shapes keep turning up every year, and that is what you want.

<PlotDiagram variant="row-ends" />

Whatever you keep, aim for medium to large fruit: more food per plant. When Lofthouse selected for very small butternuts, the plants lost vigour and the fruit kept poorly.

## Choose seed parents in the kitchen

Pick your seed parents while you prepare the stored squash in winter, and decide what counts for you: the colour of the flesh, how easily knife and peeler go through, the smell, how well the fruit kept. Lofthouse works through about sixteen fruit at a time.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Scoop the seed first">Scoop each fruit's seed into its own labelled cup before you go any further.</Step>
  <Step title="Look at the fruit">Note how it kept since harvest, how it cuts and peels, and the colour and thickness of the flesh.</Step>
  <Step title="Judge it as food">If you also choose by flavour, do it only at the table, and only with a crop you already eat, prepared the way you always prepare it. Don't taste it raw if you normally cook it. If a fruit is bitter, stop eating it and throw that fruit's seed out.</Step>
  <Step title="Keep the best">Keep the cups from the best fruit. The rest go on the compost.</Step>
</Steps>

Selection works fast. His long-neck butternuts now have skin thin enough for an ordinary peeler, his squash turn a deeper orange each year, and because hard fruit was never kept, they all cut easily now.

## Canning squash

Leftover cooked squash becomes pie filling in Lofthouse's house. He mashes and freezes some of it, and he also bottles squash in jars in a pressure canner, which he finds lighter and brighter than shop-bought filling. That is his practice, not advice: food-safety authorities do not consider mashed or puréed squash safe to can at home by any method, because low-acid food that is canned at home can carry botulism, a poisoning that can kill. Freezing, which he also does, avoids that danger; his household freezes grated summer squash the same way, to bake into cakes through the winter.

## Bitter fruit

Bitterness in a squash, melon or cucumber is a warning sign of natural toxins. A <Term id="bitter-fruit">bitter fruit</Term> is not food, and its seed must stay out of your population, or the bitterness can return in later generations. After growing a perfumed pocket melon near his melons, Lofthouse discarded that year's melon seed rather than risk the bitterness crossing in.

<Safety title="Lofthouse tastes his squash raw: do not copy him">Before he keeps seed from a squash, Lofthouse tastes the fruit, raw as well as cooked, and counts on the foul taste of cucurbit poisons to give a bitter one away. He also says ornamental gourds belong in a breeding project only if someone is willing to taste the poisons back out of their offspring. That is his practice, not a step to follow: a bitter squash, melon or cucumber can make you seriously ill. Keep ornamental gourds away from your food squash: they are close to wild squash and can make their offspring bitter. Never taste a squash raw just to test it. Judge flavour only at the table, as in [Choose seed parents in the kitchen](#choose-seed-parents-in-the-kitchen), and stop at any bitterness.</Safety>

## Save the seed

- **Squash:** put the seed in a colander and clean it with a strong jet of water. It needs no fermenting: any gel left on it dries and blows away when you winnow.
- **Melon and watermelon:** there is little gel, so rinse the seed in a colander straight away.
- **Cucumber:** leave seed cucumbers on the vine until they turn yellow and soft. {/* General seed-saving knowledge, not from the book. */} Each seed sits in a <Term id="gel-coat">gel coat</Term> that holds back sprouting. Use <Term id="seed-fermentation">seed fermentation</Term>: leave the seed in its juice in a jar until the gel breaks down, then rinse. Label the jar and keep it out of the kitchen: it is seed, not food.

Dry everything spread thin.
