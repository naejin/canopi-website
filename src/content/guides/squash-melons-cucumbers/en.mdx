Will your zucchini cross with your pumpkin? Can you save seed from a shop-bought butternut? Why did one squash come out bitter? This guide answers those questions, then shows how to grow squash, melons and cucumbers as a <Term id="landrace">landrace</Term>: a mixed population that suits your garden a little better every year.

<QuickAnswer>
Bees move pollen between plants, so varieties of one species cross freely, and that is why these crops adapt so fast. The three main squash species, pepo, moschata and maxima, almost never cross with each other. In Lofthouse's experience, hybrid squash, melons and cucumbers are fine to save seed from. Rinse squash and melon seed; ferment cucumber seed. A very bitter fruit is neither food nor a seed parent.
</QuickAnswer>

## Why start here

Each plant carries two kinds of flower. The male ones only shed pollen; the female ones have a small fruit at their base. A plant built this way is <Term id="monoecious">monoecious</Term>.

Bees carry pollen from plant to plant, so most seeds have a different father from their mother plant. These are <Term id="outcrossing">outcrossing</Term> crops: their genes are reshuffled every summer, and the plants that suit your soil, weather and pests show up quickly.

Lofthouse's muskmelons began with seed from the few fruits he had the year before, mixed with varieties from farm stands, catalogues and grocery shops. Many failed: some never came up, others fell to insects or the cold. Two plants did so well that together they bore more fruit than the whole rest of the patch, and their seed started his landrace.

You don't need special seed. Some hybrid crops can leave their offspring without pollen, but in his experience squash, melon, cucumber and watermelon hybrids don't. A supermarket butternut or an F1 courgette can join your mix.

## Know your species

Check the species before the variety. Varieties of one species cross freely; different species almost never do. Lofthouse grows thousands of squash a year and found a single natural cross between species in twelve years.

So your zucchini will cross with your carving pumpkin, since both are pepo, but not with your butternut, which is moschata. Seed catalogues usually give the Latin name.

### Pepo

Many pepo are picked young as summer squash. A zucchini left on the vine until its seed is ripe becomes a marrow, which you can use as a winter squash.

### Moschata

Butternut and its relatives have the best name of the three for shrugging off pests and diseases: their hard vines and fruit stalks keep squash vine borers out. They keep their quality through long storage.

### Maxima

Hubbard, buttercup and banana types grow vigorously, ripen quickly and crop well. Where vine borers are common, though, they ruin these thick, juicy stems and corky fruit stalks.

### Melons

Muskmelon (cantaloupe) and honeydew are one species, so they cross. Lofthouse wants his muskmelons netted and orange inside, so his green-fleshed melons grow in another field. Cucumbers and watermelons are separate species, and their hybrids are fine to save from too.

## Starting in a short season

If your summers are too short for most varieties, don't hunt for the one that ripens. Sow many and let the season choose.

The first year of Lofthouse's moschata landrace, frost came 88 days after sowing, and three varieties in four made no fruit. He gathered what green fruit there was, left it indoors for a few months, and only then took out the seed. Early frosts kept up the pressure. By the third year the plants gave plenty of ripe fruit in an 84-day season.

You can use the same trick anywhere. Seed can grow long before its fruit is ripe, and it keeps maturing inside a picked fruit. Before the first frost, bring in every fruit you want seed from, ripe or green. Keep the green ones in the house for a few months before you open them. Their seedlings may be a little less vigorous, but they grow.

His watermelons started from about 700 seeds, the offspring of crosses among hundreds of varieties. He harvested five fruits that first year and counts that as a good start. The most dependable seed came from growers nearby, with his climate, soil and pests, so ask your neighbours first.

## Keep what defines it, let the rest vary

A landrace doesn't have to look like a jumble. Pick the one or two traits that make the crop what it is for you, keep selecting for those, and let the rest wander.

- **Crookneck:** yellow skin and a crooked neck. Anything else may vary.
- **Zucchini:** long, slim fruit on a bushy plant, in any skin colour.
- **Muskmelon:** netted skin and orange flesh.

Lofthouse grows acorn and Delicata together as one <Term id="hybrid-swarm">hybrid swarm</Term> and doesn't mind what shape or skin colour comes out. To keep both shapes, grow them as <Term id="sister-lines">sister lines</Term>: acorn at one end of the row, Delicata at the other. Each end mostly pollinates itself, and the crosses gather in the middle.

<PlotDiagram variant="row-ends" />

His butternuts began as classic, long-neck and round types sown together, and their offspring came in every shape. His market customers liked the long necks best, so he now sows about nine long-neck seeds to one round. The long neck stays dominant, and the diversity stays in the population.

Don't chase tiny fruit. When he selected the smallest butternuts in a field of their own, they stored badly, their seed was small and the plants were weak, so he gave up. He now aims for medium to large fruit, which also gives more food for the same work and space.

## Choose seed parents in the kitchen

Lofthouse judges every squash before he saves its seed, a batch of about sixteen at a time. What you look for is up to you: how the fruit kept, how easily it cuts and peels, its smell and the colour of its flesh.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Check the stored fruit">Note how each fruit has kept since harvest. Cut it open and look at the colour and thickness of the flesh.</Step>
  <Step title="Judge it as food">Only squash you would normally eat is judged at the table. A bitter fruit is not food: read the caution below.</Step>
  <Step title="Keep the best">Save seed only from fruits that pleased you on every count. The rest go on the compost.</Step>
</Steps>

Selection works fast. By choosing deep orange flesh, he made his squash darker every year. Because he cut every fruit he judged and threw out the hard ones, his squash became easy to cut. In his long-neck butternuts he also chose thin, tender skin, which now comes off with an ordinary vegetable peeler.

## Bitter fruit

Strong bitterness in a squash, melon or cucumber is a warning sign of natural toxins. A <Term id="bitter-fruit">bitter fruit</Term> is not food, and its seed must stay out of your population, or the bitterness can return in later generations. Lofthouse once threw out a whole year's melon seed after a cross with a small, perfumed "pocket melon" spoiled the fruit.

<FoodCaution risk="bitter-cucurbits" />

Ornamental gourds are close to wild squash, and in his experience they can bring that bitterness into a pepo population.

## Save the seed

- **Squash:** put the seed in a colander and clean it with a strong jet of water. It needs no fermenting: any gel left on it dries and blows away when you winnow.
- **Melon and watermelon:** there is little gel, so rinse the seed in a colander straight away.
- **Cucumber:** each seed sits in a <Term id="gel-coat">gel coat</Term> that holds back sprouting. Use <Term id="seed-fermentation">seed fermentation</Term>: leave the seed in its juice until the gel breaks down, then rinse.

Dry everything spread thin.

## Crossing species (advanced)

Tetsukabuto is a Japanese maxima × moschata hybrid. Its male flowers wither before they shed pollen, so it cannot pollinate itself, but among other squash the bees bring it pollen.

Lofthouse sowed the seed it set. He first kept the plants whose fertility had come back, then chose for maxima flavour on thin, hard vines, and calls the result 'Maximoss'. Growers where vine borers are a problem report that it resists them: their reports, not a trial. A second line, 'Moschamax', went towards the butternut shape. He thinks hand-pollinating chosen parents would speed this up.
