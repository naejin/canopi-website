Can you eat dry beans that are not quite soft? Why do recipes insist on soaking? Is a slow cooker safe for beans? This guide explains why raw and undercooked legume seeds are dangerous, what traditional preparation does, and which legumes need extra care. It describes Lofthouse's practice without amounts on purpose: for the method, follow your national food-safety authority.

## Which legumes

The warning covers the dry, ripe seeds of every legume a seed saver is likely to grow. Lofthouse grows many of them to spread the risk of a bad year, among them:

- **Beans:** common beans of every colour, tepary, lima, runner beans, cowpeas, soybeans.
- **Peas and lentils:** garden and winter peas, lentils, chickpeas.
- **Favas, lupini and grasspea**, which each carry a danger of their own (see [Lupini, grasspea and fava](#lupini-grasspea-and-fava)).

Fresh green pods are a separate, milder case, covered below. To grow and save any of these, see [Beans and other legumes](/guides/beans-and-legumes/).

## Why raw or undercooked seeds are dangerous

Legume seeds, beans above all, contain natural compounds that Lofthouse calls <Term id="anti-nutrient">anti-nutrients</Term> or, more bluntly, poisons. Eaten raw or undercooked, the seeds can make you ill.

Traditional preparation exists to bring those compounds down. In Lofthouse's words it rests on three things: long soaking, rinsing, and cooking at high heat.

Lofthouse goes further. He wonders whether the stomach upsets so many people blame on beans come from poisons that cooking did not fully destroy. That is his guess, not a finding.

## His routine, in words

This is how Lofthouse prepares peas and beans, described so you can see what each step is for. The times and amounts are left out on purpose.

<Steps>
  <Step title="His sorting">He picks out stones and rubbish and rinses the seed.</Step>
  <Step title="His long soak">He soaks the seed in cold water for a long while, pouring off the water and rinsing several times.</Step>
  <Step title="His hard boil and rest">He brings the beans to a hard boil, turns off the heat, leaves them to stand, and rinses them again.</Step>
  <Step title="His final cooking">Only then do they go in with the other ingredients, cooking until fully soft.</Step>
</Steps>

Each step, in his account, lowers these compounds; none is said to remove them all.

## Pressure cooking and altitude

Lofthouse's favourite tool for dry beans is the pressure cooker. He trusts it to get hot enough to destroy the poisons quickly and completely. That is his view, not a promise that any cooking makes beans safe, and his own method still starts with the soak.

Altitude matters too. In his high-elevation kitchen, pressure cooking softens beans much faster than an open pot. If you live high up, check your national food-safety authority's advice for your altitude.

## Slow cookers

<Safety title="Do not start raw beans in a slow cooker">It may never get hot enough to destroy their toxins. Use it only to warm beans already fully cooked another way.</Safety>

Lofthouse advises against slow cookers for cooking beans for exactly this reason, and keeps them for reheating.

## Green pods

Green beans, picked young as a vegetable, contain far less of these compounds than dry seed, Lofthouse says, but not none. He still cooks them well done, and prefers a pressure cooker or a hot pan of oil to boiling them. He does not eat the pods of tepary or lima beans at all.

## Chickpea flour

Chickpea flour is ground from raw, dry chickpeas. Lofthouse distrusts dishes where that flour is only briefly warmed, such as thin pancakes: in his view, such short cooking does not undo the seed's poisons. The traditional route, as he describes it, is to soak the chickpeas, pressure-cook and mash them, then fry them as falafel; that is his account, and recipes differ. Before you make anything from chickpea flour, check your national food-safety authority's advice.

## Lupini, grasspea and fava

These three carry dangers beyond those of other legumes.

<Points ruled="accent">
- **Lupini.** Of all the legumes he names, lupini are the most poisonous in his account (see the warning below). The recipe he describes prepares them by a long leaching in water changed again and again, or in running water. Ask your national food-safety authority before eating them.
- **Grasspea.** Lofthouse lists it among his legumes. {/* plain warning, not from the book */} Grasspea seeds can cause lathyrism, a lasting paralysis. Ask your national food-safety authority before eating them.
- **Fava.** Some people inherit favism, a condition in which eating favas can cause a serious reaction. If it could run in your family, ask a doctor first.
</Points>

<Safety title="Never taste raw legume seeds">Lofthouse says he tastes raw bean seeds out of curiosity, and that lupini are the most poisonous he has tasted. Do not copy him: raw beans and lupini can poison you, and nothing raw from the seed jar goes in your mouth.</Safety>

Beans from your seed jar raise one more question: seed kept for sowing may have been treated, or stored in ways food is not.

## Before you start

<Checklist id="before-you-start">
  <Item id="read-guidance" when="any">Find your national food-safety authority's guidance on dry beans and pulses, and follow it.</Item>
  <Item id="sort-seed" when="after-harvest">Sort out stones, mouldy seed and damaged seed.</Item>
  <Item id="keep-apart" when="after-harvest">Keep seed for sowing in its own labelled jars, apart from beans for the table.</Item>
  <Item id="label-special" when="after-harvest">Label lupini, grasspea and favas clearly, so no one mistakes them for ordinary beans.</Item>
  <Item id="favism" when="any">If favism could run in your family, ask a doctor before favas reach the table.</Item>
</Checklist>
