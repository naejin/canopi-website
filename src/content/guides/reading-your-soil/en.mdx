Is your soil sandy or clay? Why does it smell sour? Is anything living in it? A jar, a spade, your nose and two simple traps answer all three. Soil changes from bed to bed, so test each area on its own, as Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis have done many times over in their Alaska yards. What you are counting is the <Term id="soil-food-web">soil food web</Term>, introduced in [Living soil](/guides/living-soil/).

<QuickAnswer>
Good soil is dark, crumbly and smells of fresh earth; a sour or rotten smell means it lacks air. Shaken in a jar of water, soil settles into sand, silt and clay you can measure. Then count what lives there: sort a dug square, sink a cup for what walks by, and drive the tiny animals out of a sample with a lamp. In the authors' reading, worms, springtails, mites, beetles and fungal threads mean a working food web; few finds mean work to do.
</QuickAnswer>

## Start with a handful

Take a few handfuls from different spots and compare them. Look at the colour, sniff, and rub a pinch between your fingers.

- **Gritty:** sand.
- **Smooth, like flour:** silt.
- **Slippery, and it keeps a shape when squeezed:** clay.

The authors picture good soil as dark as coffee and crumbly, keeping water yet letting the excess run away; poor soil is pale, packed hard, too dry or soggy, and may smell bad. What counts as good also depends on what you grow.

## Colour and smell

Colour mostly tells you about organic matter and air. Dark brown comes from organic matter, much of it <Term id="humus">humus</Term>.

| What you see or smell | What it suggests |
|---|---|
| Dark brown | Plenty of organic matter |
| Red or yellow tints | Iron; usually a sign of good drainage and air |
| Purple-black | Manganese; also usually good drainage and air |
| Grey | Little organic matter, and often too little air |
| A fresh, earthy smell | Thread-forming bacteria at work: the smell of good soil |
| Rotten eggs, vomit, vinegar or sharp ammonia | Decay without air |

The bad smells matter most. Airless (<Term id="anaerobic">anaerobic</Term>) soil, the authors explain, favours disease organisms and kills the helpful life that needs air (<Term id="aerobic">aerobic</Term>). They blame poor texture, too little pore space, standing water and compaction. Keep off wet soil and add organic matter on top rather than digging it in: see [Vegetable beds without digging](/guides/no-dig-beds/) and [Mulch](/guides/mulch/). Smelling is the whole test.

## The jar test

A jar shows your soil's <Term id="soil-texture">texture</Term>: its shares of sand, silt and clay. You need a 1-litre jar (the book says a quart), about 500 ml (2 cups) of water, a tablespoon of liquid water softener to stop the clay clumping, and soil from the top 30 cm (12 in) of one area.

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Leave plenty of room above the soil and water, so the jar can be shaken hard.

<Steps compact>
<Step title="Fill and shake">

Put everything in the jar, close it and shake hard until nothing lies on the bottom.

</Step>
<Step title="Let it settle">

Sand drops within minutes, silt over a few hours, much of the clay only after up to a day; organic bits float longest.

</Step>
<Step title="Measure">

After a day (the book says 24 hours), measure each layer.

</Step>
<Step title="Work out the shares">

Each layer's thickness ÷ the total × 100.

</Step>
</Steps>

The authors aim for 30–50% sand, 30–50% silt and 20–30% clay in a garden soil, plus 5–10% organic matter. That is roughly a <Term id="loam">loam</Term>, with sand, silt and clay in near-equal parts.

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Use one jar per area and label each one.

## Dig and sort

Lift a square of soil, roughly 30 cm (12 in) a side, onto a tarp or into a box. Go through it with your fingers and note every worm, beetle and grub big enough to pick out. In a square that size of good garden soil, the authors expect 10 to 50 earthworms.

Naming finds is hard at first. Ask a naturalists' group, a gardening club or a university; in the authors' experience you learn fast.

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Put the soil back when you are done.

## A pitfall trap

A dig misses the animals that run about on top. For those, sink a cup of about 1 litre (the book says a quart) so its rim sits flush with the ground; where it rains a lot, the authors shade it with a cover, even an open umbrella. Use one trap per area, left for several days up to a week, and check it often.

Expect beetles, millipedes and centipedes, perhaps a slug or a worm. An empty trap, in the authors' reading, means links lower down the web are missing.

The book adds a little antifreeze or a mothball or two to kill the catch, so the animals do not eat each other before the count. The authors note these are not bait and can be left out.

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Our suggestion: leave the cup empty, count the catch and let it go.

<Safety title="Antifreeze and mothballs are poisons">
Both are poisonous to children, pets and wild animals; the book itself says to skip antifreeze around children and pets. Leave them out: an empty trap works, and you only lose part of the catch to hungry neighbours.
</Safety>

## A Berlese funnel

Animals too small to sort by hand are counted with a <Term id="berlese-funnel">Berlese funnel</Term>, named after its inventor, Berlese: light and warmth from above drive them down through a mesh into a pot.

/>
</Figure>

The funnel is a 1-litre plastic bottle with its base cut off, standing neck down. The authors' figures for the rest:

- **Light:** a 40–60 W incandescent bulb about 15 cm (6 in) above the soil, on for three days or more, ideally a week.
- **Sample:** soil with its surface litter, down to about 20 cm (8 in).
- **Mesh:** a scrap of window screen about 5 cm (2 in) across, with 1.5–3 mm holes, wedged in the neck.
- **Pot:** about 1 litre, a large yoghurt pot for example; it catches the animals and steadies the bottle.

The book also offers mothballs on the soil in place of the bulb, and antifreeze or ethyl alcohol in the pot to kill what falls in. The pot does its job without them; you just lose a few animals to the others.

<Safety title="A hot lamp can start a fire">
A bulb close to dry soil, litter and a plastic bottle can set them alight; the authors themselves warn against overheating. Stand the funnel on a surface that cannot burn, away from curtains, paper and fabric, out of reach of children and pets, and check on it often. Alcohol in the pot catches fire easily near a hot bulb, and antifreeze and mothballs are poisons: leave all three out.
</Safety>

Look at the catch with a magnifying glass: mites, <Term id="springtail">springtails</Term>, tiny beetles, larvae of several kinds. The authors, lifelong gardeners, say most were new to them. Any trap shows only what was moving at the time: a snapshot, not a full count.

## What your finds mean

| Find | What the authors read in it |
|---|---|
| Earthworms or their castings | The whole web is probably there, since worms eat bacteria, fungi and protozoa. A wormy bed is likely run by bacteria. |
| Beetles, centipedes, millipedes, spiders, even a few slugs | A healthy web. Ground and rove beetles, spiders and birds keep slugs in check. |
| Many mites in the funnel, fast hunters among them | A well-stocked web: hunters stay where there is prey. Vegetable beds want many bacteria-eating mites and variety. |
| Springtails that jump when you brush the litter aside | Plenty of organic matter. |
| White threads in rotting leaves or under mulch; mushrooms | Fungi at work. |
| A slimy tan, yellow or pink blob creeping over mulch or lawn | A slime mould: a curiosity that helps cycle nutrients, not a plant problem. |
| Birds hunting on the lawn; molehills | Worms and grubs below. Deal with the food source, not with poison. |
| Few finds, or none | The web needs rebuilding. |

White threads are fungal <Term id="hyphae">hyphae</Term> (more in [Mycorrhizal fungi](/guides/mycorrhizal-fungi/)). Found little? Start with [Compost that feeds soil life](/guides/compost-for-soil-life/) and [Mulch](/guides/mulch/).

A test anyone can run: lay leaves on a patch of soil. In a garden free of pesticides and salt fertilisers, leaves gone within about six months in a temperate climate tell the authors the base of the web is probably healthy.

Mushrooms are a sign to read, never a harvest.

## Lawn weeds as signals

| Weed | The authors' reading |
|---|---|
| Moss | The soil is already fungal and acidic. They push it back toward bacteria with a thin layer of bacterial compost on top. |
| Chickweed | Too much nitrate, usually from lawn fertiliser: stop it and build up the fungi. |
| Dandelions | A surface soil short of calcium. Their deep roots bring it up over time, and more fungi speed this up. |
| Clover or couch grass | Too little nitrogen being cycled: add grazers such as protozoa and nematodes with compost. |
| Many mushrooms, or fairy rings | The fungal side is out of balance: use more varied compost. |

The authors also use compost tea on lawns; read [Compost tea](/guides/compost-tea/) before you do.

## Lab tests

Standard soil tests measure nutrients and pH, not life. Some labs also measure fungal and bacterial biomass, the figure that matters in the authors' view. They read a clearly alkaline pH around the roots as a sign of a bacterial soil, and an acid one as fungal: see [Trees like fungi, vegetables like bacteria](/guides/fungal-or-bacterial/).
