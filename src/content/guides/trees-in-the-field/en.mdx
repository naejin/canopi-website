You grow grain or vegetables and would like trees too, without giving up the field. This guide covers the ways trees and annual crops share ground, a branch of <Term id="agroforestry">agroforestry</Term>, following Eric Toensmeier's *The Carbon Farming Solution*. Trees with animals are in [Trees for livestock](/guides/trees-for-livestock/), all-perennial gardens in [A layered homegarden](/guides/food-forest/).

<QuickAnswer>
You can keep sowing annual crops among trees. Run rows of useful trees through the field, or hedgerows you cut back each year for mulch, or let scattered trees and old stumps grow. Choose trees with deep roots and light shade, ideally bare while your crop grows, and cut hedges back before they shade the crop. In dry places trees compete hard for water: there, keep them few.
</QuickAnswer>

Trees can stand scattered through a field, run in rows between strips of crops, grow as hedges you cut back, or share the field only while they are young. The author treats each as a building block to combine. And crops grown together can yield more than the same land split between them, a gain he reports for many mixtures.

Which to try first depends mostly on your water. On dry land, start with scattered trees and stumps that regrow: hedges between crops drink too much there. With more rain, try rows of crop trees or cut hedgerows. In any climate, new trees leave room for crops while they are small; and if you have a pasture and mild winters, try grain in the pasture.

## Scattered trees

<Term id="parkland">Parkland</Term>, trees scattered through cropland, is the most widespread kind of agroforestry, the author says; in Europe it takes the form of fruit and nut trees among crops and vines.

Some trees lower the crop's yield under their crowns, others raise it; even the first kind give fruit, nuts, fodder or firewood and build the soil, so weigh both. Parkland stores little carbon, with its few trees on dry land, but many better systems grew from it.

## Let the stumps regrow

<Term id="farmer-managed-natural-regeneration">Farmer-managed natural regeneration</Term> plants nothing: the live stumps and roots already in a field sprout again, and each year you cut some new stems, prune others hard and let a few grow into trees, for firewood, poles, fodder, shelter and better soil.

It began in Niger in the 1980s, where tree planting had mostly failed, and spread across millions of hectares of dry farmland. Its pioneer, Tony Rinaudo, saw the obstacle as social: the law made trees the state's, and farmers saw field trees as weeds. Before you try it, find out who owns trees on your land under local law.

A few trees drop their leaves when the rains come and leaf out in the dry season, the reverse of most. Under them the crop gets light and water while it grows, and the field stays green all year: the book calls this evergreen agriculture.

Crops under these trees often yield more in the studies the author cites, though farmers say it takes years to show. Three cautions: cut the tree too hard and it leafs out in the rains, shading the crop after all; a wide planting of one tree invites its pests; and one star tree should not crowd out other useful ones. Look among your own native trees for this habit, the author urges.

## Rows of crop trees

In <Term id="strip-intercropping">strip intercropping</Term>, rows of trees grown for their own harvest (timber, fruit, nuts) alternate with field crops: the most common temperate system, says the author.

The book reports French trials in which timber trees in grain fields cost the crop nothing at first and only a slow decline later, and 100 hectares of trees and crops together matched the output of 130 to 140 hectares farmed separately. The crop's tillage and feeding even pushed the trees' roots deeper, so they coped better with drought.

Look for trees with **deep roots** that leave the topsoil to the crop, **light shade**, and **late leaves**, so an early crop gets its light first.

Put shade-tolerant crops near the tree rows and sun lovers in the open middle. Trees take water, light and nutrients, and some release substances that hold nearby plants back (<Term id="allelopathy">allelopathy</Term>), so expect a dip in yield right next to the rows.

Space the rows for your widest machine, which can also scrape trunks; {/* general tip, not from the book */}in a garden, for the barrow.

In the Guatemalan highlands, Roberto Muj runs a one-hectare market garden of tree rows with flowers, beans, vegetables and herbs between them.

## Alley cropping

In <Term id="alley-cropping">alley cropping</Term>, hedges of woody plants, usually a <Term id="nitrogen-fixer">nitrogen fixer</Term>, run between alleys of crops. You cut the hedges back regularly (<Term id="coppice">coppice</Term>) and lay the cuttings on the alleys as mulch. Unlike tree rows, the hedge is there mostly to serve the crop.

It brings organic matter, mulch and nitrogen, and it holds soil; the cost is competition. Light you can manage: cut the hedge before it shades the crop. Water you cannot, so in the author's view alley cropping is **a bad fit for semi-arid and arid places**.

On a slope, the same hedges laid along the contour hold the soil ([Contour hedgerows](/guides/water-and-slopes/#contour-hedgerows)). The cuts are in [Keep them low](/guides/trees-with-edible-leaves/#keep-them-low).

## Crops while the trees are young

While new trees are small, sow sun-loving annuals or short-lived crops between them (<Term id="successional-intercropping">successional intercropping</Term>): they bring food and income before the trees bear. A Nigerian farmer grows plantain between young rubber trees. More in [First years](/guides/food-forest/#first-years).

## Rest the soil with trees

In an <Term id="improved-fallow">improved fallow</Term>, a tired field is planted thickly with fast-growing woody plants, usually nitrogen fixers, instead of being left to grass. In a few years they make plenty of firewood and soil carbon.

The catch: ploughing when crops return burns much of that carbon off again. So some farmers keep fallow species that resprout, sow crops between the cut stumps and cut them back for mulch several times a year: the fallow becomes a lasting hedge-and-crop field. In the studies the author cites, it raised maize yields most on worn-out soils.

## Grain in a perennial pasture

Pasture cropping comes from Australia. A pasture of warm-season perennial grasses is grazed while it grows; when it goes dormant, a cool-season grain is drilled straight into it, without ploughing, and harvested as the pasture wakes. It needs winters mild enough to grow a grain. In the studies the author cites, the grain yields less than in an ordinary no-till field but costs less to grow, and in one of them the soil gained carbon.

## Trees for your climate

Pick trees for your climate from the blocks below, natives first, and check your local list of invasive plants before planting anything from elsewhere. Where winters bring snow, trees can give back part of their carbon gain: see [Honest limits](/guides/carbon-farming/#honest-limits).

Olives and African oil beans are not food as picked. Pigeon peas and the seeds of edible acacias are dry beans, and only named edible acacia species are food.

<FoodCaution risk={["needs-processing", "dry-beans"]} />

<ForClimate zones="tropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
For the humid tropics.

- **Pigeon pea**: a short-lived nitrogen fixer for hedgerows, cut back to renew it. Its dry seeds are a staple (caution above).
- **Bananas or plantains**: they can replace the annual crop between nitrogen-fixing hedgerows.
- **African oil bean** (*Pentaclethra macrophylla*): a nitrogen-fixing tree that sometimes drops its leaves in the rainy season, the author's candidate for the humid African tropics. Its beans need traditional processing (caution above).
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones={["tropical", "arid"]} source="carbon-farming-solution">
For dry savanna farms on the desert's edge, like the Sahel: the book's semi-arid tropics.

- **Apple-ring acacia** (*Faidherbia albida*): bare in the rainy season, so crops grow in its light; its leaves and pods feed livestock in the dry season. The book reports planting about 100 trees a hectare and thinning to about 25 as they mature.
- **Edible-seeded acacias**: Australian dryland shrubs that Niger's farmers plant as windbreaks along field edges. Only some acacia species have edible seed (caution above).
- **Baobab**: a long-lived tree of African parkland, kept for leaves, fruit and fodder; for the leaves, see [Trees with edible leaves](/guides/trees-with-edible-leaves/).

Shea and African locust bean, also kept in these fields, can lower the yield of the crop right under them.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="subtropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Mild winters with light frost; most of this block is for the tropical highlands, from Roberto Muj's garden above.

- **Avocado**: one of his tree rows.
- **Macadamia**: another row; frost damages it, so frost-free highlands only.
- **Mulberry**: a third row tree, whose leaves are a crop too ([Trees with edible leaves](/guides/trees-with-edible-leaves/)).

</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="mediterranean" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Winter rain and a dry summer: water decides how many trees.

- **Olive**: Greek farmers sow annual crops and grow vines among the trees. The fruit is not eaten from the tree (caution above).
- **Carob**: traditionally grown with olives, almonds, vines and field crops; it takes dry, stony slopes but needs a fair share of winter rain to set pods.
- **Grape**: the vine of these mixed fields.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="temperate" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "warm temperate"; the jujube line comes from dry western China.

- **Walnut and cherry**: timber rows in French grain-field trials the book reports, where about 50 mature trees a hectare kept crop losses lowest.
- **Paulownia**: a Chinese timber tree with deep roots and light shade that leafs out late, so winter grain beneath it gets its light.
- **Jujube**: a fruit tree for cold, dry places, grown between vegetables to cut wind damage.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="continental" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "cold temperate".

- **Black walnut**: a common alley tree in North America. It releases a substance that holds some neighbouring plants back, so test your crops near it.
- **Sea buckthorn** <Invasive />: a spiny, suckering nitrogen fixer grown in alleys with field crops in North Korea and northern China.

<Safety title="Spiny shrubs">
Sea buckthorn has sharp spines: keep it away from paths, and wear gloves to work it.
</Safety>
</ForClimate>
