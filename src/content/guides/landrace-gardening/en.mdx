## The idea in 30 seconds

Most garden seed is bred to be uniform. An <Term id="heirloom">heirloom</Term> is an old variety kept the same by saving its seed apart from other varieties, often for decades. An <Term id="f1-hybrid">F1 hybrid</Term> comes from crossing two inbred parents, and every plant in the packet is alike.

A <Term id="landrace">landrace</Term> is the opposite: a mixed population whose plants cross with each other. Its seed is <Term id="open-pollinated">open-pollinated</Term>, so you can save it and sow it again. Every year your weather, soil, pests and taste decide which plants leave seed, so the crop shifts a little toward your garden.

<Note title="Lofthouse's view of heirlooms">
Joseph Lofthouse describes heirlooms mostly by their inbreeding: generations of saving seed from a few plants kept apart. That is his framing; many seed savers value heirlooms for their history and their reliability.
</Note>

## What happens over the seasons

The first year is a trial, and losses are expected. The plants that fail show you what your garden will not support. The survivors carry what it needs, and from the second year they cross with each other. By the third year the offspring of the best plants start to perform.

## Two ways to start

You can start fast or gently. Both end in the same place: one jar of seed per crop that keeps changing with your garden.

<Columns>
<Card label="Fast" title="Sow a big mix">

Sow roughly equal amounts of 5 to 50 varieties of one crop together, a <Term id="grex">grex</Term>. Expect heavy losses in the first year and a lot of variety in the second.

</Card>
<Card label="Gentle" title="Add one row at a time">

Carry on with what you already grow and save its seed. Bring in one newcomer a year, grown beside your own: whatever does well goes into the same jar.

</Card>
</Columns>

### Plant so they cross

Most pollen lands on nearby plants. Varieties grown in separate blocks only meet along their edges. Mixed together, almost every plant has a neighbour of another variety.

### Your first landrace, step by step

<Note title="Where to find seed">
Neighbours, a market where farmers sell what they grow, seed swaps, mixed packets, even a bag of mixed soup beans from the grocery.
</Note>

<Steps>
<Step title="Pick a crop that crosses easily">

Squash, corn, melons, cucumbers and fava beans cross readily, so new combinations arrive quickly. The list below compares crops.

</Step>
<Step title="Gather seed from many sources">

Diversity matters more than pedigree. A few seeds each of many varieties beat a full packet of one.

</Step>
<Step title="Sow them mixed and close together">

Jumble the varieties in the same row or bed, as in the mixed diagram above.

</Step>
<Step title="Grow without coddling">

Give your usual care and no more. Plants that only survive with extra help pass that weakness on to their seed.

</Step>
<Step title="Save seed from everything that thrives">

Keep seed from every plant that grew well with your usual care, in a range of shapes and colours. For crops you store, such as squash, favour the fruit that kept well. If you also choose by flavour, do it at the table, never in the garden: only fruit of a crop you already eat, prepared the way you always prepare it (see [Judge what you harvest](/guides/choosing-seed-parents/#judge-what-you-harvest), and the caution just below). One jar per crop is enough; records are optional.

</Step>
<Step title="Replant, share, repeat">

Mix in some older seed each year and swap with neighbours. A landrace kept by many gardens is larger and safer than one kept by a single garden.

</Step>
</Steps>

<FoodCaution risk="bitter-cucurbits" />

<Note title="Select generously">
Save more seed from the best plants and some from those that are merely good. A wide selection keeps the population flexible.
</Note>

## Which crops are easiest?

Annual crops that cross freely become landraces fastest. Crops that mostly pollinate themselves, or must survive a winter before they flower, take longer. The tiers below are in Lofthouse's experience, in his dry mountain garden.

A crop that relies on <Term id="outcrossing">outcrossing</Term>, like corn or squash, mixes its varieties every year. A <Term id="self-pollinating">self-pollinating</Term> crop, like most beans, peas and lettuce, crosses only now and then.

<CropExplorer />

For a crop that is not listed: annuals whose flowers draw many pollinators, or whose pollen travels on the wind, are toward the easy end.

<Note title="Pollen-sterile hybrids">
Lofthouse lists the commercial hybrids of several crops as often <Term id="male-sterile">male sterile</Term>: carrot, beet, onion, the cabbage family, radish and sunflower, and he flags lettuce too. They make no pollen and can pass this on. Check the flowers for anthers and pull plants that have none.
</Note>

## Read the flower

A flower's shape tells you how often it crosses, and tomatoes show this clearly. In most garden tomatoes the <Term id="anther">anthers</Term> fuse into a cone around the <Term id="stigma">stigma</Term>, so each flower pollinates itself. In wild-type flowers the stigma sticks out, where a visiting bee brushes it with pollen from other plants.

## Crosses you will not want

Mixing is the point, but a few crosses spoil a crop for the kitchen.

<FoodCaution risk="bitter-cucurbits" />

- **Gourds into squash.** Ornamental gourds cross with edible squash. Grow them well away from food squash, or not at all.
- **Hot into sweet peppers.** Heat crossed into sweet peppers takes years to select out. Keep them apart.
- **Sweet corn with flour or pop corn.** The cross gives starchy sweet corn. Grow corn types used in different ways in separate patches.
- **Heirlooms you love.** Mixing changes a variety for good. If you want to keep a favourite as it is, keep a packet of it aside.

## Keeping a landrace healthy

A landrace stays strong when it stays large and varied. Six habits do most of the work.

<Points>

- **Add new seed now and then.** Fresh genes prevent inbreeding and bring traits you lack.
- **Mix in older seed.** Lofthouse sows about a tenth to a third of each crop from earlier years' seed, so one odd season does not reshape it.
- **Grow enough plants.** Never replant from just a handful. Close spacing fits more plants into a small bed.
- **Favour crossed seed.** Seed from natural crosses pushes the population to cross even more.
- **Look after pollinators.** Insect-pollinated crops need bees. Avoid pesticides and grow flowers nearby.
- **Share it.** Every garden growing the same landrace makes its population larger.

</Points>

## Saving the seed

Seed comes out of the plant in one of two ways. **Dry seed**, from pods and heads (beans, peas, corn, grains, lettuce, the cabbage family), is left to dry on the plant, then threshed, screened and <Term id="winnow">winnowed</Term>. **Wet seed**, from inside a fruit (tomatoes, cucumbers, melons, squash), is washed out of the flesh; tomato and cucumber seed first sits in its own juice until the jelly around it breaks down, which is <Term id="seed-fermentation">seed fermentation</Term>. Either way, finish by drying the seed fast, spread out thin.

Each step, crop by crop, is in [Saving seed: dry and wet](/guides/saving-seed/).

### Store it

Keep seed cool, dark, dry and safe. Seal it only once it is dry right through: Lofthouse's check is to close a little of it in a jar, set the jar briefly in the sun, and look for droplets inside; if any form, dry it longer. Glass jars with metal lids keep mice out; in his experience, boxes and plastic tubs do not. Drying, containers, insects and backups are in [Storing seed](/guides/storing-seed/).

{/* A general tip, not a method from the book (which only mentions running germination tests): it gives no counts, times or rates. */}
Unsure whether older seed still grows? Do a <Term id="germination-test">germination test</Term> before sowing: sprout a few seeds in a damp cloth, and if only some come up, sow more thickly.
