Flowers are food. Nectar is the sugar a bee or a butterfly flies on; pollen is the protein bees raise their young on. A bed can be full of colour and still feed almost nothing, if its flowers hold little of either, or if they all open in the same few weeks. This guide follows Kim Eierman, who designs gardens for <Term id="pollinator">pollinators</Term> in the northeastern United States: what to choose, how much of it, and how to keep food coming all season.

<QuickAnswer>
Plant your region's own wild flowers first, in groups of three or more of a kind, with something flowering from the first warm days to the last. Choose single, open flowers of many shapes; skip double flowers and invasive plants, even when bees visit them. Add trees and shrubs, the largest masses of flowers, and the plants caterpillars eat.
</QuickAnswer>

## What flowers offer

- **Nectar** is mostly water and sugar: fuel for flying and keeping warm. How sweet it is, and how much a flower makes, changes from one plant to the next, sometimes within one species.
- **Pollen** brings protein, fats and minerals, and bees feed it to their larvae. Its quality varies widely too, so a diet from many kinds of flowers is healthier than a lot of one, in Eierman's account.
- **Pollen only.** Some flowers make no nectar and still feed bees with pollen. Most roses are like this: little nectar, plenty of pollen.
- **Wind-pollinated plants**, such as grasses, conifers, oaks and hazels, make their pollen for the wind. What insects can gather from them is scant and poor. They still earn their place, as food for caterpillars and shelter and food for birds, but a garden of them alone gives bees almost nothing.

Weather matters as well. Heat, cold and drought cut the nectar a plant makes, and long rainy, cloudy spells water it down. Water your flowers before they wilt: a thirsty plant stops feeding its visitors.

## Natives first

A <Term id="native-plant">native plant</Term> is one that has grown in a region for a very long time without people bringing it there. So a plant is never just "native": it is native to a place, and a species native to one valley may be a stranger a few hundred kilometres away.

Why plant them first? Local insects evolved with local plants. Many bees are <Term id="specialist-pollinator">specialist pollinators</Term>, gathering pollen from only a few related plants, and many caterpillars eat nothing else. Native plants also suit the soil, rain and winters around them. Eierman cites studies in which natives drew far more pollinators than plants from elsewhere.

Plants from elsewhere are not all a problem. Many stay put and feed bees that visit many kinds of flowers; beekeepers sometimes add a few for their honey bees. What they seldom do is feed caterpillars. Some escape: a <Term id="naturalised-plant">naturalised plant</Term> seeds itself into the wild, and an <Term id="invasive-plant">invasive plant</Term> spreads until it crowds out what grew there. Take invasives out even when bees crowd onto them, and look up every unfamiliar plant on your region's invasive list before you buy it.

The best-known case is butterfly bush <Invasive />, sold around the world as "the" butterfly plant. Adult butterflies drink its nectar, but Eierman points out that in North America no butterfly's caterpillars eat it, and it seeds itself into the wild; she calls it a nectar bush. The lesson holds anywhere: flowers for adult butterflies, with nothing for their caterpillars, raise no butterflies (see [host plants](#host-plants)).

To find your region's wild plants, ask a native-plant society, a nursery that grows from local seed, or a botanic garden. Then copy the layers of a wild place near you, from trees down to ground cover; grassland and desert have layers too, only harder to see. [Food forests](/guides/food-forest/) shows how to plant in layers.

## Flowers that feed

- **Double flowers.** A <Term id="double-flower">double flower</Term> carries extra petals where its stamens and nectar should be. It has little food, and what it has is hard to reach. Skip doubles, however pretty.
- **Sterile hybrids** set no seed and often give little food. Eierman treats them as garden ornaments: grow one if you love it, but do not count it as forage.
- **Wild forms first.** The <Term id="straight-species">straight species</Term>, the wild form grown from seed, carries the most genetic variety. Next comes a <Term id="cultivar">cultivar</Term> of a native plant, a <Term id="nativar">nativar</Term>, grown from seed (<Term id="open-pollinated">open-pollinated</Term>); last, one grown from cuttings, a <Term id="clone">clone</Term>, with every plant identical. Labels rarely say which: ask the nursery.
- **Botanical names.** One plant can have several common names, and one common name can mean different plants from one region to the next. Buy by the botanical name, and know that names are sometimes revised, so a plant may still be sold under its old one.
- **Odd colours and shapes.** A flower bred far from its wild form may not be recognised by the visitors it evolved with. Eierman's example is from the Americas: hummingbirds prefer the red tubes of a wild honeysuckle to a yellow selection of it.

Roses show the difference well. A wild rose with one ring of petals is open, and generous with pollen; a full double garden rose offers almost nothing. If you grow roses for pollinators, grow simple ones, native to your region where you can.

## Flower shapes

How far a visitor's tongue reaches decides which flowers it can drink from. Short-tongued bees, hoverflies and wasps need open, shallow flowers. Long-tongued bees, butterflies, moths and birds can reach down narrow tubes. Eierman's examples happen to be widely grown: borage has a very short flower tube, white clover a short one, lavender and viper's bugloss medium ones.

Flowers also signal who they are for, a pattern called a <Term id="pollination-syndrome">pollination syndrome</Term>. Treat it as a tendency, not a rule; hungry insects take what there is.

<Points>
- **Beetles** Open bowls with a strong, fruity smell.
- **Bees** Blue, violet, yellow or white flowers with a light scent, often marked with a <Term id="nectar-guide">nectar guide</Term> that points the way in.
- **Butterflies** Bright flowers with somewhere to stand: a wide lip or a flat cluster.
- **Flies** Dark or pale flowers, some smelling of rot.
- **Birds** Where nectar feeders live: red tubes and funnels with no scent, since birds cannot smell.
- **Moths** Pale flowers with a sweet scent at night.
</Points>

The more shapes you plant, the more kinds of visitors you feed.

## Enough of each

Many bees and butterflies stick to one kind of flower for a whole foraging trip, a habit called <Term id="floral-constancy">floral constancy</Term>. One plant of a kind is a snack, not a meal: the visitor wastes its strength flying on to find the next.

So plant <Term id="floral-target">floral targets</Term>. Eierman's rule is three or more plants of one kind set together. You can also repeat the same plant through the garden, so a visitor finds it again and again; a meadow-like planting does this by itself. A tree or a shrub in flower is a target on its own.

Balance many kinds, for many visitors, with enough of each, so they are found. In a small garden, repeat small groups rather than dotting single plants of many kinds. Pots count too: [From lawn to flowers](/guides/lawn-to-flowers/) covers balconies and small spaces.

## Bloom succession

Plan for <Term id="bloom-succession">bloom succession</Term>: one plant coming into flower as another fades, from the first active days of the year to the last. Many bees live only a few weeks as adults, and each kind flies in its own part of the season. If nothing flowers in its weeks, it goes hungry.

Find your gaps with a bloom inventory:

<Steps compact>
<Step title="Note what flowers">Through one year, write down every tree, shrub and smaller plant that flowers in your garden, and when. Early or late in each season is precise enough. Leave out double flowers: they do not count as food.</Step>
<Step title="Find the gaps">Look for the weeks with nothing in bloom, or only one or two kinds. Eierman aims for at least three kinds flowering at once.</Step>
<Step title="Plant for them">Choose your next plants for the gaps, before adding more of what already flowers.</Step>
</Steps>

The two ends of the season are where most gardens fall short. The first flowers of the year feed the bumblebee queens that start their colonies alone; the last ones feed bees about to spend the cold season asleep, and insects setting off on migration. A plant that flowers for only a week still counts, if that week is otherwise empty. Summer can thin out too, a <Term id="nectar-dearth">nectar dearth</Term> that hits honey bees hard ([Garden helpers](/guides/garden-helpers/#honey-bees)).

Do not forget trees and shrubs. One tree in flower holds more food than a whole bed of small plants, and many of the year's first flowers are on woody plants. Where they grow wild, willows and maples are among the earliest, and limes (basswoods) are major nectar trees. Set shrubs and small trees at the edges of a flower bed, where their shade will not cover the sunny flowers.

## Your climate

The principles above hold everywhere; the length of the flower season and the best time to plant do not.

<ForClimate zones={["temperate", "continental", "boreal"]} source="pollinator-victory-garden">
Where winters are cold. The book is written for the northeastern United States, where insects fly from early spring to late autumn.

- **Early spring:** bumblebee queens wake in cool weather, before most other bees, and need the first flowers, often on trees and shrubs.
- **Late autumn:** your region's own asters and goldenrods (*Solidago*) feed the last bees before winter.
- **Planting:** Eierman finds autumn a good time to plant in much of this range.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones={["subtropical", "arid"]} source="pollinator-victory-garden">
In warm regions and deserts, bees fly in winter too.

- **Winter flowers:** add plants that bloom in the cool season, so winter is not a gap.
- **Planting:** where autumn is still too hot for new plants, Eierman plants in winter.
</ForClimate>

## Host plants

Most caterpillars eat the leaves of only a few plants their kind evolved with, their <Term id="larval-host-plant">larval host plants</Term>. A butterfly lays her eggs on them and nowhere else. No host plant, no next generation, however many flowers there are.

Trees and shrubs host the most kinds. In the eastern United States, a study Eierman cites puts native oaks first, with cherries and willows close behind. The ranking will differ where you live; woody plants matter everywhere.

Make a host-plant list: which butterflies and moths live near you, which plants their caterpillars need, and which of those you already grow. A plant that feeds several kinds earns its place first. Short of room? Share with your neighbours: their tree may host the caterpillars of the butterflies your flowers feed. Eierman's goal is at least ten host plants.

One North American example: monarch caterpillars eat only milkweeds, so gardens there plant their region's own milkweeds. Eierman warns North American gardeners against tropical milkweed, which is not native there and can spread a monarch parasite and upset the migration.

Accept chewed leaves: a host plant doing its job looks eaten. And after flowering, leave some stems and seed heads standing, since many insects nest or spend the cold season in them ([Homes for pollinators](/guides/homes-for-pollinators/)).
