Flowers are food: nectar is the sugar a bee or a butterfly flies on, pollen the protein bees raise their young on. A colourful bed can still feed almost nothing, if its flowers hold little of either or all open in the same few weeks. This guide follows Kim Eierman, who designs gardens for <Term id="pollinator">pollinators</Term> in the northeastern United States: what to choose, how much of it, and how to keep food coming all season.

<QuickAnswer>
Plant your region's own wild flowers first, in groups of three or more of a kind, with something flowering from the first warm days to the last. Choose single, open flowers of many shapes; skip double flowers and invasive plants, even when bees visit them. Add trees and shrubs, the largest masses of flowers, and the plants caterpillars eat.
</QuickAnswer>

## What flowers offer

- **Nectar** is mostly water and sugar: fuel for flying and keeping warm. How sweet it is and how much a flower makes vary between plants, even within a species.
- **Pollen** brings protein, fats and minerals, and bees feed it to their larvae. Its quality varies too, and Eierman favours a mixed diet from many kinds of flowers.
- **Pollen only.** Some flowers make no nectar and still feed bees with pollen. Most roses are like this: little nectar, plenty of pollen.
- **Wind-pollinated plants**, such as grasses, conifers, oaks and hazels, make their pollen for the wind, and insects gather little of value from it. They still feed caterpillars and birds, but a garden of them alone gives bees almost nothing.

A bed that bees ignore in a heatwave or a drought, or after a wet, grey week, may simply have little nectar, or only watery nectar, to give. Water is the part you control: keep your flowers from wilting.

## Natives first

A <Term id="native-plant">native plant</Term> is one that has grown in a region for a very long time without people bringing it there. So a plant is never just "native": it is native to a place, and a species native to one valley may be a stranger a few hundred kilometres away.

Local insects evolved with local plants. Many bees are <Term id="specialist-pollinator">specialist pollinators</Term>, gathering pollen from only a few related plants, and many caterpillars eat nothing else. Natives also suit the local soil and weather. Eierman cites studies in which natives drew far more pollinators than plants from elsewhere.

Plants from elsewhere are not all a problem. Some behave well and feed bees that visit many kinds of flowers, though they seldom feed caterpillars. Others escape. A <Term id="naturalised-plant">naturalised plant</Term> seeds itself into the wild; Eierman advises avoiding them, since one may turn into a problem later. An <Term id="invasive-plant">invasive plant</Term> spreads until it crowds out what grew there. Take invasives out even when bees crowd onto them, and look up every unfamiliar plant on your region's invasive list before you buy it.

The best-known case is butterfly bush <Invasive />, sold worldwide as "the" butterfly plant. Adults drink its nectar, but Eierman points out that in North America no caterpillar eats it, and it seeds itself into the wild; she calls it a nectar bush. The lesson holds anywhere: flowers with nothing for caterpillars raise no butterflies (see [host plants](#host-plants)).

To find your region's wild plants, ask a native-plant society, a nursery that grows from local seed, or a botanic garden. Then copy the layers of a wild place near you, from trees down to ground cover. [Food forests](/guides/food-forest/) shows how to plant in layers.

## Flowers that feed

- **Double flowers.** A <Term id="double-flower">double flower</Term> carries extra petals where its stamens and nectar should be. Its little food is hard to reach. Skip doubles, however pretty.
- **Sterile hybrids** set no seed and often give little food. Eierman treats them as garden ornaments: grow one if you love it, but do not count it as forage.
- **Wild forms first.** The <Term id="straight-species">straight species</Term>, the wild form grown from seed, carries the most genetic variety. Next comes a <Term id="cultivar">cultivar</Term> of a native plant, a <Term id="nativar">nativar</Term>, grown from seed (<Term id="open-pollinated">open-pollinated</Term>); last, one grown from cuttings, a <Term id="clone">clone</Term>, with every plant identical. Labels rarely say which: ask the nursery.
- **Botanical names.** Shop with the botanical name: it is the only one that means the same plant everywhere. Note its older name too, since botanists revise names and labels lag behind.
- **Odd colours and shapes.** A flower bred far from its wild form may not be recognised by the visitors it evolved with. Eierman's example is from the Americas: hummingbirds are likely to prefer the red tubes of a wild honeysuckle to a yellow selection of it.

A wild rose with one ring of petals is open, and generous with pollen; a full double garden rose offers almost nothing. For pollinators, grow single-flowered roses, native to your region where you can.

## Flower shapes

How far a visitor's tongue reaches decides which flowers it can drink from. Short-tongued bees, most hoverflies and wasps need open, shallow flowers. Long-tongued bees, butterflies, moths and birds can reach down narrow tubes. Eierman's examples are widely grown: borage has a very short flower tube, white clover a short one, lavender and viper's bugloss medium ones.

Flowers also signal who they are for, a pattern called a <Term id="pollination-syndrome">pollination syndrome</Term>. Treat it as a tendency, not a rule; hungry insects take what there is.

<Points>
- **Beetles** Open bowls with a strong, fruity smell.
- **Bees** Blue, violet, yellow or white flowers with a light scent, often marked with a <Term id="nectar-guide">nectar guide</Term> that points the way in.
- **Butterflies** Bright flowers with somewhere to stand: a wide lip or a flat cluster.
- **Flies** Dark or pale flowers, some smelling of rot.
- **Birds** Where nectar feeders live: red tubes and funnels with little or no scent, found by their colour.
- **Moths** Pale flowers with a sweet scent at night.
</Points>

The more shapes you plant, the more kinds of visitors you feed. Eierman singles out phacelias as a honey-bee favourite. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Borage and lavender grow wild around the Mediterranean; elsewhere they are garden forage for bees that visit many flowers, not a replacement for your own wild flowers, so keep them from seeding into the wild. The annual *Phacelia tanacetifolia* is sown around the world as bee forage and green manure.

## Enough of each

Many bees and butterflies stick to one kind of flower for a whole foraging trip, a habit called <Term id="floral-constancy">floral constancy</Term>. One plant of a kind is a snack, not a meal: the visitor wastes its strength searching for the next.

So plant <Term id="floral-target">floral targets</Term>. Eierman's rule is three or more plants of one kind set together. Repeating a plant through the garden works too, and a meadow-like planting does it by itself. A tree or a shrub in flower is a target on its own.

Balance many kinds with enough of each. In a small garden, repeat small groups rather than dotting single plants. Pots count too: [From lawn to flowers](/guides/lawn-to-flowers/) covers balconies and small spaces.

## Bloom succession

Plan for <Term id="bloom-succession">bloom succession</Term>: one plant coming into flower as another fades, from the first active days of the year to the last. Many bees live only a few weeks as adults, each kind in its own part of the season; if nothing flowers then, it goes hungry.

Find your gaps with a bloom inventory:

<Steps compact>
<Step title="Note what flowers">Through one year, write down every tree, shrub and smaller plant that flowers in your garden, and when. Early or late in each season is precise enough. Leave out double flowers: they do not count as food.</Step>
<Step title="Find the gaps">Look for the weeks with nothing in bloom, or only one or two kinds. Eierman aims for at least three kinds flowering at once.</Step>
<Step title="Plant for them">Choose your next plants for the gaps, before adding more of what already flowers.</Step>
</Steps>

Most gardens fall short at the two ends of the season. Where bumblebees live, the first flowers of the year feed the queens that start their colonies alone; the last ones feed bees about to spend the cold season asleep, and insects setting off on migration. A plant that flowers for a week still counts, if that week is otherwise empty. Summer can thin out too, a <Term id="nectar-dearth">nectar dearth</Term> that hits honey bees hard ([Honey bees are one bee among thousands](/guides/garden-helpers/#honey-bees-are-one-bee-among-thousands)).

Do not forget trees and shrubs: one tree in flower holds more food than a whole bed of small plants, and many of the year's first flowers are on woody plants. Where they grow wild, willows and maples are among the earliest, and limes (basswoods) are major nectar trees. Set shrubs and small trees at a bed's edges, where they will not shade the flowers.

## Your climate

The principles above hold everywhere; the length of the flower season and the best time to plant do not.

<ForClimate zones={["temperate", "continental", "boreal"]} source="pollinator-victory-garden">
Where winters are cold. The book is written for the northeastern United States, where insects fly from early spring to late autumn.

- **Early spring:** bumblebee queens wake in cool weather, before most other bees, and need the first flowers, often on trees and shrubs.
- **Late autumn:** your region's own asters and goldenrods (*Solidago*) feed the last bees before winter.
- **Planting:** in much of the United States, Eierman finds autumn a good time to plant.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones={["subtropical", "arid"]} source="pollinator-victory-garden">
In subtropical and desert regions, Eierman advises winter-flowering plants as well.

- **Winter flowers:** cool-season bloomers keep winter from being a gap.
- **Planting:** where autumn is still too hot for new plants, Eierman suggests planting in winter.
</ForClimate>

## Host plants

Most caterpillars eat the leaves of only a few plants their kind evolved with, their <Term id="larval-host-plant">larval host plants</Term>. A butterfly lays her eggs on them and nowhere else. No host plant, no next generation, however many flowers there are.

Trees and shrubs host the most kinds. In the mid-Atlantic United States, a study Eierman cites puts native oaks first, then cherries and willows. The ranking will differ where you live; woody plants matter everywhere.

Make a host-plant list: the butterflies and moths near you, the plants their caterpillars need, and which of those you already grow. A plant that feeds several kinds comes first. Short of room? Share with neighbours: their tree may host the caterpillars of butterflies your flowers feed. Eierman's goal is at least ten host plants.

A North American example: monarch caterpillars eat only milkweeds, so gardens there plant their region's own. Eierman warns gardeners there against tropical milkweed, which is not native and can spread a monarch parasite and upset the migration.

Accept chewed leaves: a host plant doing its job looks eaten. After flowering, leave some stems and seed heads standing; many insects nest or spend the cold season in them ([Nests, shelter and winter homes for pollinators](/guides/homes-for-pollinators/)).
