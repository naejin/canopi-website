Will your tomatoes cross if you grow several kinds together? Do you need to keep them apart to save seed? The short answer comes first. The second half is for gardeners who want their tomatoes to cross more, so the crop can adapt to their garden.

<QuickAnswer>
Garden tomatoes mostly pollinate themselves. Joseph Lofthouse puts their natural crossing at about 3 to 5%, depending on the shape of the flower and the bees around. So varieties grown side by side mostly come true, with the odd surprise. Flowers whose stigma pokes out past the anthers cross more. To help your tomatoes adapt, alternate varieties close together, save from plants with open flowers, and look after the bumblebees. Save the seed by fermenting it.
</QuickAnswer>

## Do tomatoes cross?

Rarely. Tomatoes are <Term id="self-pollinating">self-pollinating</Term>: in most flowers, a plant’s own pollen reaches the stigma before a bee can bring any from elsewhere. Lofthouse estimates that garden tomatoes cross at about 3 to 5%. Across his mostly selfing crops, he finds the rate moves with the weather, the insects about and the variety.

For ordinary seed saving, that means:

- **You can grow several varieties in one bed and save seed from each.** Most of it will come true.
- **Expect the odd surprise.** A cross shows up the next year as a plant unlike the rest of its variety.
- **Beefsteaks cross more than most,** because their flowers are often loosely closed (see below).

<CropCard crop="tomato-garden" />

Tomato seed sits in a jelly coat. You free it by <Term id="seed-fermentation">seed fermentation</Term>: squeeze the seeds and juice into a jar and leave it until the <Term id="gel-coat">gel coat</Term> breaks down, after a few days. Then add water, pour off the pulp that floats, rinse the seed that sinks, and dry it well. Label the jar and keep it out of the kitchen: it is seed, not food.

## Why tomatoes need more crossing

Each time tomatoes travelled, only a small sample went: from the Andes to Mexico, from Mexico to Europe, and from Europe around the world. Then came long years of heirloom keeping, with each variety kept pure. Every step was a <Term id="genetic-bottleneck">genetic bottleneck</Term>, and the long selfing brings <Term id="inbreeding-depression">inbreeding depression</Term>.

For you, a narrow base means slow adaptation: when a new pest or a new climate arrives, domestic tomatoes have little to choose from. How narrow? Lofthouse points to a study where all the domestic lines it sampled, put together, held less genetic diversity than a single wild tomato type.

## Raise crossing in any garden

You do not need wild tomatoes to get more crosses. Change how you plant and what you save.

- **Alternate varieties along the row.** Plant A, B, A, B rather than a block of each, so every plant has a different neighbour.
- **Plant close.** In his dry garden, Lofthouse sets plants in tight clumps, or in a row with plants about 15 cm (6 in) apart. In a humid garden, crowded plants stay damp: see the note on humid gardens under “Selecting for your garden” below.
- **Save from the most open flowers.** Each summer, mark the plants whose stigmas stick out, and save their seed first.
- **Feed and house the wild bees.** Grow bee flowers near the tomatoes, keep a patch of bare ground you never dig for bees that nest in soil, and use no insecticides. Tomato flowers offer bees pollen but no nectar; in Lofthouse's garden the pollinating is done by wild bees, bumblebees among them, rather than honeybees.
- **Give odd plants a good spot.** A plant unlike its row may be a natural cross. Grow its seed next year where it can do well.

## “Promiscuous” and “panamorous” tomatoes

This is Lofthouse’s long-running breeding project, and both words are his.

- **Promiscuous** tomatoes are <Term id="self-incompatible">self-incompatible</Term>: they cannot set seed with their own pollen, so every seed is a cross.
- **Panamorous** tomatoes can still pollinate themselves, but have flowers built for crossing. He estimates this could make crossing up to ten times as likely as in heirlooms.

### How they were made

The aim was a domestic tomato with a wild flower: one that bees visit and that cannot fertilise itself. Such a population behaves like a <Term id="hybrid-swarm">hybrid swarm</Term>, with every seed a new mix. The source was two wild, self-incompatible species with big, showy blooms, *Solanum pennellii* and *S. habrochaites*. Pollen had to go from wild to domestic: the domestic tomato is always the mother, because the reverse cross fails. From the offspring, plants with wild-type flowers were kept. A <Term id="back-cross">back-cross</Term> runs the other way too, bringing bigger fruit into the wild species.

### His lessons, if you try it

- **Use many wild pollen donors at the start.** He recommends 7 to 20, rather than the usual single father. Too few donors was an early mistake.
- **Pick one wild species, not both.** Crosses that mixed three species brought fertility problems that lasted.
- **Expect poor early generations**, and keep the strong survivors.
- **Select for flowers first.** Large, bright, open flowers are easy to see, and the trait is inherited. He tried to keep full self-incompatibility by culling plants that set fruit on their own pollen, but with thousands of plants it was too much work.

### Where it stands

When the book was written, the project was seven to nine generations in, and its seed was shared through collaborators. His hope is tomatoes that adapt by themselves to pests and diseases, including <Term id="late-blight">late blight</Term> in humid climates. That is a hope, not yet a result.

<Safety title="His flavour screen is not a safety test">
Lofthouse and his collaborators taste every fruit of these wild crosses before keeping its seed. The wild parents also brought new flavours, some fruity, some sour or odd. In his account the odd ones linger through the crossing and grow rarer each year as he picks seed parents, and orange and yellow fruit won the best reviews from his tasters. He also writes that a nightshade can taste fine and still make him want to vomit: in this family, flavour does not reveal poison. Wild tomato relatives and early crosses with them are not ordinary garden tomatoes. Do not eat their fruit, and never use taste to judge whether a plant is safe.
</Safety>

## Selecting for your garden

Whatever tomatoes you grow, the seed you choose shapes next year’s plants.

{/* general tip, not from the book: the humid-garden advice in this box */}

- **Choose the plant shape you want.** In the project he favours small, compact plants that are quick and productive; other growers report huge ones. Your selection decides.
- **Do not save from plants with <Term id="blossom-end-rot">blossom end rot</Term>.** Lofthouse pulls the whole plant as soon as one fruit shows it. He holds that a tendency to it is inherited, so saving from such plants carries it on.
