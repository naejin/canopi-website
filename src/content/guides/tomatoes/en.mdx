Will your tomatoes cross if you grow several kinds together? Do you need to keep them apart to save seed? The short answer comes first. The second half is for gardeners who want their tomatoes to cross more, so the crop can adapt to their garden.

<QuickAnswer>
Garden tomatoes mostly pollinate themselves. Joseph Lofthouse puts their natural crossing at about 3-5%, depending on the shape of the flower and the bees around. So varieties grown side by side mostly come true, with the odd surprise. Flowers whose stigma pokes out past the anthers cross more. To help your tomatoes adapt, alternate varieties close together, save from plants with open flowers, and look after the bumblebees. Save the seed by fermenting it.
</QuickAnswer>

## Do tomatoes cross?

Rarely. Tomatoes are <Term id="self-pollinating">self-pollinating</Term>: in most flowers, a plant’s own pollen reaches the stigma before a bee can bring any from elsewhere. Lofthouse estimates that garden tomatoes cross at about 3-5%. Across his mostly selfing crops, he finds the rate moves with the weather, the insects about and the variety.

For ordinary seed saving, that means:

- **You can grow several varieties in one bed and save seed from each.** Most of it will come true.
- **Expect the odd surprise.** A cross shows up the next year as a plant unlike the rest of its variety.
- **Beefsteaks cross more than most,** because their flowers are often loosely closed (see below).

<CropCard crop="tomato-garden" />

Tomato seed sits in a jelly coat. You free it by <Term id="seed-fermentation">seed fermentation</Term>: squeeze the seeds and juice into a jar and leave it until the <Term id="gel-coat">gel coat</Term> breaks down, after a few days. Then add water, pour off the pulp that floats, rinse the seed that sinks, and dry it well.

## Read the flower

The flower tells you how likely a plant is to cross. Look closely at an open bloom.

In most garden tomatoes the <Term id="anther">anthers</Term> are joined into a tight cone around the <Term id="stigma">stigma</Term>. Pollen can neither get in nor get out. That cone, more than anything, is why tomatoes self.

A flower built for crossing looks different. Look for:

- an <Term id="exserted-stigma">exserted stigma</Term>, poking out past the tip of the cone;
- a loose cone, with anthers that gape apart or are not joined at all;
- large, bright petals rather than small, dull ones;
- petals that leave the centre open to bees instead of shielding it;
- a puff of pollen when you tap the flower.

Some cherry tomatoes still have a stigma that sticks out. They are a good place to start looking.

## Why tomatoes need more crossing

Each time tomatoes travelled, only a small sample went: from the Andes to Mexico, from Mexico to Europe, and from Europe around the world. Then came long years of heirloom keeping, with each variety kept pure. Every step was a <Term id="genetic-bottleneck">genetic bottleneck</Term>, and the long selfing brings <Term id="inbreeding-depression">inbreeding depression</Term>.

Lofthouse cites a study in which one wild tomato type held more genetic diversity than every domestic line it looked at, taken together. With so little to choose from, domestic tomatoes are slow to adapt to a new pest or a new climate.

## Raise crossing in any garden

You do not need wild tomatoes to get more crosses. Change how you plant and what you save.

- **Alternate varieties along the row.** Plant A, B, A, B rather than a block of each, so every plant has a different neighbour.
- **Plant close.** Lofthouse sets plants in tight clumps, or in a row with plants about 15 cm (6 in) apart.
- **Save from the most open flowers.** Each summer, mark the plants whose stigmas stick out, and save their seed first.
- **Look after the bees.** Tomato flowers make no nectar, so honeybees mostly ignore them. In his garden, bumblebees and other wild bees do the work, ground-nesting digger bees above all. Use no insecticides, grow flowers for bees nearby, and leave some bare, undisturbed ground where they can nest.
- **Give odd plants a good spot.** A plant unlike its row may be a natural cross. Grow its seed next year where it can do well.

## “Promiscuous” and “panamorous” tomatoes

This is Lofthouse’s long-running breeding project, and both words are his.

- **Promiscuous** tomatoes are <Term id="self-incompatible">self-incompatible</Term>: they cannot set seed with their own pollen, so every seed is a cross.
- **Panamorous** tomatoes can still pollinate themselves, but have flowers built for crossing. He estimates this could make crossing up to ten times as likely as in heirlooms.

### How they were made

Two wild species, *Solanum pennellii* and *S. habrochaites*, are self-incompatible and have large, showy flowers that bees love. The project crossed domestic tomatoes, as mothers, with their pollen; the cross fails the other way round. The offspring were then selected for wild-type flowers. Every seed of such a population is a new mix, so it behaves like a <Term id="hybrid-swarm">hybrid swarm</Term>. The project also works in reverse, with a <Term id="back-cross">back-cross</Term> that carries larger fruit into the wild species.

### His lessons, if you try it

- **Use many wild pollen donors at the start.** He recommends 7 to 20, rather than the usual single father. Too few donors was an early mistake.
- **Pick one wild species, not both.** Crosses that mixed three species brought fertility problems that lasted.
- **Expect poor early generations.** The first crosses were often too slow for his season and poorly suited to his soil and climate. The survivors showed <Term id="hybrid-vigour">hybrid vigour</Term>.
- **Select for flowers first.** Large, bright, open flowers are easy to see, and the trait is inherited. He tried to keep full self-incompatibility by culling plants that set fruit on their own pollen, but with thousands of plants it was too much work.

### Where it stands

When the book was written, the project was seven to nine generations in, and its seed was shared through collaborators. His hope is tomatoes that adapt by themselves to pests and diseases, including late blight in humid climates. That is a hope, not yet a result.

Wild tomato relatives and early crosses with them are not ordinary garden tomatoes. Never use taste to judge whether a plant is safe.

## Selecting for your garden

Whatever tomatoes you grow, the seed you choose shapes next year’s plants.

<HisGarden
  his="He grows tomatoes sprawling on the ground, with no stakes and no sprays, in very dry air. He never saves seed from fruit lying in mud, and his vines have come to arch and hold their fruit off the soil."
  you="In a humid garden, sprawling unsprayed plants invite leaf disease, so stake or cage them as usual. You can still select: save from the plants that stay healthy in your conditions."

- **Choose the plant shape you want.** In the project he favours small, compact plants that are quick and productive; other growers report huge ones. Your selection decides.
- **Do not save from plants with blossom end rot.** Lofthouse pulls the whole plant as soon as one fruit shows it, so the trait is not passed on.
