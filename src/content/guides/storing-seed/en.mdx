Your seed is cleaned and dry. Now it has to wait months, maybe years, without going damp, getting eaten or getting lost. This guide starts from where you will keep it, a cupboard, a fridge, a freezer or a shed, and ends with a short checklist.

<QuickAnswer>
Dry the seed until a closed jar of it, set briefly in the sun, shows no droplets inside. Seal it in glass jars with metal lids, which mice can't chew through, and keep them in the coolest dark place you have: a cupboard suits most dry seed, and colder keeps it longer. Freeze each new batch to kill insects; in Lofthouse's experience, dry seed of temperate crops takes it without harm, while tropical seed may not. Keep a second copy somewhere else.
</QuickAnswer>

## Cool, dark, dry and safe

The old seed savers' advice is "cool, dark and dry". Lofthouse adds "safe": from mice, insects and mishaps.

## Is it dry enough to seal?

Seal damp seed and it rots or moulds in the jar, so check first. Lofthouse's home check is the sun test: close a little seed in a jar or a plastic bag, leave it briefly in the sun, then bring it into the shade. Droplets inside mean it is too wet to store. It is a rough check, so when in doubt, dry it longer.

If it needs more drying:

- **Spread it thin** on a tray or a sheet, somewhere airy and out of the sun.
- **Use a dehydrator** on a low setting, as Lofthouse does.
- **Seal it with a <Term id="desiccant">desiccant</Term>,** a dry material that pulls moisture out of the seed. Seal the desiccant and the seed (in paper envelopes) in the same jar. Lofthouse uses dried white rice; rice used this way stays with your seed supplies, never in the kitchen.

Bought seed in paper packets often holds more moisture than suits long keeping, so dry it too before it joins your collection. It can stay in its packet.

<Note>Lofthouse grows temperate crops. Seed of tropical crops may not take kindly to strong drying or to freezing, so try a little first.</Note>

## Where will your seed live?

Find the place you have. The cooler it is, the longer seed lasts: Lofthouse uses a rule of thumb from chemistry, that every 10 °C (18 °F) cooler roughly doubles storage life and every 10 °C warmer roughly halves it. Real seed life still varies a lot from crop to crop.

<Points>

- **A cupboard or shelf indoors.** Good enough for most dry seed. Lofthouse reads the "cool" of the old advice as ordinary room temperature, out of direct sun.
- **A fridge.** Seed lasts longer than in a cupboard. Keep the jars sealed so they take up no damp.
- **A freezer.** Longest of all. In Lofthouse's view, dry seed in a freezer is paused and starts ageing again when it comes out. Freeze only fully dry seed, sealed.
- **A garage, shed or cellar.** Mice and insects find seed here first. Use glass jars only, and check them often.
- **Somewhere warm**, such as a hot loft or a shelf by the stove. Avoid it: heat ages seed fast.

</Points>

## Containers that mice can't chew

Use glass jars with metal lids. Plastic tubs and cardboard boxes don't stop mice: Lofthouse has twice lost nearly all his seed to them, and only the seed in glass survived.

- **One jar, many packets.** A wide-mouth jar holds lots of small packets, so you don't need a jar for each variety.
- **A bag for the field.** Glass breaks when dropped. Take out what you will sow in a bag, and pour any leftover back into the jar at home.
- **A label on everything.** Crop and year, on each jar and each packet.

## Insects in the seed

Freeze two kinds of seed: every batch that comes in, swapped, bought or given, before it joins the rest; and any jar where you find insects. Clean-looking seed can still carry eggs from the harvest, and insects get through paper, plastic and the gap under a loose lid.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Dry first">Only freeze seed that is already dry enough to store. Freezing can kill damp seed.</Step>
  <Step title="Seal">Close it in a waterproof, airtight bag or jar, so no condensation soaks in when it comes out of the cold.</Step>
  <Step title="Freeze">In his experience, two days or so in the freezer does it. His germination tests on temperate seed, before and after, showed no harm.</Step>
  <Step title="Shake">Shake the jar hard before and after freezing: it crushes eggs and insects.</Step>
  <Step title="Back into glass">Store it in a glass jar so nothing gets back in.</Step>
</Steps>

## Backups: one accident shouldn't take it all

In Lofthouse's experience, people lose more seed than pests do: jars get mislaid, a box is left out in the rain, a collection is thrown out by someone who didn't know what it was.

- **Keep a second copy** with friends or family.
- **Give seed away.** He has lost varieties and got them back from people he had shared them with years before.
- **Split copies by risk.** Two copies in the same house, or the same flood zone, are really one copy.

## Check each winter

{/* General tip, not from the book (which mentions germination tests without a method): no counts, times or rates. */}
Seed fades slowly, so check the oldest jars before sowing time. Run a <Term id="germination-test">germination test</Term>: sprout a counted handful on a damp cloth and see how many come up. If few do, sow that seed more thickly this year, or grow it out and save fresh seed from it.
