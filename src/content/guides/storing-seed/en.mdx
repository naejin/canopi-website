Your seed is cleaned and dry. Now it has to wait months, maybe years, without going damp, getting eaten or getting lost. This guide covers how to tell it is dry enough to seal, what to keep it in, and where.

<QuickAnswer>
Keep seed cool, dark, dry and safe. Dry it until a closed jar of it, left in the sun, shows no droplets inside. Then seal it in glass jars with metal lids, which mice can't chew through. The cooler the spot, the longer it keeps: a fridge beats a cupboard and a freezer beats a fridge, as long as the seed went in dry. Keep a second copy somewhere else.
</QuickAnswer>

## Is it dry enough to seal?

Sealing damp seed is the fastest way to lose it: it rots or moulds in the jar. Lofthouse's home check is the sun test. Put some seed in a closed glass jar or plastic bag in the sun. If droplets form on the inside, it's too wet to store.

It is a rough check: when in doubt, dry it longer.

### Ways to dry it

- **Spread it out** on a tray, a sheet or a tarp, in a thin layer, somewhere airy and out of the sun.
- **A dehydrator** set low. Lofthouse uses about 35 °C (95 °F).
- **A <Term id="desiccant">desiccant</Term>:** a dry material sealed in a jar with the seed, pulling moisture out of it. Lofthouse uses white rice that he has dried thoroughly, in a large sealed jar, with the seed added in paper or cloth envelopes for about a week. A friend of his gets similar results with dried lichen. Rice used this way stays with your seed supplies, not in the kitchen.

Bought seed in paper packets often holds more moisture than is ideal for long keeping. Dry it the same way before it joins your collection; you can dry it in its packet.

<Note>Lofthouse grows temperate crops. Seed of tropical crops may not take kindly to strong drying or to freezing, so try a little first.</Note>

## Containers that mice can't chew

Twice, after a move, mice found a box of Lofthouse's seed left in a garage. They chewed through plastic tubs and cardboard and ate almost everything. The only seed left was in a glass jar.

Since then he keeps seed in glass jars with metal lids, from small jam jars up to big ones, and uses lidded buckets for large amounts. A wide-mouth jar holds a lot of small packets, so you don't need a jar per variety.

Glass breaks if you drop it in the field, so take what you plan to sow out in a bag and pour any leftover back into the jar at home. Label every jar and packet with the crop and the year.

## Insects in the seed

Insects chew through paper and plastic and slip through the smallest cracks. Some arrive as eggs inside the harvest itself, so a packet can look clean and still be infested.

Freezing kills them. Lofthouse's routine:

<Steps>
  <Step title="Dry first">Only freeze seed that is already dry enough to store. Freezing can kill damp seed.</Step>
  <Step title="Seal">Put it in a waterproof, airtight bag or jar, so no condensation soaks in when it comes out of the cold.</Step>
  <Step title="Freeze">In his experience, two days or so in a home freezer does it. He has run germination tests on temperate seed before and after and seen no harm.</Step>
  <Step title="Shake">Shake the jar hard before and after freezing. It crushes eggs and insects.</Step>
</Steps>

Then store it in glass so nothing gets back in. Freeze every batch of seed that comes in, swapped, bought or given, before it joins the rest.

## Heat and time

Heat ages seed. Lofthouse uses a rule of thumb from chemistry: every 10 °C (18 °F) cooler roughly doubles how long seed lasts, and every 10 °C warmer roughly halves it.

Those are the numbers of his worked example. Take them as an illustration of the rule, not a promise: real seed life varies a lot from crop to crop.

In his view, dry seed in a freezer is paused: it starts ageing again when it comes out.

## Accidents, disasters and backups

In Lofthouse's experience, people lose more seed than pests do. Collections get thrown out after a death in the family, taken in a separation, forgotten in the back of a truck in the rain, dropped, misplaced, or lost with an unpaid storage unit.

- **Keep backup copies** of your seed with friends or family.
- **Give seed away.** He has lost varieties more than once and got them back from someone he had shared them with years before.

<HisGarden
  his="He has never lost seed to a disaster but plans for one: he keeps collections in three different counties, each exposed to different risks such as flood, wildfire or earthquake, so no single event can destroy them all."
  you="Think about the disaster most likely where you live, and where your copies are kept. Two copies in the same flood zone, or the same house, are really one copy."
/>

If you live where the ground shakes, fix your seed shelves to the wall and give them a lip so jars can't slide off. For extra safety, line each jar with a bag so the seed stays together even if the glass breaks.

## Check each winter

{/* General tip, not from the book (which mentions germination tests without a method): no counts, times or rates. */}
Seed fades slowly, so check the oldest jars before sowing time. Run a <Term id="germination-test">germination test</Term>: sprout a counted handful on a damp cloth and see how many come up. If few do, sow that seed more thickly this year, or grow it out and save fresh seed from it.
