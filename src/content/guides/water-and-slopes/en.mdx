Your soil washes downhill in every storm, or the rain runs off a dry field before it can soak in. This guide covers the small earthworks and living hedges that hold water and soil where they fall, following Eric Toensmeier's *The Carbon Farming Solution* (for how they fit a carbon-storing farm, see [Carbon farming](/guides/carbon-farming/)). Trees with crops on flat ground are in [Trees among field crops](/guides/trees-in-the-field/).

<QuickAnswer>
Rain that runs off takes soil with it. Slow it down and let it soak in: dig small planting pits, build low berms and stone lines across the slope, and plant hedgerows along the contour that you cut each year for mulch. Soil builds up behind them until the slope turns into terraces. Keep the ground covered with mulch or plants. In dry places do not plant more trees than the land once carried, and water carefully so you do not salt the soil.
</QuickAnswer>

## Why rain carries soil away

Bare, ploughed ground erodes, and on unprotected hillsides, the author warns, annual crops yield a little less each year. Perennial cover changes that: he cites erosion and nitrogen losses under perennial crops at less than a twentieth of those under annuals.

Soil itself can hold more water. Ground rich in <Term id="soil-organic-matter">soil organic matter</Term> soaks up more rain, so it copes better with both drought and flood ([Living soil](/guides/living-soil/)).

Start at the top, where the water starts: tree roots hold a slope, and strips of trees and grass along a stream catch what still runs off ([Windbreaks and living fences](/guides/windbreaks-and-living-fences/)).

Instead of tilling a whole dry field, dig small pits and plant a clump of crops in each: a <Term id="zai-pit">zaï pit</Term>. The pit catches rain, and the compost or other organic matter you put in it. The waffle gardens of the US Southwest work the same way: sunken beds with raised edges that hold water.

The book gives no sizes or spacings, so neither does this guide: copy what your neighbours do, or ask a local farm adviser. Mulch in and around the pits ([Mulch](/guides/mulch/)).

## Small earthworks

The book's water-harvesting toolkit, in plain words:

- lines of stones laid along the contour;
- low earth berms with a basin above them, and basins that let water soak in;
- terraces;
- snow traps and buried drains;
- mulch;
- tanks that catch water from roofs.

None of it is new: the author reports water-harvesting works some 6,400 years old in the Thar desert of India. At sites in sub-Saharan Africa, one study he cites found that water harvesting gave two and a half times the crop yield. He sees it mainly as a way to cope with dry years, and its carbon gain as coming from the extra growth.

{/* general tip, not from the book */}On steep or unstable ground, get local advice before you dig.

A <Term id="contour-hedgerow">contour hedgerow</Term> is [alley cropping](/guides/trees-in-the-field/#alley-cropping) laid along the contour: rows of woody shrubs, usually a <Term id="nitrogen-fixer">nitrogen fixer</Term>, or of a deep-rooted grass, cut each year for mulch or fodder (<Term id="coppice">coppice</Term>). Soil washing down stops behind each row, and over the years the ground between rows levels out into steps: living terraces.

The costs, plainly: hard work at the start, a slow payback, and a hedge that competes with the crop. In Ethiopia, farmers set vetiver rows slightly off the contour so that oxen can still plough between them.

For a whole hillside, the Philippine plan known as SALT keeps forest on the top, puts hedgerows and water harvesting on the middle and lower slope, with tree crops in about one band in three, and keeps gardens and houses in the valley. In North Korea, farmers are mending worn hillsides, once ploughed for annual crops, by planting strips of perennial legumes along the contour, with tree crops such as chestnuts and Asian pears.

## Terraces

Terraces are level beds cut into a slope. Some built a thousand years ago still work, the author notes, but they take great labour to build; local builders know the stone and soil. Where hedgerows run short of water, as in China's cold, dry north and west, terraces are built and their edges planted with useful perennials instead. The Mexican highlands have their own version, the metepantli, edged with plants (see the subtropical block below).

## Keyline

<Term id="keyline">Keyline</Term> is a whole-farm plan from 1950s Australia, by P. A. Yeomans. It places ponds high on the land, rips the soil and plants rows slightly off the contour to spread water from the gullies toward the drier ridges, and adds managed grazing.

It is used mostly on farms of hundreds of hectares. {/* general tip, not from the book */}On a small plot, take the idea, moving water from wet ground to dry, rather than the machinery. Research on it is scarce, in the author's account; farmers who use it report more soil organic matter.

## Water without salting the land

Badly run irrigation in dry places leaves salt behind, and the author cites estimates that a large share of the world's dry farmland has been salted this way. Well-managed drip irrigation helps.

Plants that grow in salt (<Term id="halophyte">halophytes</Term>) can make salted land productive again; perennial ones can even lower the salt in the soil, in the studies he cites. Look for native salt plants first: Richard Felger, a researcher, looked worldwide for crops for salty soil and found his best candidate, nipa, in his own region.

## Limits in dry places

- **Trees drink.** Plant no more trees than the land carried before it was farmed, and lean on thrifty perennials such as cacti, succulents and warm-season grasses.
- **Hedgerows between crops compete too hard for water** in semi-arid and arid places ([Alley cropping](/guides/trees-in-the-field/#alley-cropping)).
- **Orchards compete for water** with towns and other farms in dry years, the author warns.

## Plants for your climate

Pick from the blocks below, natives first. Check your local list of invasive plants before planting anything from elsewhere.

<Safety title="Spines and thorns">
Agave, prickly pear and mesquite have sharp spines or thorns: plant them away from paths and play areas, and wear gloves and long sleeves to work them.
</Safety>

Olives are not food as picked, and the author does not count the seeds of Siberian pea shrub as food at all.

<FoodCaution risk="needs-processing" />

<ForClimate zones="tropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
For steep fields in the humid and dry tropics.

- **Vetiver**: a deep-rooted grass for contour rows, from fairly dry to very wet places, and it survives long flooding. The author reports that the sterile-seeded kinds used for this do not spread; check locally all the same.
- **Pigeon pea**: a short-lived nitrogen-fixing shrub for hedgerows, cut back to renew it.
- **Bananas**: one of the tree crops in the perennial bands of the SALT plan.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="subtropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
For the tropical highlands, where the book's terrace examples come from.

- **Maguey** (*Agave americana*) <Invasive />: an edge plant of the Mexican metepantli terraces; spiny (see above).
- **Prickly pear** (nopal) <Invasive />: a cactus also grown on those terrace edges.
- **Vetiver** (tropical block) suits the highlands as well.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="mediterranean" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Winter rain and a long dry summer: most rain must soak in where it falls.

- **Carob**: often planted on the driest, stoniest slopes, but it needs a fair share of winter rain to set pods.
- **Olive**: a long-lived slope tree that tolerates some salt. The fruit is not eaten from the tree (caution above).
- **Pistachio**: a nut tree for long, dry summers, also fairly tolerant of salt.

In dry years orchards compete hard for water: plant to your rainfall.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="arid" source="carbon-farming-solution">
For deserts and salty ground, where earthworks matter most.

- **Date palm**: the canopy of oasis gardens, and among the tree crops the book lists as salt-tolerant.
- **Mesquite** <Invasive />: a thorny pod tree that grows where little else does, some kinds even on salty ground; the author himself warns that it spreads aggressively in many dry regions. Fed to livestock as their only feed for long periods, its pods can make animals ill or even kill them: see [Feeding animals from trees](/guides/trees-for-livestock/#poisonous-and-invasive-fodder-plants).
- **Saltbush** (*Atriplex*): a salt-tolerant shrub, grown with mesquite for fodder.
- **Nipa** (*Distichlis palmeri*): a wild grain grass of the salt marshes in the Sonoran desert's river deltas; not yet a crop, but the kind of native salt plant to look for.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="temperate" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "warm temperate": tree crops grown along the contour, with field crops between.

- **Chestnut**: a staple nut tree that holds a slope when planted along the contour; its kinds range from cold winters to the subtropics.
- **Persian walnut**: at home in the warm temperate and Mediterranean zones, and also grown on the contour among annual crops.

</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="continental" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "cold temperate": long frosts, warm summers. The examples come from North Korea's worn hillsides and China's cold, dry north.

- **Chestnut**: the author's first choice of staple tree for cold, humid climates, grown in contour strips with perennial legumes.
- **Asian pear**: the fruit tree planted beside the chestnuts in those strips.
- **Siberian pea shrub**: a very hardy nitrogen-fixing shrub the author lists for erosion control and windbreaks; grown for the soil, not for food (caution above).

Where hedgerows run short of water, build terraces and plant their edges with useful perennials, as in China's dry north.
</ForClimate>
