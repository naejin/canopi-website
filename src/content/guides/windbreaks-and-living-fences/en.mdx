Plant your boundary instead of fencing it, and one strip can shelter crops, keep animals where you want them, and give fodder, poles or fruit. In Eric Toensmeier's ranking (see [Carbon farming](/guides/carbon-farming/)), trees among crops store more carbon than better annual cropping, though less than layered perennial gardens. Plant across the wind, cut the hedge back so it stays thick, and choose plants that give something besides shelter. Start with your region's own species, and read the warnings on thorny and poisonous hedges before you plant near animals or children.

## What a planted edge does

<Points>
- **Across the wind** A <Term id="windbreak">windbreak</Term> is a long strip of trees, shrubs or other perennials set against the prevailing wind.
- **Around the field** A <Term id="living-fence">living fence</Term>, or hedge, is a managed row of shrubs and trees that marks a boundary and steers animals.
- **Along the water** A <Term id="riparian-buffer">riparian buffer</Term> is a perennial strip between cropland and a stream.
</Points>

All three slow the wind, and the dust and soil it strips from bare ground. With the ditch or bank that often runs beside them, hedges hold rainwater long enough to soak in ([Keep rain and soil on your land](/guides/water-and-slopes/)). They give wildlife cover and a route between habitats, and they house the insects and other predators that keep pests in check ([Pests without sprays](/guides/pests-without-sprays/)).

## Windbreaks: plant across the wind

Set the strip at right angles to the wind that does your land the most harm. The crop row right beside it yields less, because the trees take light, water and nutrients there. Across the sheltered field, the author reports, yields generally rise overall.

Shelter pays most in dry climates and drought years, and most of all, he finds, for vegetables, tree crops and vineyards. In snowy places the strip catches drifting snow, and the meltwater can repay what the trees drink. He cites a review of 78 studies from the former Soviet Union in which wheat behind windbreaks yielded about a quarter more: one region's result, not a promise.

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Use the strip the trees compete for as a path or lane, or for a crop you care less about.

## Living fences and hedges

Europe, Africa, Asia and Latin America all have old hedging traditions. Before barbed wire, Osage orange <Invasive /> hedges fenced farms across much of the United States. Many hedges were torn out once farming was mechanised; where they survive, small hedged fields make the countryside the French call <Term id="bocage">bocage</Term>.

Build one in either of two ways:

- **A cut hedge.** Plant a row and keep it thick at the base by <Term id="coppice">coppicing</Term> or hard pruning, sometimes weaving the stems. [Trees with edible leaves](/guides/trees-with-edible-leaves/#keep-them-low) shows the cuts; [Coppice, willow and bamboo](/guides/coppice-and-bamboo/) shows how to plan them.
- **Living posts.** Push <Term id="live-stake">live stakes</Term> in as fence posts: they root, leaf out and carry the wire.

A hedge lets you choose when animals reach a field, and keeps deer off your crops. It also yields craft wood, semi-wild foods and medicines.

## Hedges that also produce

A hedge takes ground, so choose plants that pay twice: fodder ([Feeding animals from trees](/guides/trees-for-livestock/)), nuts ([Nuts as a staple](/guides/nut-staples/)), poles and firewood ([Coppice, willow and bamboo](/guides/coppice-and-bamboo/)), or fibre and tannin ([Fibre, dye and soap](/guides/fibre-dye-and-soap/)). A <Term id="nitrogen-fixer">nitrogen fixer</Term> in the row also feeds the soil. Some trees chemically hold back what grows near them, called <Term id="allelopathy">allelopathy</Term>: check before planting one beside a crop.

Look close to home first: every region has its own windbreak and nitrogen-fixing plants, the author points out. Check each one against your local list of invasive plants.

## Thorny and poisonous

A hedge of plants that are spiny, poisonous, dense or not worth eating stops animals: a <Term id="green-corral">green corral</Term>. It costs little, and matters most while young trees inside it establish. The flip side, the book warns, is that a badly planned system poisons the animals it holds. Know every plant your animals can reach.

<Safety title="Poisonous hedge plants">
Jatropha works as a corral because its leaves are toxic to livestock. Castor <Invasive />, often grown along field borders, has deadly poisonous seeds, and petroleum plant <Invasive /> burns skin and eyes: read [Oils and resins that burn the skin](/guides/fibre-dye-and-soap/#oils-and-resins-that-burn-the-skin) before planting either. Keep all three away from wells and play areas, and out of animals' reach unless keeping them off is the point.
</Safety>

<Safety title="Thorns">
Osage orange <Invasive /> has vicious thorns, and cattle have choked to death on its sticky fruit. Agaves, mesquite <Invasive />, wild honey locust <Invasive />, teri-pod and peach palm trunks are spiny too. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Wear gloves and eye protection to cut them, and keep the trimmings off paths and out of pasture.
</Safety>

## Strips along water

A buffer of perennials between a field and a stream catches nutrients washing off the field and eases flooding downstream. It can still produce food crops, hay, timber or biomass. On farmed floodplains, strips laid across the flow, called <Term id="water-break">water-breaks</Term>, slow floodwater. Willows bind streambanks and help clean dirty water ([Keep rain and soil on your land](/guides/water-and-slopes/)).

## For your climate

The principles hold everywhere; the plants do not. A few from the book:

<ForClimate zones="tropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
- **Madre de cacao** (*Gliricidia sepium*): stakes that root into living fence posts, as in Cuba.
- **Moringa** <Invasive />: a living fence that is also a [leaf tree](/guides/trees-with-edible-leaves/).
- **Tahitian chestnut**: a wet-lowland nut tree for living fences and coastal shelter.
- **Pongam** <Invasive />: a windbreak and fodder tree whose floating pods spread it along water.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="subtropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Mild winters with light frost.

- **Teri-pod**: a spiny hedge with tannin-rich pods.
- **Eucalypts** <Invasive />: fast windbreaks; the author notes their thirst and allelopathy become serious only where rain is under 400 mm (16 in) a year.

**For the tropical highlands:** chachafruto, a nitrogen-fixing tree grown from live stakes as a living fence and fodder bank ([Perennial beans and edible tree pods](/guides/perennial-beans-and-pods/)).
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="mediterranean" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Winter rain and a dry summer.

- **Carob**: a windbreak whose pods feed animals.
- **Holm oak** (encina): an acorn oak also planted as a windbreak.
- **Eucalypts** <Invasive />: only where rain tops 400 mm (16 in) a year.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="arid" source="carbon-farming-solution">
For dry places: the book's arid and drier semi-arid lands.

- **Wattleseed acacias**: dryland nitrogen fixers that make good windbreaks, planted as hedges by farmers in Niger.
- **Mesquite** <Invasive />: a spiny living fence and windbreak, only where it is native.
- **Agaves**: spiny living fences once established; *Agave americana* <Invasive /> has gone wild across the world's drylands.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="temperate" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "warm temperate", which includes cool-summer places such as southern Britain.

- **Willows**: windbreaks and snow fences, with leaves for fodder.
- **Poplars**: windbreaks; in Italy, pruned into living trellises for vines.
- **Honey locust** <Invasive />: wild spiny forms made living fences; for pods, choose grafted sweet-podded varieties.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="continental" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "cold temperate", where the snow a windbreak holds is reason enough to plant.

- **Siberian pea shrub**: hundreds of kilometres of windbreaks on the Canadian prairies.
- **Poplars**: the main tree of a vast shelterbelt across northern China.
- **Osage orange** <Invasive />: hardy here, but heed the warnings above.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="boreal" source="carbon-farming-solution">
- **Willows**: in the author's view, a good choice for the far north.
- **Siberian pea shrub**: hardy into the boreal zone.
- **Sea buckthorn** <Invasive />: a spiny, suckering windbreak shrub for cold, dry or salty ground.

A dark evergreen canopy over snow can warm more than it stores: see [Honest limits](/guides/carbon-farming/#honest-limits).
</ForClimate>
