Do your carrots and your apple tree want the same soil? Not according to Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis. This guide sorts your plants into their two camps and shows how to feed each bed so it leans the right way.

<QuickAnswer>
In the authors' model, plants that live one season (vegetables, annual flowers) want a soil where bacteria outweigh fungi, plants that live for years (shrubs, perennials, trees) want fungi to dominate, and lawn grass sits in between. You steer a bed with what you feed it: fresh, green, fine material worked in favours bacteria; aged, brown, coarse material left on top favours fungi. Keep compost tea off anything you will eat.
</QuickAnswer>

## Two kinds of soil

Ground left alone moves from bare soil and weeds to grassland, then shrubs and forest: this is <Term id="succession">succession</Term>. At each step, the authors say, fungi make up more of the soil's living weight, while the bacteria stay about as numerous as before.

Digging pushes the other way. Tilled soil that is often bare leans bacterial; old ground that nobody disturbs leans fungal. The authors describe the balance with the <Term id="fungal-bacterial-ratio">F:B ratio</Term>: the weight of living fungi against that of living bacteria.

## Why plants care: two forms of nitrogen

When protozoa and nematodes eat microbes, the nitrogen they release comes out as ammonium. In the authors' account, bacterial slime keeps the root zone slightly alkaline, which suits the bacteria that turn ammonium into nitrate; where fungi dominate, the acids they make keep the soil acid, and more nitrogen stays as ammonium.

From this come their second and third rules. Vegetables, annuals and grasses mostly do best on nitrate, in a bacterial soil; trees, shrubs and perennials mostly do best on ammonium, in a fungal soil. A plant can live on the other form, but most grow better on one.

The quick way to tell is how long the plant stays in the ground. One season: bacterial. A year or more: usually fungal. The authors see three reasons for this:

- Short-lived plants leave soft litter that bacteria eat; woody plants leave tough litter that suits fungi.
- Many soil fungi are root partners (<Term id="mycorrhiza">mycorrhizal</Term> fungi) that need a living root, and a root that lives for years carries more of them.
- Fungi grow slowly and break easily.

Soluble fertilisers supply nitrogen mostly as nitrate, which in the authors' view does not suit the fungal-soil plants in the long run.

## Which way does your bed lean now?

Start with its history. A bed dug or tilled every year and fed from a bag leans bacterial and is short of fungi. The authors also read many earthworms and worm casts as a sign of bacterial soil. White fungal threads, the <Term id="hyphae">hyphae</Term>, in leaf litter or under mulch show fungi at work.

A fuller census is in [What's in your soil?](/guides/reading-your-soil/)

## Four levers to steer a bed

You steer a bed by what you give it and how you give it. The authors' rules come down to four levers.

<Points>
- **What you add.** Fresh, green material (grass clippings, pulled weeds, kitchen scraps) feeds bacteria; aged, brown material (fallen leaves, twigs, wood chips, bark) feeds fungi. The authors count clippings and straw as green even once dry: they keep their sugars.
- **Where you put it.** Left on the surface, it favours fungi, which grow up into it; worked in, it favours bacteria. The same leaves can do either.
- **How fine and how wet.** Ground fine and moist, it is taken over fast by bacteria, some of which hold fungi back; coarse and fairly dry, it lets fungi lead. The authors call a mulch dry below about 35% moisture.
- **Which compost and which tea.** A compost carries its community into the soil below, so it moves a bed its way; a tea takes after the compost it is brewed from.
</Points>

If a wet green mulch smells sour, give it more air and less water. In the authors' own yards, autumn leaves grow fungi faster than wood chips do. Nothing goes to an extreme: a bacterial bed still needs some fungi to hold its crumbs.

Digging is not a lever. The authors stop tilling established beds; their one exception is turning old fungal ground, such as former woodland, into a vegetable bed ([Vegetable beds without digging](/guides/no-dig-beds/)).

## A tree in a lawn, a shrub in the vegetable plot

A lawn is bacterial ground. Where a long-lived plant stands in one, the authors give it a fungal island: fungal compost and brown mulch out to the <Term id="drip-line">drip line</Term>, kept off the trunk, and no grass under the tree.

Do the same for a currant bush in a vegetable plot. The authors' warning cases are a lilac that never flowers and a spruce dying in a well-fed lawn. Alder, aspen, beech, birch and cottonwood are the exception: they cope with a bacterial soil while young.

Soil under lawn trees and beside paths is often compacted; the authors find that aerating it helps only briefly unless compost, mulch and tea bring life back.
