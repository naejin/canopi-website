Fruit is the easy part of a perennial garden; this guide covers the foods that fill a plate every day, from Eric Toensmeier's global inventory in *The Carbon Farming Solution*. For why perennials store carbon, see [Carbon farming](/guides/carbon-farming/).

<QuickAnswer>
Yes: trees, shrubs, palms and vines already give starch, protein, oil and sugar, not only fruit. Toensmeier sorts these <Term id="staple-crop">staple crops</Term> into six kinds; mixing kinds covers more of a diet. Plant what farmers already grow in your climate, natives first, and grow annuals between them through the years before the first big harvest. Some staples, such as most acorns and olives, are not food as picked: read [the warnings](#staples-that-need-processing) before you choose.
</QuickAnswer>

## What counts as a staple

A staple is a food eaten routinely that makes up much of a region's diet and supplies starch or sugar, protein or fat. Perennial ones come from long-lived plants: nuts, starchy fruits, dry beans and pods, oilseeds, sweet saps, starchy trunks, tubers on vines and, one day, perennial grains.

Every crop in the book survives its harvest and, once established, needs no tilling: see [the five harvest forms](/guides/carbon-farming/#five-ways-to-harvest-a-perennial-without-killing-it). For leaf crops, see [Trees with edible leaves](/guides/trees-with-edible-leaves/).

Many peoples once lived largely on perennials: chestnuts and acorns in eastern North America, olives and dates around the Mediterranean, bananas, sago and breadfruit in the Pacific.

He counts sugar, uneasily, because sugar crops take so much farmland. Most protein crops are legumes, many of them <Term id="nitrogen-fixer">nitrogen fixers</Term>.

Mixing kinds may balance a diet's proteins as rice and beans do, he guesses, though he has not studied it. The humid tropics have the most choice; colder, drier places have fewer, most yielding less than annuals there, in his view.

## Grown now, or still being bred

A handful are global crops: bananas, olives, coconuts, chestnuts, hazelnuts, avocados. More than half of those in the book are regional crops, grown mostly where first farmed; he counts that as good news, since spreading a proven crop is easier than taming a new one.

Perennial grains are not ready: perennial rice is nearest, most others are years or decades away, and grassy perennials store far less carbon than woody ones. Do not wait for them.

Planting stock is hard to find, improved varieties harder still. Start with your region's useful native plants, the book's own first step, and check your local list of invasive plants before planting anything from elsewhere.

## Years before the first harvest

Annuals feed you the year you sow them. Toensmeier notes that many perennial staples take three to five years or longer to bear, and some twenty years or longer.

Bridge the gap three ways:

- **Grow annuals between young trees** until their shade closes in.
- **Add quick perennials.** Bananas fruit within a year or two, and edible wattleseed acacias often in their first year, in his account.
- **Choose early bearers.** Grafted trees are <Term id="clone">clones</Term> of a chosen tree and repeat its habits. For a household, he favours a long picking season, as with avocado, over one heavy harvest.

Plant long-lived staples where you expect to stay, not on rented land. Expect work too: on machine-run farms, he found tree staples took far more hours per tonne than grain.

Draw the annual beds between your young trees in your Canopi design.

## Let animals eat them first

People change their diet slowly; pigs and cattle are less fussy. So the author sees livestock feed as the first place perennial staples can replace grain. Three examples: breadfruit fed to pigs and poultry in the tropics, acorns fattening pigs in Spain's oak pastures, and honey locust <Invasive /> pods dropped into pasture, their seeds well digested by cattle and goats, in his account.

Trees that drop their crop when ripe suit grazing best. If you also gather nuts from the ground for people, mind the manure: the author reports nut harvests spoiled by it, a food-safety problem. More in [Feeding animals from trees](/guides/trees-for-livestock/).

## Staples that need processing

Many staples are not food as picked; processing them is often hand work, mostly by women, he notes. Know which:

- **Acorns**: most are bitter with tannins.
- **Olives**: not edible from the tree.
- **Akee** <Invasive />: the unripe fruit and the seeds can kill, children above all.
- **Cashews**: the oil in the shell burns skin.
- **Breadfruit and jakfruit seeds**: like all nuts of the mulberry family, not eaten as picked.

<FoodCaution risk={["needs-processing", "akee", "cashew"]} />

More nuts and beans that need care are in [Nuts as a staple](/guides/nut-staples/) and [Perennial beans and pods](/guides/perennial-beans-and-pods/); nut allergies apply here as anywhere, the author adds. Chaya and cassava leaves are poisonous raw: see [which leaves are dangerous](/guides/trees-with-edible-leaves/#which-ones-are-dangerous).

<Safety title="Spiny trees">
Wild honey locusts <Invasive /> and the best food mesquites <Invasive /> carry long, hard spines, and chestnut burrs prick: wear gloves, and keep spiny trees away from paths and play areas.
</Safety>

## What to plant in your climate

The kinds above hold everywhere; the species do not. The book sorts climates by coldest winter and rain, and calls its categories a rough start. Each block picks a few staples of different kinds; natives still come first.

<ForClimate zones="tropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
For the humid lowlands, unless noted.

- **Coconut** (oil): takes salt and wind on coasts, and other crops are often grown under the palms.
- **Winged bean** (protein and oil): a climbing perennial bean with nearly every part eaten.
- **Shea** (oil), in the drier tropics: the daily fat of African savannas, so slow to bear that farmers spare the trees when they clear land.

<FoodCaution risk="dry-beans" />
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="subtropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Mild winters with light frost; the book's tropical highlands are here too.

- **Macadamia** (oil): frost damages it; the rough-shelled kind takes slightly cooler weather.
- **Pecan** (oil): a heavy crop one year, a light one the next.
- **Perennial pigeon pea** (protein): a shrub legume cut back each year to keep it young; seed is scarce outside India. See [Perennial beans and pods](/guides/perennial-beans-and-pods/).

<FoodCaution risk="dry-beans" />
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="mediterranean" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Winter rain and a dry summer.

- **Holm oak** (starch plus): in Spain's oak pastures farmers graft sweet selections, far less bitter than other acorns, in the author's account. See [Nuts as a staple](/guides/nut-staples/).
- **Almond** (protein and oil): suited to dry climates, and it needs some winter chill to bear.

<FoodCaution risk="needs-processing" />
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="arid" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Under 250 mm (10 in) of rain a year, by the book's measure; it calls these dryland crops.

- **Date palm** (sugar): the top layer of desert oases; it ripens only after a long, hot, dry spell.
- **Mesquite** <Invasive /> (starch plus): sweet pods from very dry and salty ground, and the best food kinds are the thorniest. Its thorns injure, and livestock fed only on its pods can fall ill: see [Feeding animals from trees](/guides/trees-for-livestock/).
- **Piñon pines** (protein and oil), in cold deserts: mostly gathered wild, with big crops only some years.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="temperate" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book calls this "warm temperate", a band that includes cool-summer places such as southern Britain.

- **Korean pine** (protein and oil): a staple of eastern Asia for humid places, grown on hillsides in Korea; a good crop comes every other year. See [Nuts as a staple](/guides/nut-staples/).
- **Evodia** (oil): an oil tree of Korea and China, hardy in cold winters and busy with bees; its seeds are not eaten.
- **Runner bean** (protein): a climbing bean that regrows from its roots where winters are mild.

<FoodCaution risk="dry-beans" />
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="continental" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "cold temperate": long frosts and warm summers.

- **Heartnut** (protein and oil): the walnut the book rates best for cold climates, with nuts that leave the shell easily.
- **Yellowhorn** (protein and oil): a small tree for cold, dry places that bears within a few years but lives only a few decades, in the author's account.
- **Sea buckthorn** <Invasive /> (oil): a spiny, suckering nitrogen fixer for poor, dry or salty ground.

<HisGarden book="carbon-farming-solution" his="Sea kale and other perennial cabbage relatives set plenty of seed in his garden; he names them as candidates for breeding a perennial oilseed crop." you="Treat untried crops as trials: grow a few beside proven staples and watch how they do." />
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="boreal" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "boreal", with some cold mountains further south. Prefer trees that drop their leaves: here, the author expects dense evergreens to warm more than they store.

- **Siberian stone pine** (protein and oil): a wild staple of northern Asia, perhaps the toughest of all for the author; plant this evergreen in a mix, not a block.
- **Siberian and Manchurian hazels** (protein and oil): shrubs hardy in boreal winters. See [Nuts as a staple](/guides/nut-staples/).
- **Siberian pea shrub** (protein, not yet food): a nitrogen-fixing windbreak whose seeds were famine food; the author asks amateur breeders to find truly edible plants.
