Would you like peas or greens weeks before the usual season? Could a crop sown in autumn feed you in spring? Can beans learn to take a late frost? This guide is about <Term id="season-shifting">season shifting</Term>: growing a crop in a season it does not normally grow in, and letting the weather decide which plants leave seed. You need a spare bed, some seed you can afford to lose, and a few years.

## Three moves

<Steps>
  <Step title="Sow out of season">Sow part of your seed in autumn instead of spring, or weeks before your usual date.</Step>
  <Step title="Let the weather choose">Give the plants no protection. Cold, frost or wet does the <Term id="culling">culling</Term>.</Step>
  <Step title="Save from the survivors">Keep seed only from plants that lived and cropped, and sow it the same way next year.</Step>
</Steps>

Each round, the line fits its new season a little better. Do not be put off by heavy losses: Joseph Lofthouse started his frost-hardy beans from about one seedling in twenty.

## What to sow in autumn

Cold-tolerant leafy crops are the easiest first trials. Spinach, kale and its cabbage-family cousins, and peas all appear on Lofthouse's list of crops that may come through winter and give food early in spring. Lettuce, turnip and grains are on it too.

<CropCards crops={["spinach", "cabbage-kale-broccoli", "pea"]} />

**When.** In his garden, the moment is just before the autumn rains. He sows, then leaves winter to clear out the tender plants. Expect big differences: between species, and between varieties of the same crop. The ones that come through are your seed parents.

**Roots.** Crops that live into a second year can be selected the same way. Lofthouse has lines of carrot, parsnip and sunroot that spend his winters in the open with no cover, and he suspects beet could learn autumn sowing too.

**A head start on spring.** One of his neighbours sows spinach at the end of summer. The small plants sit out the winter and are ready to pick in spring, before most gardens are even sown. More on that lean time in [Food all year](/guides/food-all-year/).

### Favas and grains

Where winters are mild, Lofthouse recommends sowing favas in autumn; he puts the limit at around USDA zone 8. His own valley is colder, and there he is still experimenting: see [Favas: sow early](/guides/beans-and-legumes/#favas-sow-early).

Among grains, rye and many wheats get through his winters; oats and barley do not do so reliably, and he grows barley as a spring crop. He also notes that some grains need a cold spell before they will flower, called <Term id="vernalisation">vernalisation</Term>, and that a very early spring sowing can give it to them.

## Keep two lines, not one

Lofthouse warns that selecting for winter hardiness may bring along traits you would not want in a summer crop. His answer is to split the crop: one line sown in autumn, one sown in spring, each saved from its own plants. These are <Term id="sister-lines">sister lines</Term>.

In the bed, the two lines can look alike. Lofthouse finds them hard to tell apart and says a planting map would help him. At the very least, write the line and the year on every jar.

## Earlier in spring

Lofthouse's frost-tolerant beans began when a late spring frost spared only about 5% of his seedlings; sowing the survivors' seed a month early, year after year, has since moved his bean sowing three to four weeks sooner.

What does that buy? In his garden, the early beans are in before the autumn rains that can spoil the main crop. And with an early crop as well as the main one, picking is spread out and less of a rush.

To try it:

- Keep your main sowing at its usual date, so the whole crop is never at risk.
- Sow a handful of seed a few weeks early, in a patch of its own.
- Save those survivors on their own, and sow their seed early again next year.

**Free trials.** Self-sown plants that come up in odd seasons cost you nothing, so let them grow. He keeps an eye on the favas that sow themselves in his garden each autumn, though nearly all die before spring, in case one day a plant gets through. For him, that is landrace breeding in a nutshell: ask a crop for more than it is used to, then watch for the plant that copes.

**Your own spots.** Lofthouse suggests the same approach could breed crops for a greenhouse, a cold frame, or the shelter of a wall, fence or boulder. Select in the spot you will really use.

## Dodging pests and weather

The less time a crop spends in the ground, the fewer things can go wrong, in Lofthouse's view. He selects for quicker maturity. For warmer climates, he suggests sowing so that a crop grows when its main pests and diseases are not about: a short-season squash, for example, can go in earlier or later than a long-season one.

In his garden, cabbage moths turn up when the summer storms arrive. Kale and Brussels sprouts that came through winter are picked before then, and so are some cabbage-family crops sown in spring. See [Pests and diseases](/guides/pests-without-sprays/).

Gardeners in hot places tell him that quick-maturing varieties let them fit two crops into one year. That is their report, not his own result.
