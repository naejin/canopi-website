Would you like peas or greens weeks before the usual season? Could a crop sown in autumn feed you in spring? Can beans learn to take a late frost? This guide is about <Term id="season-shifting">season shifting</Term>: growing a crop in a season it does not normally grow in, and letting the weather decide which plants leave seed. You need a spare bed, some seed you can afford to lose, and a few years.

## Three moves

<Steps>
  <Step title="Sow out of season">Sow part of your seed in autumn instead of spring, or weeks before your usual date.</Step>
  <Step title="Let the weather choose">Give the plants no protection. Cold, frost or wet does the <Term id="culling">culling</Term>.</Step>
  <Step title="Save from the survivors">Keep seed only from plants that lived and cropped, and sow it the same way next year.</Step>
</Steps>

Each round, the line fits its new season a little better. Do not be put off by heavy losses: a handful of survivors is enough to start a line.

Which shift to try depends on what you are short of:

| You want | What to try | More |
|---|---|---|
| Food early in spring, in the lean weeks | Sow in autumn and let winter remove the tender plants | [What to sow in autumn](#what-to-sow-in-autumn) |
| A summer crop a few weeks sooner | Sow a small batch a little early each year | [Earlier in spring](#earlier-in-spring) |
| A harvest that misses a pest or a wet spell | Move the sowing, or favour quicker plants | [Dodging pests and weather](#dodging-pests-and-weather) |
| A crop for a greenhouse, cold frame or the shelter of a wall | Select in that spot; Lofthouse suggests it could work there too | |

## Keep two lines, not one

Will a crop chosen by winter still do well in summer? Not always, Joseph Lofthouse warns: hardiness can come with traits a summer crop is better without. So he keeps the crop as two lines, one sown in autumn and one in spring, each saved from its own plants. These are <Term id="sister-lines">sister lines</Term>.

In the bed, the two lines can look alike. Lofthouse finds them hard to tell apart and says a planting map would help him. At the very least, write the line and the year on every jar.

## Earlier in spring

Want a summer crop a few weeks sooner? Move the sowing forward a little at a time, without betting the whole crop:

- Keep your main sowing at its usual date.
- Sow a handful of seed a few weeks early, in a patch of its own.
- Save those survivors on their own, and sow their seed early again next year.

Lofthouse's frost-tolerant beans began when a late spring frost spared only about 5% of his seedlings; sowing the survivors' seed a month early, year after year, has since moved his bean sowing three to four weeks sooner. More on these beans in [Beans that shrug off frost](/guides/beans-and-legumes/#beans-that-shrug-off-frost).

For him, the gain is a bean crop that is in before the autumn rains can spoil it, and picking spread over two harvests instead of one rush.

**Free trials.** Plants that sow themselves and come up in an odd season cost you nothing. Let them grow, and save from any that cope.

## What to sow in autumn

Which crops are worth a first try? Peas, spinach, and kale with its cabbage-family cousins are good bets for food early in spring.

<CropCards crops={["spinach", "cabbage-kale-broccoli", "pea"]} />

Sow several species, and several varieties of each, side by side: they differ a lot in how well they come through, and whatever is alive in spring gives you your seed parents. Lofthouse sows just ahead of his autumn rains, so the rain does the watering.

How other crops have fared in his cold valley:

| Crop | In Lofthouse's experience |
|---|---|
| Rye, many wheats | Come through his winters |
| Oats, barley | Not reliably; he sows barley in spring |
| Carrot, parsnip, sunroot | He has lines that winter in the open with no cover |
| Favas | Still an experiment for him; he recommends autumn sowing where winters are mild, in USDA zone 8 and milder |

Some grains will not flower until they have had a cold spell, called <Term id="vernalisation">vernalisation</Term>; he notes that a very early spring sowing can give it to them. For favas, see [Favas: sow early](/guides/beans-and-legumes/#favas-sow-early).

A late-summer sowing can be a head start too: one of his neighbours picks spinach in spring from seed sown at the end of summer. More on the lean weeks in [Food all year](/guides/food-all-year/).

## Dodging pests and weather

Can you pick a crop before its pest shows up? Often, by moving the sowing. In Lofthouse's garden, kale and Brussels sprouts that came through winter are eaten before the cabbage moths come with the summer rains. More on those caterpillars in [Pests and diseases](/guides/pests-without-sprays/#living-with-insects).

The less time a crop spends in the ground, the fewer chances it has to be hit, so Lofthouse also selects for quick ripening. In warm climates, he suggests fitting a quick crop, such as a short-season squash, into the part of the year when its main pests and diseases are quiet.

Gardeners in hot places tell him that quick-maturing varieties let them fit two crops into one year. That is their report, not his own result.
