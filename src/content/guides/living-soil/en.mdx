Garden soil is full of life, and that life feeds your plants. This guide shows who lives underground, how plants trade with them, what drives them away, and the three habits that bring them back.

<QuickAnswer>
Your plants pay soil microbes with sugars from their roots. Bacteria and fungi grow on that food; tiny grazers then eat them and release the surplus nutrients right beside the roots. Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis call the whole network the <Term id="soil-food-web">soil food web</Term>, and they argue that a gardener who feeds it can stop feeding the plants. In their method you feed it with compost, mulch and, if you choose, compost tea, and you stop what breaks it, above all digging and soluble fertilisers.
</QuickAnswer>

## Who lives in a spoonful of soil

Most of this life sits in the top 10 cm (4 in), among roots and fallen leaves. Here is what the authors count in one teaspoon of good garden soil:

Then come the animals you can see: mites, <Term id="springtail">springtails</Term>, beetles, centipedes and earthworms. In good soil, the authors report up to 50 earthworms in 0.09 m² (1 sq ft). The book also describes archaea, algae and slime moulds, which change little in what a gardener does.

## How plants feed the soil, and the soil feeds them back

A plant does not just wait for food to reach its roots. It spends a large part of what its leaves make on <Term id="root-exudates">root exudates</Term>: sugars and proteins that seep from the roots and wake up the bacteria and fungi it needs. These microbes also feed on the cells that growing root tips shed, and they crowd into the <Term id="rhizosphere">rhizosphere</Term>, the thin layer of soil around each root. The authors add that a plant steers which microbes it attracts, and changes the mix as its needs shift through the season.

The grazers are the key. A protozoan or a nematode needs less nitrogen than the microbes it eats contain, so it gets rid of the extra as waste, right where the root can use it. That release is <Term id="mineralisation">mineralisation</Term>, and the authors put up to 80% of a plant's nitrogen down to the wastes of protozoa.

The authors picture each microbe as a little bag of fertiliser. Nutrients locked inside living bodies stay put when it rains, and the grazers open the bags beside the roots. Soluble fertiliser works the other way round: in their view, most of it drains away past the roots.

### Nitrogen from the air

Peas, beans and clover carry <Term id="rhizobia">rhizobia</Term> in small swellings on their roots. These bacteria take nitrogen from the air, and some of it stays in the soil for the plants that come after. Alders do the same with a different partner.

## Who eats whom

Read the web from the bottom up. Each level eats the one below, but many soil animals feed at several levels, which is why it is a web and not a chain.

In the authors' view, a big, varied web also keeps disease in check: harmful organisms have to compete for food, air and space, and some get eaten. Balance still matters, they add. Too many grazers hurt the microbes, and then the plants.

<Note title="Moles and birds">
The authors read moles and digging birds as a sign of food below, such as grubs and worms. Look at what they are hunting before you reach for a poison. Other signs are in [What's in your soil?](/guides/reading-your-soil/).
</Note>

## Does your plant want fungi or bacteria?

Leave bare ground alone and it fills with weeds, then grasses, then shrubs and finally forest: this slow change is <Term id="succession">succession</Term>. The authors point out that the soil changes with it. Young soil that is often disturbed is run mostly by bacteria; old forest soil that nobody digs is run mostly by fungi.

Their rule of thumb: **a plant that lives one season likes a bacterial soil; a plant that lives for years likes a fungal one.** Vegetables and annual flowers sit on the left, shrubs, perennials and trees on the right, and lawn grass near the middle. The authors tie this to the form of nitrogen each group prefers, and they describe a soil by its <Term id="fungal-bacterial-ratio">fungal-to-bacterial ratio</Term>.

## What breaks the web

According to the authors, a garden usually loses its soil life in a handful of ways. Once it is gone, the gardener has to do by hand, and from a bag, what the soil used to do.

<Points>
- **Digging and rototilling.** They tear fungal threads, kill worms, crush small animals and collapse the crumbs. See [Vegetable beds without digging](/guides/no-dig-beds/).
- **Soluble salt fertilisers.** They feed the plant directly and, the authors say, harm or drive off much of the soil life; earthworms move out.
- **Pesticides, fungicides, weedkillers.** They kill some members of the web outright and upset the rest.
- **Compaction.** It squeezes out the air spaces. Nematodes need open soil, so they die or leave, and less nitrogen reaches the roots.
- **Bare soil and raked-away leaves.** They take away the web's food and its cover.
</Points>

After years of chemical care, the authors expect a garden to take a year or more to rebuild its soil life. A garden that has long been organic needs only adjustments.

<Note title="Two ways to look at soil">
Joseph Lofthouse, in [Landrace gardening](/guides/landrace-gardening/), does not amend his soil: he grows crops that suit it. Lowenfels and Lewis work on the soil itself. Both approaches can share one garden.
</Note>

## Three tools to bring it back

The authors restore soil life, and then keep it, with the same three tools, chosen bed by bed to suit what grows there.

<Columns>
<Card label="Inoculate" title="Compost">

Adds a whole community of soil organisms, with the food to keep it going. Spread on the surface, its life moves down into the soil below.

[Compost that feeds soil life →](/guides/compost-for-soil-life/)

</Card>
<Card label="Feed and cover" title="Mulch">

Leaves, wood chips or clippings left on top, where they rot slowly in place. Coarse, woody mulch feeds fungi; fresh, green mulch feeds bacteria.

</Card>
<Card label="Optional" title="Compost tea">

Compost brewed in water with an air pump, then poured or sprayed. The authors rely on it; read about both its claims and its risks before you try it.

</Card>
</Columns>

For some plantings the authors add a fourth tool: <Term id="mycorrhiza">mycorrhizal</Term> fungi, root partners that can be missing from new or disturbed ground ([Mycorrhizal fungi](/guides/mycorrhizal-fungi/)). And they give up digging altogether ([Vegetable beds without digging](/guides/no-dig-beds/)). Mark which beds get fungal or bacterial care in your Canopi design.

The authors close their book with an old forest that nobody has ever fertilised, and that still thrives on its own web.
