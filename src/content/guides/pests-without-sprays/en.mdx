Can you grow plants that stand up to pests without spraying them? What can you do about the birds or raccoons that strip your corn? Should you save seed from a tomato with blossom end rot? And what do the letters on a tomato packet really promise? This guide answers with the way Joseph Lofthouse gardens: he lets his local pests help choose next year's seed parents.

<QuickAnswer>
Each year, save seed only from plants that did well alongside your local pests and diseases, and pull badly affected plants early so they shed no pollen. Over the years the crop builds resistance from many genes, which pests rarely beat all at once. Animals can be selected around too: Lofthouse's corn grew taller and stiffer once he saved only cobs the animals had left alone.
</QuickAnswer>

## Two kinds of resistance

Which lasts longer: one strong defence, or many small ones?

- **<Term id="vertical-resistance">Vertical resistance</Term>** rests on one gene with a big effect. It holds well, until a pest or disease finds a way round that gene. Then every plant that relied on it falls at once.
- **<Term id="horizontal-resistance">Horizontal resistance</Term>** is spread over many genes, each adding a little. A pest that gets past one of them still meets all the others.

Lofthouse credits the idea to Raoul Robinson's book *Return to Resistance*, which he recommends.

**The letters on tomato packets.** Catalogues often print a row of letters after a tomato's name, each standing for a single resistance gene. Many gardeners read a long row as a tough plant. Lofthouse reads it the other way: as a stack of single-gene defences, each one a lock that a pest could pick.

**Uniform crops fail together.** When every plant in a field carries the same defences, a disease that beats one beats them all, and it can spread across a region fast. Lofthouse's example is the US corn blight of 1970.

## Selecting with your pests

The method is simple, and slow. Grow the crop where its pests and diseases are, and save seed from the plants that do well, year after year. You choose on what you can see, the plant's <Term id="phenotype">phenotype</Term>; the resistance builds up underneath.

**Pull the worst early.** When a plant is badly diseased or infested, Lofthouse pulls it before it flowers, so none of its pollen reaches next year's seed. This is <Term id="roguing">roguing</Term>.

**Start from varied seed.** For his tomato project, he and his collaborators picked older varieties with no named resistance genes. These tomatoes cross freely, so many small defences keep being shuffled into new combinations. See [Tomatoes that cross](/guides/tomatoes/).

**His choice: nothing sprayed.** Lofthouse sprays nothing on his garden, organic products included, and his plants live or die on their own. That is his practice and his view. You can save seed from survivors without gardening exactly as he does.

## Birds and mammals

At harvest, save seed only from cobs the animals left alone, on tall plants with strong stalks. Skip any plant that was knocked flat, even if only part of its cob was eaten.

Lofthouse has done this twice with corn. In one sweet corn, the short plants held their cobs at just the right height for pheasants. Later, raccoons and skunks took to raiding another corn. Each time, after some years of saving only from untouched cobs on tall plants, the raids stopped: the stalks grew stiffer and the cobs sat higher.

## Blossom end rot

Should you save seed from a plant with <Term id="blossom-end-rot">blossom end rot</Term>? Lofthouse's answer is no. When even one fruit on a tomato or squash shows it, he pulls the whole plant and keeps none of its seed. His tomatoes and squash no longer get it.

Many gardeners put it down to soil or uneven watering. Lofthouse sees an inherited weakness instead, and finds it easy to select out.

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Your growing conditions may play a part as well. Either way, leaving their seed out costs you nothing.

## Living with insects

These are Lofthouse's own experiments. Treat them as ideas to test, not as sure things.

**Cabbage caterpillars.** Want the birds to find them for you? Lofthouse grows red cabbage and kale, reasoning that a green caterpillar stands out on a red leaf, where predators spot it. Timing can help as well: see [Dodging pests and weather](/guides/season-shifting/#dodging-pests-and-weather).

**Hornworms.** In his account, the big caterpillars on his tomatoes turn into long-tongued moths that visit deep flowers. So he shares the plants with them: there is plenty of fruit. Some of the caterpillars also host parasitic wasps, which he welcomes.

**Colorado potato beetle.** This one is his experiment, and he reports that the beetles, which live in his garden year-round, now leave his potatoes alone. His rules for them: a crop plant that keeps drawing beetles is <Term id="culling">culled</Term>; beetles found on potatoes or tomatoes are crushed by hand; and beetles that keep to a wild nightshade weed, which he lets grow in parts of the garden, are left in peace. He expects it to work only with pests that stay all year, not with ones blown in on the wind. Keep in mind that a weed left as a host may also shelter other pests and seed itself around. Its berries can be poisonous; keep it away from where children play.

<FoodCaution risk="nightshades" />

**Butterflies.** Where showy milkweed comes up in his fields, he lets it stay, even in a row, for the monarch butterflies it feeds.

**Fuzz.** He is exploring whether fuzzy leaves or fruit put off insects and animals. So far it is an idea, not a result.

## Keep your options open

Resistance takes years to build, and a new pest can turn up in any season. Grow several crops, and several species within a family, so that no single pest takes everything. Keep some older seed in store too, in case a line falters. For crops usually kept as identical <Term id="clone">clones</Term>, Lofthouse goes back to seed to breed new, varied plants.
