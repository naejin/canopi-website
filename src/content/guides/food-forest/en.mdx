You have a few fruit trees, perhaps a vegetable patch, and you keep hearing about food forests. This guide shows how to grow a garden in layers, starting from what you have, and what to plant first where you live. It follows Eric Toensmeier's *The Carbon Farming Solution*; why these gardens hold so much carbon is in [Carbon farming](/guides/carbon-farming/).

<QuickAnswer>
A <Term id="homegarden">homegarden</Term> is a small, dense planting around a house, stacked in layers: tall trees, smaller trees, shrubs, herbs and ground cover, climbers, and roots and mushrooms underground. It is one of the oldest ways of farming in the tropics; food forests and forest gardens are the same idea in cooler places. Start from what you grow now, add layers a few at a time, mix in plants that feed the soil, and grow annual crops in the gaps while the trees are young. In Toensmeier's survey, few ways of growing food store as much carbon.
</QuickAnswer>

## What a homegarden is

Forget the lawn and vegetable bed that "home garden" calls to mind. In the book's sense, a homegarden is a yard-sized patch beside the house where trees and crops share the space on many levels, often with a few animals. In Southeast Asia it may go back some 13,000 years, Toensmeier reports, and across the humid tropics it is still an everyday sight.

Food forest and forest garden are the names the permaculture movement gave the idea when it took it to cooler countries in the 1970s; in the author's view, it owes the tropical homegarden more than it admits, and should study it. The idea travels well: the book reports such gardens in tropical highlands, Mediterranean climates, dry places from Arizona to Jordan, and humid temperate and even boreal countries.

Toensmeier ranks <Term id="multistrata">multistrata</Term> plantings, the many-layered kind, at or near the top of every way of growing food for the carbon they store. He is frank about their limit in cold climates: almost no staple perennials besides fruit have been domesticated there, and he calls breeding them a priority.

Most of the carbon, and most of the shade, sits in the woody top layers. Lower down, the author notes, shrubs hold more carbon than herbs and grasses, so give the understorey woody plants too. Light decides what fits below: trees that go bare for part of the year let more grow beneath them than a dense evergreen crown.

Climbers need a post, and a living one works: in the tropics, farmers train vines up a palm trunk or a <Term id="nitrogen-fixer">nitrogen fixer</Term>, a tree that feeds nitrogen into the soil. The shadiest corners suit mushrooms, but only grown from bought spawn of a named edible kind: mushrooms that come up on their own are not food, and even in a spawn bed, never eat one you have not identified ([Mycorrhizal fungi](/guides/mycorrhizal-fungi/)).

<FoodCaution risk="wild-foods" />

And you do not need every layer to begin: some old tree-crop systems in the book are just one crop under one shade tree.

## Start from what you have

Researchers the book cites set three priorities for homegardens, and they make a good plan for yours: look after the garden you have; improve its layout, care and plants; then extend it. Many layered gardens began as something else: at Maya Mountain Research Farm in Belize, an old citrus orchard became a homegarden that feeds the household and sells produce.

If you start from an orchard, look at the ground between the trees. Bare soil there costs carbon, in the author's view. Fill it with lower layers, or at least cover it ([Mulch](/guides/mulch/), [Living soil](/guides/living-soil/)).

Natives first: every region has its own nitrogen fixers and useful plants. Before you bring anything in from elsewhere, check your local list of invasive plants.

## Give every plant room

The book gives no spacings for homegardens, but it does give principles.

<Points>

- **Plants compete** for water, light and nutrients, and some hold neighbours back with substances they release (<Term id="allelopathy">allelopathy</Term>). Still, well-chosen mixtures often out-yield the same plants grown apart, in the studies the author cites.
- **Mix fast and slow trees.** Fast growers store carbon sooner; slow ones catch up after the first years and make denser wood.
- **Several jobs for each plant, several plants for each job**, two permaculture principles the author leans on.
- **Support plants take room.** Nitrogen fixers and mulch plants use space that food could fill; the author counts that as the price of a garden that keeps its soil.
- **Add layers slowly.** The author counts underestimating how hard a complex planting is to run among permaculture's mistakes, his own teaching included.
- **Fewer trees where water is short.** Trees drink: see [Limits in dry places](/guides/water-and-slopes/#limits-in-dry-places).

</Points>

While the trees are small, there is sun between them. Grow annual crops and short-lived plants there: the book calls this <Term id="successional-intercropping">successional intercropping</Term>, and it puts food on the table while you wait.

As the shade spreads, swap the lower layers for crops that like shade. The garden moves through stages much as an abandoned field turns back into woodland (<Term id="succession">succession</Term>).

Some tree crops take years to bear and a few take decades, the author warns, so plan what you will eat in the meantime.

Harvest without killing the plant. Bananas and other clump plants are cut one stem at a time (<Term id="managed-multistem">managed multistem</Term>), and many shrubs regrow when cut to the ground (<Term id="coppice">coppice</Term>; the cuts are in [Keep them low](/guides/trees-with-edible-leaves/#keep-them-low)).

## Animals and mushrooms

Many homegardens keep small animals: poultry, small livestock, in some of China's temperate gardens even fish. The book's tree-crop farms add two ideas: poultry working through the planting for insects, and pigs cleaning up fallen nuts once the harvest is in.

<Safety title="Animals among young trees">
Animals can damage young trees and crops, and they can be poisoned by toxic plants within their reach. Keep them out until the trees are established, and know which plants in your garden are toxic to them.
</Safety>

## A first stack for your climate

Below is one example stack per climate, from the book's own examples and species: a starting point, to swap natives into. Check your local list of invasive plants before planting anything from elsewhere.

In snowy places, a dark evergreen crown soaks up sunlight that the snow would have sent back (its <Term id="albedo">albedo</Term>), and that eats into the carbon the tree stores. So the continental and boreal stacks lean on trees that drop their leaves.

Some plants in these stacks are not food as picked: olives, and the seeds of Siberian pea shrub, which the author does not count as food at all. Pigeon peas are dry beans.

<FoodCaution risk={["needs-processing", "dry-beans"]} />

<ForClimate zones="tropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
For the humid tropics, where homegardens are oldest.

- **Breadfruit**: a tall staple-fruit tree for hot, wet lowlands; it will not take the cooler subtropics.
- **Bananas or plantains**: the middle layer, cut stem by stem.
- **Cacao**: a shade lover for the floor beneath taller trees, as in Brazil's plantings under palms.
- **Passion fruit**: a climber that farmers in tropical Africa train up nitrogen-fixing trees used as living trellises.

</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="subtropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Mild winters with light frost; the last line is for the tropical highlands.

- **Avocado**: the Mexican types are the hardiest; a middle to tall layer.
- **Pigeon pea**: a short-lived nitrogen fixer for the lower layer, cut back to renew it, and a living trellis too. Its dry seeds are a staple: see the caution above and [Perennial beans and edible tree pods](/guides/perennial-beans-and-pods/).
- **For the tropical highlands**, the book's example from Veracruz, Mexico, stacks avocado and macadamia over bananas over coffee. Macadamia only where it never freezes.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="mediterranean" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Winter rain and a dry summer: water decides how many trees you can carry.

- **Olive**: long-lived, for the top layer, and it takes coppicing. The fruit is not eaten from the tree (caution above).
- **Grape**: the climber; Greek farmers grow it with olives and annual crops.
- **Pistachio**: a nut tree for long, dry summers; it needs a cold spell in winter to set fruit.

</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="arid" source="carbon-farming-solution">
For deserts and oases, the book's arid band.

- **Date palm**: the canopy of the old oasis gardens of Africa and West and South Asia; it needs a long, hot, dry season to ripen.
- **Pomegranate and fig**: two of the fruit trees grown in the palms' shade.
- **Grain**: oasis farmers sow annual grain beneath it all.

Where water is short, add cacti and succulents rather than more trees.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="temperate" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "warm temperate". This stack comes from China's temperate homegardens.

- **Japanese pagoda tree** (*Styphnolobium japonicum*): a canopy tree the book counts among nitrogen fixers.
- **Pear and peach**: the fruit layer.
- **Grape**: the climber.
- **Mushrooms and medicinal herbs**: in the shade under the trees. Mushrooms from bought spawn only (see [The layers](#the-layers)); use a medicinal herb only if you can name it with certainty, and ask a health professional first.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="continental" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "cold temperate": long frosts, warm summers.

- **Heartnut**: in the author's account, the best walnut for cold climates; its nuts leave the shell easily.
- **Hazel**: a shrub layer that bears every year, unlike many cold-climate nuts.
- **Siberian pea shrub**: a very hardy nitrogen fixer and windbreak, grown here for the soil, not for food (caution above).

</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="boreal" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Bitter winters: few food plants have been domesticated this far north, in the author's view.

- **Butternut**: a nut tree hardy into the boreal zone, grown as a new crop in Estonia.
- **Siberian or Manchurian hazel**: shrub hazels that take boreal cold.
- **Sea buckthorn** <Invasive />: a nitrogen fixer for deep cold and poor or salty soil; spiny, and it suckers.

<Safety title="Spiny shrubs">
Sea buckthorn has sharp spines: plant it away from paths and play areas, and wear gloves to work it.
</Safety>

Here, in the author's view, evergreens over snow can cancel their whole carbon gain: choose trees that drop their leaves.
</ForClimate>
