You grow two zucchini, or a sweet corn beside a popcorn, or three kinds of bean, and you want to save seed. Will they mix? The answer comes in two parts: can these two crops cross at all, and if they can, how close is too close.

<QuickAnswer>
Only plants of the same species cross, as a rule. A zucchini and a jack-o'-lantern pumpkin are one species and cross readily; a zucchini and a butternut are two species and rarely do. Most pollen lands on the nearest flowers, so crossing drops fast with distance. Lofthouse treats self-pollinating crops as separate at 3 m (10 ft) and outcrossing crops at 30 m (100 ft), and accepts the few percent of crosses that still happen; if you need pure seed, use the much larger distances of a seed-saving organisation. Varieties that flower at different times do not cross.
</QuickAnswer>

## Same species or not?

Crossing happens within a species. Garden names hide this: "pumpkin" can belong to any of three squash species, while muskmelon, cantaloupe and honeydew are all one. Look up the species, not the name. Find your crop in the index below, or open any entry.

### Does a cross change this year's crop?

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
In most crops, no. The squash or pepper you pick this year belongs to the mother plant; a cross only shows in plants grown from its seed. A sweet pepper grown next to a hot one is still a sweet pepper, but its seed may grow hot plants next year.

Corn is the exception, because the kernels are the seeds: a cross shows on the cob in the same season. One coloured plant in a white patch leaves coloured kernels scattered over the nearby white cobs, and sweet corn pollinated by starchy corn can give starchy kernels.

## How close is too close?

First, find out how your crop is pollinated; the list above gives the group for each one.

- **<Term id="self-pollinating">Self-pollinating</Term> crops** (tomatoes, peas, common beans) mostly fertilise their own flowers. Crosses are rare; in his garden, 3 m (10 ft) between varieties is enough.
- **<Term id="outcrossing">Outcrossing</Term> crops** need pollen from other plants, so they cross easily. Squash and corn are <Term id="monoecious">monoecious</Term> (separate male and female flowers on one plant); spinach is <Term id="dioecious">dioecious</Term> (male plants and female plants), so every seed is a cross.

Then think of distance as a dial, not a wall. Pollen mostly reaches the nearest flower it can fertilise, and Lofthouse's rule of thumb is that crossing falls with the square of the distance: double the gap, a quarter of the crosses. Take it as his intuition, not a promise; bees and storms can carry pollen much further.

Those are distances for a household landrace. If you plan to share seed under a variety name, use the larger distances of a seed-saving organisation in your country.

## Pick a layout for your goal

<Term id="isolation-distance">Isolation distance</Term> is only one tool. Choose the layout that matches what you want from the bed.

### You want crosses, for a landrace

Do the opposite of isolating: sow the varieties jumbled together, with plants that look different side by side so their crosses stand out. Self-pollinating crops cross more when planted close and visited by plenty of pollinators, so keep something in flower all season.

<PlotDiagram variant="mixed" />

### Two corns, one field

Choose varieties that flower at different times: plants not in flower together cannot cross. This is <Term id="isolation-by-time">isolation by time</Term>, and it is how Lofthouse grows flour and sweet corn in one field.

### Several colours you want to keep

Give each colour a block of its own, blocks touching. Crossing stays at the shared edges, so the middle of each block keeps its colour, and blocks that do not touch hardly cross at all. Save seed from the middle of each block. He keeps <Term id="sister-lines">sister lines</Term> in one field this way.

<PlotDiagram variant="colour-blocks" />

### Two kinds in one row

Plant one kind at each end. The crosses gather where they meet in the middle; save seed from the ends.

<PlotDiagram variant="row-ends" />

Planning on a garden map helps here: in [Canopi](/) you can place your varieties in blocks or at row ends and see the distances between beds before you sow.

## Crosses to avoid

Some crosses cost years of work to undo.

- **Hot peppers with sweet peppers.** Once heat is in your sweet peppers, selecting it back out is slow. Lofthouse keeps them apart.
- **Sweet corn with starchy corn.** Keep them apart, by distance or by flowering time.
- **Ornamental gourds with food squash.** Gourds are close to a wild ancestor, and they share a species with zucchini and pumpkins.
- **Wild relatives with any crop.** Lofthouse now gives a wild ancestor a field of its own at first: keeping it apart is easier than removing a trait later.

<Safety title="Keep wild relatives away from your seed crops">
Grow wild relatives of a crop well away from the plants you save seed from. After a cross with a wild type, plants grown from that seed can bear bitter, poisonous fruit.

Lofthouse catches such crosses by tasting every fruit before he keeps its seed. Once, a tiny perfumed melon in his patch tasted so foul that he threw away all the melon seed he had saved that season. He also warns that a nightshade's poison may taste pleasant and still leave him feeling sick. Do not copy his test: a small taste can poison you, spat out or not, and a pleasant one proves nothing.
</Safety>

Wild genes can also bring traits you then spend years selecting out, such as <Term id="hard-seed">hard seed</Term> that will not take up water ([how he cleared it from his tepary beans](/guides/beans-and-legumes/#hard-seed)).

## How much crossing can you accept?

For a landrace, Lofthouse finds a few <Term id="off-type">off-types</Term> no trouble as long as you look at what you harvest. Lofthouse handles every fruit at harvest, and the ones he does not want go to the compost.

He is at ease with crosses between garden varieties. By his account, people have been taming crops for as long as 40,000 years, long enough to remove most unwanted traits, so two domesticated varieties give domesticated offspring; the risky crosses are those with wild types, above. He is as relaxed about plant counts: he wonders whether the minimum numbers of seed parents in seed-saving books were pushed by big seed companies to discourage home seed saving, and gives no such number himself.

If you want to keep one heirloom exactly as it is, grow it well apart from its relatives, or keep a packet of the original seed in reserve. To be sure a cross does happen, make it by hand. Before you save from a hybrid, read [Can I save seed from this?](/guides/hybrids-heirlooms-landraces/).
