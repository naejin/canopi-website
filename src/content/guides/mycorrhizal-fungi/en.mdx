Should you buy a packet of root fungi? Which kind does your tree or your tomato use, and what is killing the ones already in your soil? Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis treat these fungi as a fourth tool beside compost, mulch and compost tea, needed only where the fungi may be missing. A <Term id="mycorrhiza">mycorrhiza</Term> is the partnership itself, root and fungus working as one. For the wider picture, start with [Living soil](/guides/living-soil/).

<QuickAnswer>
Nearly every plant trades sugars from its roots with soil fungi that bring back water and nutrients, phosphorus above all. Trees such as oak, birch, beech and pines use fungi that wrap the root, the kind that make mushrooms; vegetables, grasses, shrubs and perennials use fungi that grow inside it. The authors say undisturbed soil already has them: they inoculate seedlings in potting mix, soil after digging, fungicides or heavy fertiliser, compacted or building-site soil, and new trees and shrubs. For vegetables they go further and inoculate every sowing; in undug, unsprayed beds that is their habit, not a need. Cabbages, beets and spinach take no partner, so skip them. Never eat a mushroom you find in the garden.
</QuickAnswer>

## A trade at the root

A fungus cannot make its own food. A mycorrhizal one lives on the sugars a root releases into the soil, its <Term id="root-exudates">exudates</Term>. In return it gathers water and nutrients and passes them into the root: phosphorus most of all, but also nitrogen and traces such as zinc, copper and iron, freed from forms the root could not take up alone.

The secret is reach. A root soon empties the soil right around it. The fungus's threads, its <Term id="hyphae">hyphae</Term>, grow beyond that worn-out zone and into pores far too narrow for any root. By the authors' figures, the partnership can make a tree's working root surface 700 to 1,000 times larger, and at least 90%, probably 95%, of plants take part.

The authors credit the fungi with more, as their view: one network can feed several plants at once, even of different kinds; a colonised root leaves disease organisms less room and food; and the fungi's sticky coating helps bind soil into crumbs.

## Two kinds, and plants that use neither

<Term id="ectomycorrhiza">Ectomycorrhizal</Term> fungi wrap the root in a sheath and grow between its outer cells. These are the ones you can see, as white threads and as mushrooms. Most conifers and many broad-leaved trees use them; the book names birch, oak, beech, hazel, hickory, pines and firs.

<Term id="arbuscular-mycorrhiza">Arbuscular mycorrhizal</Term> fungi, also called endomycorrhizal, grow into the root's cells and out into the soil. Only a microscope shows them, and they die without a host. Most vegetables, annual flowers, grasses, shrubs and perennials use them.

Two groups need fungi you cannot buy: orchids and the heath family (blueberry, heather, rhododendron, azalea) each have their own. And some plants take no partner at all: the cabbage family (cabbage, broccoli, mustard) and the beet and spinach group, fat hen included. Inoculating them, the authors say, wastes your money.

## When adding them helps

The authors' starting point: undisturbed garden soil should already hold every fungus it needs. A packet earns its place where something has broken that.

<Points>
- **Seedlings in pots.** Potting mix, even compost, holds few spores and may be sterilised.
- **New trees, shrubs and perennials.** Inoculate before planting, with the kind the plant uses.
- **After digging or tilling.** Stop digging, then inoculate seeds and plants for a season or two.
- **After fungicides or heavy chemical fertiliser.** The same: a season or two of inoculating.
- **Compacted or building-site soil.** First loosen it by pulling out plugs of soil, then inoculate, under trees too.
- **Vegetables and annuals.** The authors go further here: their own rule is to add arbuscular fungi at every sowing and transplanting, apart from the families with no partner, and they find the gain grows with a long season and in containers. In undug, unsprayed beds that is their habit, not a need.
</Points>

In the authors' Anchorage, maples long failed; the few that arrived with soil on their roots lived, and bare-root maples now take too, helped by bought fungi.

## When you can skip it

- Healthy soil that has not been dug or dosed with chemicals.
- An established tree with the same mushroom coming up beneath it year after year: the partnership is already there. The book's example is fly agaric under birch.
- Cabbages, beets, spinach and their relatives.
- Soil very rich in phosphate: the authors report that many plants form no partnership at all once phosphate passes about 50 to 80 ppm (parts per million).

<Safety title="Never eat garden or lawn mushrooms">
Mushrooms under trees and in lawns are often the fruit of these fungi, and some are poisonous: the fly agaric is one. Do not eat them, and keep children and pets away from them.
</Safety>

Treat mushrooms under a tree as a good sign, never as a harvest.

## How to apply an inoculant

An <Term id="inoculant">inoculant</Term> is a dry powder or granules of spores and fungal pieces. The authors say spores must meet a living root within 24 hours of getting wet, so everything comes down to contact.

<Steps compact>
<Step title="Match the kind">

Ecto for most conifers and trees such as oak, birch and beech; arbuscular for nearly everything else. Check the date on the pack: spores keep longer than other fungal pieces, but fresher is better.

</Step>
<Step title="Seeds and pots">

Roll seed in the powder before sowing, or mix some into the potting mix. Lawn seed, for a new lawn or for patching, takes the same treatment.

</Step>
<Step title="Transplants">

Dust the exposed roots or dip them in the powder, plant, and water in as usual.

</Step>
<Step title="Established plants">

For trees, the authors push it to the roots with a root feeder or a long syringe. Around shrubs and perennials, dig carefully into the root zone and dust any roots you uncover.

</Step>
</Steps>

Pour the powder gently, outdoors or out of any draught, and don't breathe the dust. {/* general tip, not from the book */}

The authors also add fungi to compost tea at the very end of a brew; [Compost tea](/guides/compost-tea/) explains why this site keeps tea off food plants.

## What harms them

<Points ruled="accent">
- **Digging and tilling.** Rotavating and double digging tear the threads; spores end up away from roots and must start over. See [Vegetable beds without digging](/guides/no-dig-beds/).
- **Compaction.** Foot traffic, parked cars, building work, even a ride-on mower crush the networks. Aerate, then inoculate.
- **Fungicides and some pesticides.** The authors count both as harmful; after them, inoculate for a season or two.
- **Rich fertiliser.** The authors say chemical fertiliser harms the fungi, high phosphorus stops plants from forming partnerships, and heavy nitrogen kills crop fungi while feeding weeds. Their rule: no feed with any of its three NPK numbers above 10.
- **Sterilised soil.** Sterilising kills them outright; add some back when you plant into it.
- **Mulch piled too deep.** More than 5 to 7.5 cm (2 to 3 in), the authors say, can smother them. See [Mulch](/guides/mulch/).
</Points>
