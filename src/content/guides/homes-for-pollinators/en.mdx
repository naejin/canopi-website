Flowers feed the bees and butterflies that pass through your garden. Whether they stay and breed depends on everything else: somewhere to lay eggs, cover from wind and rain, a warm spot to start the day, a drink, and a place to wait out the months when nothing flowers ([What every helper needs](/guides/garden-helpers/#four-needs)). Kim Eierman, who designs gardens in the northeastern United States, finds that most of this costs nothing: you mainly stop tidying away what insects live in.

<QuickAnswer>
Most wild bees nest in bare, sunny ground; the rest use hollow stems, old wood and crevices. Leave a patch of bare soil undug and unmulched, keep dead stems, logs and a tight brush pile, and leave some fallen leaves whole. Cut back late, in stages, and keep dead trees only where a fall can hurt no one.
</QuickAnswer>

## Bees that nest in the ground

Most wild bees dig their own nests. These <Term id="ground-nesting-bee">ground-nesting bees</Term> are nearly all <Term id="solitary-bee">solitary bees</Term>: each female digs a tunnel, stocks small cells at its end with pollen and nectar, lays an egg in each and seals them. Eierman counts about seven bee species in ten as ground nesters in North America; the share elsewhere is not in her book.

Give them a patch they can use:

- **In sun, out of the way.** Away from paths, the mower and anywhere people walk or play.
- **Light soil if you have it.** Sandy or loamy ground digs easily; heavy clay and very rich soil are harder and stay wetter.
- **Small is fine.** Even a little bare corner can hold many nests.
- **Left alone in every season.** No mulch, no landscape fabric, no digging and no tidying, even when the garden is asleep.

If bare earth looks neglected to you, plant it very thinly, or set low flowering shrubs round it, as long as they never shade it.

This is where Eierman and the Soil guides part ways. In [Living soil](/guides/living-soil/) and [Mulch](/guides/mulch/), Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis treat bare soil as a loss, with no food or cover for soil life, and mulch every bed. Bees cannot dig through mulch, so she keeps ground bare for them. Both are right, for different animals: mulch the beds you grow in, and set aside one sunny patch, away from them, that stays bare.

## Bees that nest in hollows

The other bees, bumble bees among them, are <Term id="cavity-nesting-bee">cavity-nesting bees</Term>: rather than dig, they move into a hole someone else made. Old beetle tunnels in dead wood, a hollow trunk, a gap in a dry-stone wall, an abandoned bird or mouse nest, a dense tangle of plant debris can all serve. Keep a few of each:

<Points>
- **Dead wood** A <Term id="snag">snag</Term> is worth keeping wherever its fall can harm nothing; if it is too tall, cut it down to a safe height and leave the trunk, which insects still use. Leave fallen logs to rot where they lie. As they break down they fill with mosses, ferns and seedlings, a planter you did not have to build.
- **Soft-centred stems** Elder, brambles, raspberries and other plants with <Term id="pithy-stem">pithy stems</Term> give small bees a stem to hollow out. Leave dead stems standing, or cut them, tie them in bundles and set the bundles somewhere dry.
- **A brush pile** Keep the branches you prune and stack them tight and dense, so the gaps inside stay dry in rain: bumble bees may move in. Woven into a low wattle fence, the same prunings shelter bees and look deliberate.
</Points>

Bee hotels, drilled wooden blocks or bundles of hollow tubes, can help cavity nesters too. Eierman warns that there is more to them than it looks, cleaning to keep disease out included. Learn how to care for one, from a local beekeeping or conservation group, before you buy it; the stems, wood and brush above need no care at all.

<Safety title="Dead trees and stings">
Keep a dead tree only where its fall can hurt no one and damage nothing: well away from the house, paths, roads and the neighbours. Cut a tall one down to a height that is safe. Most bees are calm and sting only when their nest is disturbed, but bumble bees and social wasps such as yellow jackets live in colonies and defend them together. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Keep nest patches, brush piles and logs away from doors, paths and play areas. If anyone in your household is allergic to stings, ask a doctor before inviting nests close to the house.
</Safety>

## Butterflies and moths

Butterflies and moths build no nests. A female lays her eggs on a <Term id="larval-host-plant">larval host plant</Term>, the plant her caterpillars can eat ([Leaves for caterpillars](/guides/flowers-for-pollinators/#host-plants)). Once grown, many caterpillars leave that plant to change into a chrysalis or cocoon somewhere quiet: in fallen leaves, in the soil or hanging from a twig. Adults rest under leaves, behind loose bark and in cracks between rocks, and shelter from bad weather in brush and log piles. Pollinating flies and many moths also need a corner with leaf litter and fallen logs to finish their lives. The same untidy places serve them all.

## Shelter, sun and water

<Points>
- **Out of the wind** Wind tires small insects and blows them off course, and a bee caught out may not reach its nest in time. A <Term id="windbreak">windbreak</Term> of flowering trees and shrubs gives shelter, a little warmth and more flowers. Mix species and stagger the rows: one shrub in a straight line is a green wall, and a single pest or disease can take it all. Keep it from shading your bare patch. [Windbreaks, living fences and hedges](/guides/windbreaks-and-living-fences/) covers the layout.
- **Grass tussocks** Clump-forming wild grasses shelter insects and feed the caterpillars of skippers, small brown and orange butterflies; a bumble bee queen may spend the winter at the base of one. Choose your region's own kinds.
- **Sun** Insects are cold-blooded and must warm up before they can fly. Plant flowers in sun, and set a few large stones in sunny spots: they hold the heat, and butterflies bask on them.
- **A safe drink** Fill a plant saucer with small, plain pebbles and pour in water to just below their tops, so insects drink from a dry perch instead of drowning. Leave bird baths to the birds. A pond with shallow, still edges works too. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Top the saucer up and change the water often.
- **Damp ground** Male butterflies gather on wet sand or mud to sip salts and minerals, called <Term id="puddling">puddling</Term>. Keep any natural damp spot you have. You can try a saucer of wet sand and gravel with a little finished compost and a pinch of salt, though Eierman admits butterflies may ignore it. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Wash your hands after handling compost.
</Points>

## Winter homes

When the weather turns too cold for flowers or flight, many insects stop growing and wait in a resting state called <Term id="diapause">diapause</Term>, as eggs, larvae, pupae or adults. They hide in dead stems, fallen leaves, loose bark, logs and the soil. A bumble bee queen spends the winter alone, often in a small hollow under the leaves; the young of solitary bees wait sealed in the nest their mother made. The book writes about cold winters; where the hard season is a dry one instead, the hiding places are the same, but the timings below are not.

- Leave stems and seed heads standing until the next season's growth starts.
- Leave some fallen leaves whole on the ground, and skip the leaf blower.
- Don't rake, chop or mow them all: Eierman warns that this kills the caterpillars, pupae and adults sheltering inside.

The Soil guides advise the opposite here too: [Mulch](/guides/mulch/) and [A year of soil care](/guides/soil-care-year/) run a mower over fallen leaves, because broken leaves feed soil life faster. A middle way: leave whole leaves under shrubs, along a hedge and in one quiet corner, and chop or gather the rest for your beds.

## Cold winters

<ForClimate zones={["temperate", "continental", "boreal"]} source="pollinator-victory-garden">
Where winters are cold (the book writes from the northeastern United States):

- Cut back last year's stems only in spring, once the days are mild: Eierman waits for daytime temperatures of about 12–13 °C (mid-50s °F), when insects can warm up and leave.
- Leave about 15 cm (6 in) of stubble, and some pithy stems standing whole.
- Cut in stages over a week or more, not all on one day.
- Keep off beds while the spring soil is wet.
- Keep de-icing salt away from beds; many plants cannot bear it.
- Cut a meadow once a year in early spring, in patches over several weeks ([A meadow, not an unmown lawn](/guides/lawn-to-flowers/#a-meadow-not-an-unmown-lawn)).
</ForClimate>
