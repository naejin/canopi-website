Flowers feed the bees and butterflies that pass through your garden. Whether they stay and breed depends on everything else: somewhere to lay eggs, cover from wind and rain, a warm spot to start the day, a drink, and a place to wait out the months when nothing flowers ([What every helper needs](/guides/garden-helpers/#what-every-helper-needs)). Kim Eierman, who designs gardens in the northeastern United States, finds that most of this costs nothing: you mainly stop clearing away the places insects live in.

<QuickAnswer>
Most wild bees nest in bare, sunny ground; the rest use hollow stems, old wood and crevices. Leave a patch of bare soil undug and unmulched, keep dead stems, logs and a tight brush pile, and leave some fallen leaves whole. Cut back late, in stages, and keep dead trees only where a fall can hurt no one.
</QuickAnswer>

## A bare patch for bees that dig

Most wild bees dig their own nests. These <Term id="ground-nesting-bee">ground-nesting bees</Term> are nearly all <Term id="solitary-bee">solitary bees</Term>: each female digs a tunnel, stocks small cells at its end with pollen and nectar, lays an egg in each and seals them. Eierman counts about seven bee species in ten as ground nesters in North America; the share elsewhere is not in her book.

<Points>
- **Pick the spot** Even a small corner will do, if it gets sun and nobody walks, plays or mows there. Sandy or loamy ground is easiest to dig; heavy clay and very rich soil are harder work for a bee and stay wetter.
- **Then leave it alone** Do not dig, tidy or cover it, in any season. Mulch and landscape fabric keep ground-nesting bees away, Eierman warns. If the bare earth bothers you, screen it with low flowering shrubs that do not shade it, or let in only a few plants, far apart.
</Points>

This is where Eierman and the Soil guides part ways. In [Living soil](/guides/living-soil/) and [Mulch](/guides/mulch/), Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis treat bare soil as a loss, with no food or cover for soil life, and mulch the beds. Eierman keeps ground bare because the bees need it. Both are right, for different animals: mulch the beds you grow in, and set aside one sunny patch, away from them, that stays bare.

## Dead wood, where it is safe

The other bees, bumble bees among them, are <Term id="cavity-nesting-bee">cavity-nesting bees</Term>: rather than dig, they move into a hole someone else made, such as an old beetle tunnel, a hollow trunk, a gap in a dry-stone wall or an abandoned bird or mouse nest. Dead wood supplies many of these holes, and butterflies and moths shelter behind its loose bark and in log piles. But a standing dead tree, a <Term id="snag">snag</Term>, can fall, so start with where it stands.

<Safety title="Dead trees and stings">
Keep a dead tree only where its fall can hurt no one and damage nothing: well away from the house, paths, roads and the neighbours. Cut a tall one down to a height that is safe; insects will still use the stump. Most bees are calm and sting only when their nest is disturbed. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Bumble bees and social wasps (paper wasps, yellow jackets) live in colonies, so a disturbed nest can mean many stings. Keep nest patches, brush piles and logs away from doors, paths and play areas. If anyone in your household is allergic to stings, ask a doctor before inviting nests close to the house.
</Safety>

Then keep the wood you would otherwise haul away. Eierman notes that bumble bees may settle in a dense brush pile, so pack your prunings tight enough to keep the rain out of the gaps, or weave them between stakes into a low wattle fence that looks intended. Fallen branches and trunks can stay where they land and rot.

Bee hotels, drilled wooden blocks or bundles of hollow tubes, can help cavity nesters too. Eierman warns that there is more to them than it looks, cleaning to keep disease out included. Learn how to care for one, from a local beekeeping or conservation group, before you buy it; the stems, wood and brush in this guide need no care at all.

## Stems and leaves through winter

When the weather turns too cold for flowers or flight, many insects stop growing and wait in a resting state called <Term id="diapause">diapause</Term>, as eggs, larvae, pupae or adults. Many of them wait in what a tidy gardener clears away.

<Points>
- **Dead stems** Plants with soft, <Term id="pithy-stem">pithy stems</Term>, elder, brambles and raspberries among them, give small bees a stem to hollow out, and the young of solitary bees spend the winter sealed inside. Leave old stems and seed heads standing until the next season's growth starts, or cut them, tie them in bundles and set the bundles somewhere dry.
- **Fallen leaves** Butterflies and moths build no nests. A female lays her eggs on a <Term id="larval-host-plant">larval host plant</Term>, the plant her caterpillars can eat ([Host plants](/guides/flowers-for-pollinators/#host-plants)), and many caterpillars then leave it to spend the winter, or to turn into a chrysalis or cocoon, in the leaves on the ground. A bumble bee queen often waits out the winter alone in a small hollow under them. Pollinating flies need leaf litter too.
- **What not to do** Raking, chopping or mowing all the leaves kills the caterpillars, pupae and adults inside them, Eierman warns. Leave some whole, and skip the leaf blower.
</Points>

The Soil guides advise the opposite here too: [Mulch](/guides/mulch/) and [A year of soil care](/guides/soil-care-year/) run a mower over fallen leaves, because broken leaves feed soil life faster. A middle way: leave whole leaves under shrubs, along a hedge and in one quiet corner, and chop or gather the rest for your beds.

Eierman writes for cold winters only, and says to shift her calendar where winters are milder; the timings in [Cold winters](#cold-winters) are hers.

## Shelter, sun and water

<Points>
- **Out of the wind** Wind tires small insects and blows them off course, and a bee caught out may not reach its nest in time. A <Term id="windbreak">windbreak</Term> of flowering trees and shrubs gives shelter, a little warmth and more flowers. Mix species and stagger the rows: a straight row of one kind of shrub is a green wall, and a single pest or disease can take it all. Keep it from shading your bare patch. [Windbreaks, living fences and hedges](/guides/windbreaks-and-living-fences/) covers the layout.
- **Grass tussocks** Clump-forming wild grasses shelter insects and feed the caterpillars of skippers, small brown and orange butterflies; a bumble bee queen may spend the winter at the base of one. Choose your region's own kinds.
- **Sun** Insects are cold-blooded and must warm up before they can fly. Plant flowers in sun, and set a few large stones in sunny spots: they hold the heat, and butterflies bask on them.
- **A safe drink** Fill a plant saucer with small, plain pebbles and pour in water to just below their tops, so insects drink from a dry perch instead of drowning. Leave bird baths to the birds. A pond with shallow, still edges works too. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Top the saucer up and change the water often.
- **Damp ground** Male butterflies gather on wet sand or mud to sip salts and minerals, called <Term id="puddling">puddling</Term>. Keep any natural damp spot you have. You can try a saucer of wet sand and gravel with a little finished compost and a pinch of salt, though Eierman admits butterflies may ignore it. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Wash your hands after handling compost.
</Points>

## Cold winters

<ForClimate zones={["temperate", "continental", "boreal"]} source="pollinator-victory-garden">
Where winters are cold (the book writes from the northeastern United States):

- Cut back last year's stems only in spring, once the days are mild: Eierman waits for daytime temperatures of about 12–13 °C (mid-50s °F), when insects can warm up and leave.
- Leave about 15 cm (6 in) of stubble, and some pithy stems standing whole.
- Cut in stages over a week or more, not all on one day.
- Keep off beds while the spring soil is wet.
- Keep de-icing salt away from beds; many plants cannot bear it.
- Cut a meadow once a year in early spring, in patches over several weeks ([A meadow, not an unmown lawn](/guides/lawn-to-flowers/#a-meadow-not-an-unmown-lawn)).
</ForClimate>
