Can two kinds of bean grow side by side and still give you seed worth saving? When do favas go in? Will that bag of soup beans from the shop grow? This guide answers all three, from choosing species to cleaning the seed.

<QuickAnswer>
Most beans pollinate their own flowers, so neighbouring varieties seldom cross: Joseph Lofthouse puts common beans at roughly 0.5 to 5%. That makes a mixed jar of beans easy to keep, and any type in it easy to lift out as a variety of its own. Favas and runner beans cross far more, because insects carry their pollen. Sow favas as soon as the soil can be worked, because hot weather stops them setting seed. And grow several legume species, so one bad year never takes them all.
</QuickAnswer>

## Many species, many seasons

Legumes are a whole family, and its members want very different weather. Some grow in the cool of spring and shrug off frost; others need summer heat. Runner beans like it mild and damp.

Lofthouse grows as many legume species as he can. A pest, a disease or an odd summer rarely hits every species in the same year, so something always comes through. Short of room? Grow one short row of a cool-season legume and one of a hot-season one.

## How much do beans cross?

Legumes are mostly <Term id="self-pollinating">self-pollinating</Term>, but not completely. Lofthouse sees anything from under 1% to about a third of seed crossed, depending on the species and the garden.

<CropTable crops={["runner-bean", "fava-bean", "common-bean", "pea", "chickpea"]} columns={["crossing", "ease"]} caption="How often legumes cross." />

Favas and runner beans stand out. Insects carry their pollen from plant to plant, bumblebees above all on favas, so they behave more like <Term id="outcrossing">outcrossing</Term> crops. Common beans and peas sit at the low end.

What this means for you:

- **A few steps apart, common beans mostly come true.** A grower in Oregon who sends Lofthouse beans keeps his varieties in beds a few steps apart. About 1 seed in 100 he harvests has an unexpected colour.
- **Jumbled together, they cross more.** Close mixing, and a lively garden full of flowers and insects, push the rate up.

## Spotting a natural cross

Crosses are rare in common beans, so plant a marker: a trait that makes a hybrid stand out. Three work well.

**Bush beside pole.** Sow a bush bean right next to a pole bean, and save seed from the bush plants. If any of their offspring start to climb, they are crosses with the pole bean. Grow those on: in the generation after, Lofthouse finds about a quarter go back to bush.

**White flowers beside coloured.** Plant a white-flowered bean next to one with coloured flowers. Next year, a coloured flower in the white patch belongs to a hybrid.

**Common bean crossed with runner bean.** A friend of Lofthouse made this cross by hand, with the common bean as the mother. The hybrids had the runner’s scarlet flowers, only paler, and they showed themselves as seedlings too. Common beans lift their <Term id="cotyledons">cotyledons</Term> high above the soil, runners keep theirs buried, and the hybrids held them at or just below the surface.

Found one? Keep its seed apart, give it a good spot next year, and grow plenty of its children. Lofthouse finds that hybrid beans are among the most vigorous plants in his garden for a few generations. From the <Term id="f2">F2</Term> on, a fresh cross keeps reshuffling its parents’ traits for two to four generations: that is when you are most likely to find a type that loves your garden.

## The bean jar

The easiest legume project there is: grow many beans mixed together, and keep them as one jar.

### Start cheap

- **A bag of mixed soup beans from the shop.** Some may not sprout, so sow plenty.
- **A packet from a bean landrace.** Lofthouse reckons one packet of his bush beans can hold some 40 different types.
- **Swaps** with neighbours and other seed savers.

Expect losses the first year. In his garden, about 9 in 10 common-bean varieties brought in from elsewhere leave no seed worth sowing again. That is the point: what survives is what suits your place.

### Each autumn: diversity or yield?

Tip the harvest out and look at it. Then choose.

<Columns>
<Card label="For diversity" title="Equal counts">

Count out about the same number of seeds of each type, and sow those. Lofthouse does this because he grows beans for breeding and sharing; otherwise a small pink bean and the pintos, which thrive with him, would take over his jar.

</Card>
<Card label="For food" title="Sow in bulk">

Sow the jar as it comes. The types that crop best in your garden slowly take over, which is what you want from a food crop.

</Card>
</Columns>

Sorting by eye steers only what shows in the seed, its <Term id="phenotype">phenotype</Term>. A pile of big white beans gives big white seed again, but the plants can still differ in every other way.

### Any bean can become a variety

Because beans pollinate themselves, any type you like can be picked out of the jar, sown on its own, and kept as a stable variety. Lofthouse did this with a bean from a natural cross a friend found.

## Favas: sow early

The classic fava mistake is to sow them with the summer beans. Lofthouse did it his first time: the plants flowered hard, aphids moved in, and no seed came. Fava flowers do not set seed in hot weather.

- **Sow as soon as the ground thaws.** The longer they flower in cool weather, the more seed you get. He often soaks the seed in water before sowing, so it comes up faster.
- **Mild winters: sow in autumn.** By his account favas are winter-hardy to about USDA zone 8, and he suggests autumn sowing where winters are that mild.

## Beans that shrug off frost

Lofthouse’s frost-tolerant common beans began with a late spring frost that killed most of his seedlings. About 5% lived. He sowed their seed a month early the next year, and kept doing so. Over the years his bean sowing has moved three to four weeks earlier. A small survival rate is plenty to start from.

## Hard seed

Some tepary beans have <Term id="hard-seed">hard seed</Term>: it will not take up water, and sprouts weeks or months late. Lofthouse soaked his seed before sowing and planted only the beans that swelled. That cleared the trait from his teparies.

## Saving the seed

Beans are a dry-seed crop, the easiest kind to save.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Dry on the plant">Leave the pods on until the plants are dry and the beans rattle.</Step>
  <Step title="Thresh">Pull the plants and <Term id="thresh">thresh</Term> them: crush the dry pods to free the beans.</Step>
  <Step title="Winnow">Pour the beans through a breeze to <Term id="winnow">winnow</Term> off the bits of pod.</Step>
  <Step title="Sort">Sort the jar by type, then choose: equal counts or bulk.</Step>
</Steps>

## Before you eat them

Dry beans and many other pulses are harmful unless properly prepared, and some, such as lupini, need special traditional preparation. Some people must avoid favas altogether.
