What should you do for your soil each season, and when do the compost, the mulch and the autumn leaves go down? This page is a list of jobs, drawn from the soil-care calendar Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis keep for their cold-winter gardens in Alaska. It follows the trees rather than the date: the year starts as buds swell and the last jobs come once the leaves are down, so it reads the same in either hemisphere. Each job links to the guide that explains how and why.

Tick each job as you do it. Nothing here needs doing all at once. If you are new to it, pick one or two jobs a season and add more each year as you see what your soil needs.

## Before you start: your climate

The authors warn that no calendar fits every garden. Hard cold stops compost tea working and freezes compost and mulch; in a drought, tea can be wasted, and mulch spread at the wrong moment can keep rain from reaching the soil.

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Where winters are mild or frost never comes, go by the same signs where you have them, new growth and falling leaves, and skip the jobs meant for frozen piles.

## Leaf-out

From the first swelling buds until the trees are in full leaf. Start a little before the buds open, while the beds are still bare: the authors look at their soil life first, so they can fill any gaps before they plant.

<Checklist id="leaf-out">
  <Item id="look-at-soil">Look at your soil life before buds open: [What's in your soil?](/guides/reading-your-soil/)</Item>
  <Item id="turn-and-start-piles">Turn last autumn's pile; start a fungal one and a bacterial one: [Compost that feeds soil life](/guides/compost-for-soil-life/)</Item>
  <Item id="mulch-back">Pull mulch back where soil needs warming, then replace and top up: [Mulch](/guides/mulch/)</Item>
  <Item id="refresh-brown">Refresh brown mulch under trees, shrubs and perennials, compost first: [Mulch](/guides/mulch/), [Trees like fungi, vegetables like bacteria](/guides/fungal-or-bacterial/)</Item>
  <Item id="sow-without-digging">Sow and plant without digging; green mulch once the soil is warm: [Vegetable beds without digging](/guides/no-dig-beds/)</Item>
  <Item id="inoculate" hint="the authors' habit; a need only if the bed was dug, sprayed or is new">Inoculate seeds and transplants (not cabbages, beets or spinach): [Mycorrhizal fungi](/guides/mycorrhizal-fungi/)</Item>
  <Item id="tea-non-food-only">Using compost tea? Only on plants nothing is eaten from; never fruit trees, berry bushes, edible perennials or vegetable beds, nor their soil: [Compost tea](/guides/compost-tea/)</Item>
</Checklist>

The authors drench the soil around their trees, shrubs and perennials with compost tea at this point in the year. This site draws a firmer line: keep it off food plants and the soil they grow in, so only ornamentals and trees that give you nothing to harvest.

## Summer

From full leaf until the first leaves turn. Vegetable beds and woody plants want different things: bacteria for the first, fungi for the second. Keep your notes from each look at the soil, so you can compare them after harvest.

<Checklist id="summer">
  <Item id="veg-green-mulch">Top up compost and green mulch on vegetable beds: [Mulch](/guides/mulch/)</Item>
  <Item id="woody-brown-mulch">Fungal compost and brown mulch around woody plants; leave dropped twigs: [Mulch](/guides/mulch/)</Item>
  <Item id="look-again">Look at your soil life again and note it: [What's in your soil?](/guides/reading-your-soil/)</Item>
</Checklist>

When a plant looks sick or stressed, the authors reach for compost tea at once. Read [its guide](/guides/compost-tea/) and its warnings before you do the same, and keep it to plants nothing is eaten from: never on food plants or the soil they grow in.

## Leaf-fall

From the first turning leaves until the trees are bare. The busiest season: the authors save every leaf they can, since brown material runs short in spring and summer.

<Checklist id="leaf-fall">
  <Item id="autumn-pile" hint="untreated lawns that dogs do not use">Gather fresh clippings for an autumn pile: [Compost that feeds soil life](/guides/compost-for-soil-life/)</Item>
  <Item id="inoculate-autumn" hint="new trees and shrubs, or soil that was dug or sprayed">Inoculate the roots of autumn plantings: [Mycorrhizal fungi](/guides/mycorrhizal-fungi/)</Item>
  <Item id="save-leaves">Chop lawn leaves in place; collect and store all the rest: [Mulch](/guides/mulch/)</Item>
  <Item id="mulch-all">Mulch every bed; after leaf-fall, the woody plants too: [Mulch](/guides/mulch/)</Item>
  <Item id="feed-before-winter">Feed vegetable beds before winter: [Vegetable beds without digging](/guides/no-dig-beds/)</Item>
  <Item id="compare">Check soil life after harvest and compare with spring: [What's in your soil?](/guides/reading-your-soil/)</Item>
</Checklist>

The authors feed vegetable beds with straw, clippings or alfalfa meal before winter, so soil life starts the spring well fed. Some of the fresh clippings can also go straight onto those beds as green mulch: clean clippings only (untreated, no dogs); see [Mulch](/guides/mulch/). Once the leaves are down, they drench the soil under trees and shrubs with compost tea; here, that means ornamental trees and shrubs only, never food plants or the soil under them.

## Winter

From bare branches until the buds swell again: the quiet season, with time to plan next year's beds.

{/* general tip, not from the book: "dry and covered" */}
<Checklist id="winter">
  <Item id="turn-if-thawed">Turn the pile a few times if it is not frozen: [Compost that feeds soil life](/guides/compost-for-soil-life/)</Item>
  <Item id="keep-leaves-dry">Keep stored leaves dry and covered: [Mulch](/guides/mulch/)</Item>
  <Item id="plan-beds">Mark each bed bacterial, balanced or fungal in your plan: [Trees like fungi, vegetables like bacteria](/guides/fungal-or-bacterial/)</Item>
</Checklist>

A Canopi design is a good place for that plan: note each bed's side, and next year you can see at a glance which compost and mulch each bed wants.
