Can you grow wheat, rye, barley or oats in a garden, without a combine? Yes. This guide covers when to sow, how to harvest and clean grain with a tarp and two buckets, which plants to keep seed from, and how to start a grain population of your own.

<QuickAnswer>
Grain needs only hand tools. Cut the heads, tread or beat them on a tarp, rake and sieve out the coarse bits, then pour the grain between two buckets in a breeze. Rye and many wheats can go in in autumn and grow on through the winter; in Joseph Lofthouse's garden, barley and oats do better sown in spring. Keep seed from plants that ripened early, stood upright and gave up their grain easily.
</QuickAnswer>

## Why grow grain

Few crops give so much food for so little equipment. Dry grain is rich in calories and keeps for a long time. Lofthouse puts the cost of a year of grain for himself at about a week of harvest work, plus perhaps another week of sowing and care. Those are his figures, from his own field; yours will depend on your soil, your tools and your pace.

Grain does not make a balanced diet on its own: it is short on vitamins. Grow it beside your vegetables, not in their place.

## Winter or spring sowing

Lofthouse's labels are practical. A "winter" grain, for him, is one he sowed in autumn that was still alive in spring. A "spring" grain is one he sowed in spring. Most of the wheats he grows work either way, though a few varieties may suit only one season.

Some grains will not flower until they have been through a cold spell, a need called <Term id="vernalisation">vernalisation</Term>. If you can only sow in spring, Lofthouse's way round it is to sow as early as the soil can be worked, so the last cold weather stands in for winter.

### Which grain in which season

- **Rye** is his safest autumn crop. By the time spring weeds come up, it is well ahead of them. In his garden, a few light passes with a rake in spring, while the weeds are still tiny seedlings, clear them; the rye shrugs it off.
- **Wheat**: many varieties come through his winters.
- **Oats and barley** are spring crops for him. Oats seldom survive his winters, and he thinks of barley as spring-sown.

## Harvest and clean grain by hand

You need shears to cut, a tarp and a stick (or your feet) to thresh, and two buckets plus a colander or coarse mesh to clean. Wear gloves and sturdy shoes. Use what you have: none of it has to be special.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Cut">Take a handful of heads in one hand and cut the stalks below them. Some grains let you snap the heads off with a tug.</Step>
  <Step title="Gather">Drop the heads onto the tarp as you go.</Step>
  <Step title="Thresh">To <Term id="thresh">thresh</Term>, tread on the pile or beat it with the stick until the grain is loose.</Step>
  <Step title="Clear">Rake the coarse <Term id="chaff">chaff</Term> off the top. If you like, <Term id="screen">screen</Term> what is left through the mesh or colander.</Step>
  <Step title="Winnow">To <Term id="winnow">winnow</Term>, stand in a breeze and pour slowly from one bucket into the other. The light chaff drifts off; the grain drops.</Step>
</Steps>

<Safety title="Gloves and shoes">Wear both while you harvest and thresh: grain bristles and chaff can pierce skin.</Safety>

Cutting by hand means the field does not have to ripen all at once. Take the first plants that are ready and come back for the rest. Dry the grain well before you put it away.

## Which plants to keep seed from

Each handful you set aside for sowing shapes next year's crop. These are the traits Lofthouse selects for, his first one first:

- **Early ripening.** Every extra week a crop stands in the field is another week of risk from birds, disease and bad weather.
- **Easy threshing.** Grain that falls free with little effort saves you work every year after.
- **Heads that hold their grain.** Heads prone to <Term id="shattering">shattering</Term> drop seed on the ground. Heads that hold it can wait in the field until you are ready.
- **Stalks that stay up.** Grain that falls over before harvest is said to show <Term id="lodging">lodging</Term>. He never keeps seed from a fallen plant.
- **Height around the waist.** A personal choice: he would rather not stoop, and taller grain shades out weeds.

Each grain has its own first goal in his garden. With oats, he wants <Term id="hull-less-grain">hull-less grain</Term> before anything else, since oats that keep their husks are hard to use; hardiness can wait. With barley, which grows short for him, he keeps only the tallest plants still standing after wind and watering.

### From a pinch of seed to a bag

Got only a few seeds of a variety? Space them about 30 cm (1 ft) apart. A plant with room makes many side shoots, or <Term id="tiller">tillers</Term>, each with its own head. Lofthouse has counted up to about 350 seeds from a single plant grown this way.

## Starting a grain landrace

Wheat and barley mostly pollinate themselves, so a grain landrace starts with many varieties in one field and lets the few natural crosses build up.

- **Gather varieties that already do well in your region.** Lofthouse started from about 16 each of wheat and barley known to suit the Rocky Mountains, plus a few of his own favourites.
- **Sow them mixed.** Jumbled together, they have the chance to cross. Both of his mixes did well sown in spring.
- **Save only what suits you.** Leave short, late and fallen plants out of the seed lot.
- **Watch for new types.** In later years, plants unlike any you sowed are the crosses.

He puts natural crossing in wheat and barley at roughly 10% in a dry climate, and lower in a damp one. He also noticed that the varieties that crossed most had their <Term id="anther">anthers</Term> hanging outside the husk. If you want more crossing, mark such plants at flowering and favour their seed.

### Autumn and spring lines

Grain that drops at harvest comes back on its own, so by his account his field is selecting for winter hardiness without his trying. He expects to end up with two <Term id="sister-lines">sister lines</Term>, one sown in autumn and one in spring.
