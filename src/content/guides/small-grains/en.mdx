Can you grow wheat, rye, barley or oats in a garden, without a combine? Yes. This guide covers when to sow, how to harvest and clean grain with a tarp and two buckets, which plants to keep seed from, and how to start a grain population of your own.

<QuickAnswer>
Grain needs only hand tools. Cut the heads, tread or beat them on a tarp, rake and sieve out the coarse bits, then pour the grain between two buckets in a breeze. Rye and many wheats can go in in autumn and grow on through the winter; in Joseph Lofthouse's garden, barley and oats do better sown in spring. Keep seed from plants that ripened early, stood upright and gave up their grain easily.
</QuickAnswer>

## Why grow grain

Grow grain beside your vegetables, not in their place: it is one part of a garden diet, not all of it. What it does give is stored energy. Dry grain is rich in calories, keeps for a long time and needs nothing but hand tools.

By Lofthouse's reckoning, his own year of grain costs him about a week of harvest, with perhaps another week of sowing and care; yours will depend on your soil, your tools and your pace.

## Winter or spring sowing

Should you sow in autumn or in spring? For most of the wheats Lofthouse grows, either will do, though a few varieties may suit only one season. Lofthouse uses these words in a practical sense: a "winter" grain, for him, is one he sowed in autumn that was still alive in spring, and a "spring" grain is simply one he sowed in spring.

Some grains will not flower until they have been through a cold spell, a need called <Term id="vernalisation">vernalisation</Term>. If you can only sow in spring, Lofthouse's way round it is to sow as early as the soil can be worked, so the last cold weather stands in for winter.

### Which grain in which season

- **Rye**, if you sow only one grain in autumn. Your one spring job is to rake the bed lightly now and then while the weed seedlings are tiny: the rake kills them and leaves the rye unharmed. For Lofthouse it is the surest autumn crop, already tall when the weeds wake up. He also counts rye as <Term id="allelopathy">allelopathic</Term>: in his account it gives off substances that hold other plants back.
- **Wheat**: many varieties come through his winters.
- **Oats and barley** are spring crops for him. Oats seldom survive his winters, and he thinks of barley as spring-sown.

## Harvest and clean grain by hand

You need shears to cut, a tarp and a stick (or your feet) to thresh, and two buckets plus a colander or coarse mesh to clean. Wear gloves and sturdy shoes. Use what you have: none of it has to be special.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Cut">Take a handful of heads in one hand and cut the stalks below your hand, with the blade pointing away from your fingers. Some grains let you snap the heads off with a tug.</Step>
  <Step title="Gather">Drop the heads onto the tarp as you go.</Step>
  <Step title="Thresh">To <Term id="thresh">thresh</Term>, tread on the pile or beat it with the stick until the grain is loose.</Step>
  <Step title="Clear">Rake the coarse <Term id="chaff">chaff</Term> off the top. If you like, <Term id="screen">screen</Term> what is left through the mesh or colander.</Step>
  <Step title="Winnow">To <Term id="winnow">winnow</Term>, stand in a breeze and pour slowly from one bucket into the other. The light chaff drifts off; the grain drops.</Step>
</Steps>

{/* general tip, not from the book: the dust advice in this box */}
<Safety title="Gloves, shoes and dust">Wear gloves and shoes while you harvest and thresh: grain bristles and chaff can pierce skin. Thresh and winnow outdoors, stand upwind of the falling chaff, and wear a dust mask if the dust bothers you.</Safety>

Cutting by hand means the field does not have to ripen all at once. Take the first plants that are ready and come back for the rest. Dry the grain well before you put it away.

## Which plants to keep seed from

Each handful you set aside for sowing shapes next year's crop. These are the traits Lofthouse selects for, his first one first:

- **Early ripening.** Every extra week a crop stands in the field is another week of risk from birds, disease and bad weather.
- **Easy threshing.** Grain that falls free with little effort saves you work every year after.
- **Heads that hold their grain.** Heads prone to <Term id="shattering">shattering</Term> drop seed on the ground. Heads that hold it can wait in the field until you are ready.
- **Stalks that stay up.** Grain that falls over before harvest is said to show <Term id="lodging">lodging</Term>. He never keeps seed from a fallen plant.
- **Height around the waist.** A personal choice: he would rather not stoop, and taller grain shades out weeds, though tall grain is said to lodge more easily.

Each grain has its own first goal in his garden. With oats, he wants <Term id="hull-less-grain">hull-less grain</Term> before anything else, since oats that keep their husks are hard to use; hardiness can wait. With barley, which grows short for him, he keeps only the tallest plants still standing after wind and watering.

### From a pinch of seed to a bag

Got only a few seeds of a variety? Give each plant plenty of room: it will make many side shoots, or <Term id="tiller">tillers</Term>, each with its own head. Lofthouse spaces such plants about 30 cm (1 ft) apart, and he reports up to about 350 seeds from a single plant grown this way.

## Starting a grain landrace

How do you breed a crop that mostly pollinates itself? With numbers. Wheat and barley cross only now and then, so you sow many varieties side by side and let the rare crosses add up over the years. Lofthouse puts natural crossing at roughly 10% in a dry climate and lower in a damp one, and began with about 16 varieties of each grain known to suit his mountains.

- **Mix the seed before you sow.** A jumbled bed gives every variety neighbours to cross with. Start from grains that already grow well in your region.
- **Mark the open flowers.** At flowering, look for heads with their <Term id="anther">anthers</Term> hanging outside the husk: in his field, those varieties crossed most. Lean on their seed if you want more crossing.
- **Look for the odd ones out.** In the years that follow, a plant unlike anything you sowed is likely a cross.
- **Keep only what suits you.** Short, late and fallen plants stay out of the seed lot.

### Autumn and spring lines

Grain that drops at harvest comes back on its own, so by his account his field is selecting for winter hardiness without his trying. He expects to end up with two <Term id="sister-lines">sister lines</Term>, one sown in autumn and one in spring.

### Perennial grains

Lofthouse also keeps a few small plots of perennial rye and wheat, which are said to descend from crosses with wild grasses. He first selected them for winter hardiness and now selects for easy threshing. The grower who gave him the seed warns that a perennial stand is less carefree than it sounds. Left alone, it turns into a grassland that crowds the grain out, so it needs some disturbance every year, such as mowing or grazing, with the dead stalks knocked down. A stand that only sows itself also drifts back toward the wild, so keep sowing your chosen seed into it.

## Before you eat or mill grain

Lofthouse holds strong views on eating grain. He believes its anti-nutrients harmed people's health when societies turned to grain farming, and that some recent varieties and harvest methods give many people mild allergies. He also prefers food whose species he can tell just by looking at it. These are his views.

<Safety title="Traditional preparations have their own risks">
To lower those anti-nutrients, he favours whole grain, sourdough, and grain that is sprouted or fermented before it is cooked. Sprouts and home ferments can grow harmful bacteria and moulds, and seed kept for sowing may have been treated. Follow your food-safety authority rather than this summary.
</Safety>
