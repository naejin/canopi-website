Most flowering plants cannot make seed unless an animal carries their pollen, and the animals that do it, mostly insects, are your garden's <Term id="pollinator">pollinators</Term>. Many of the same insects spend their young lives eating aphids and caterpillars: they are <Term id="natural-enemy">natural enemies</Term> of your pests. This guide follows Kim Eierman's *The Pollinator Victory Garden*, written for North American gardens. Wild pollinators are declining, she writes, mainly from pesticides, lawns, lost habitat and a changing climate that puts flowers and insects out of step.

<QuickAnswer>
Grow many kinds of flowers that open one after another through the season, most of them wild plants of your own region. Leave places to nest, such as bare sunny ground, dead stems and old wood; put out water; and use no pesticides, organic ones included. The same garden feeds bees and the hoverflies, wasps and beetles whose young eat aphids and caterpillars.
</QuickAnswer>

In Canopi's [consortium designer](/consortium/), the **Brings helpers** job points to this guide: it marks plants whose flowers feed these animals. Those marks come from Canopi's plant data, not from this guide, so check any plant against your local invasive list before you plant it.

## Why it matters

A bee does not visit a flower to help it. It comes to feed, and pollen rides on its body to the next flower. Without those visits many crops give less fruit, and poorer fruit. In a United Nations figure Eierman cites, 84% of the crops people grow for food need pollinators for a bigger or better harvest, and bees do most of that work.

Some crops need a special touch. Bumble bees and several other wild bees shiver their flight muscles as they work a flower, shaking the pollen loose: this is <Term id="buzz-pollination">buzz pollination</Term>. Tomatoes, peppers and blueberries gain from it; honey bees can pollinate these flowers too, but far less well. Wild bees also improve apple crops: in an estimate the author cites, a few hundred females of a North American mason bee can pollinate as much apple orchard as more than ten thousand honey bees.

More bees also means more crossing between varieties of one crop: read [Will they cross?](/guides/will-they-cross/) before you save seed, and [Tomatoes that cross](/guides/tomatoes/) for a crop where that crossing is the aim.

<Points>
- **Bees** Most bees live alone, not in hives: the female of a <Term id="solitary-bee">solitary bee</Term> makes and stocks her own nest. Many fly as adults for only a few weeks, and the small ones forage only a short way from home. Bumble bees, which live in small colonies, and the honey bee are the exceptions.
- **Hoverflies** They look like small bees or wasps but have one pair of wings, not two. The adults pollinate; the larvae of most kinds eat aphids.
- **Beetles** Among the oldest pollinators: slow, fond of pollen, and guided by scent more than colour. Lady beetle larvae eat aphids.
- **Wasps** Poor pollinators, with few hairs to catch pollen, but strong hunters that feed insects to their young. <Term id="parasitoid">Parasitoid</Term> wasps lay their eggs in or on other insects, which the larvae then eat.
- **Butterflies and moths** The adults drink nectar; the caterpillars eat leaves, often of only a few plants. Most moths feed at night; a few, like the hummingbird moths, fly by day.
- **Nectar birds** Which ones come depends on where you live; the book names hummingbirds, sunbirds, honeyeaters, spiderhunters and some parrots. Most garden birds also feed insects to their chicks, pests included.
- **Bats** In warm regions only: see [Nectar bats](#nectar-bats-in-warm-regions) below.
</Points>

Most of these visitors are <Term id="generalist-pollinator">generalists</Term> that feed on many kinds of flowers. A <Term id="specialist-pollinator">specialist</Term> depends on one plant or a small group of plants, and the plant may depend on it in turn: the author's example is a North American bee that collects pollen only from two spring beauties. The Brazil nut is another case, setting nuts only where its wild orchid bees thrive: see [Nuts as a staple](/guides/nut-staples/).

<Safety title="Sting allergies">
Most bees are calm and sting only to defend their nest, Eierman notes. {/* general tip, not from the book */}A bee at a flower is out feeding. Move slowly around bees and wasps rather than swatting at them. If anyone in your household is allergic to stings, ask a doctor what to keep at hand before you invite more of them in. Where to put nests safely: [Nests and shelter](/guides/homes-for-pollinators/).
</Safety>

## Honey bees are one bee among thousands

The western (European) honey bee is one managed, social species among the many thousands of bees in the world. You can help it without keeping a hive: grow flowers it can use, and spray nothing.

Where hives stand nearby, honey bees and wild bees compete for the same flowers. Eierman's answer is to plant more, and to plant flowering trees and shrubs, which carry the most flowers per plant. Watch too for a <Term id="nectar-dearth">nectar dearth</Term>, a hot, dry spell when flowers stop giving nectar: plants that flower through the summer carry bees over it. [Flowers all season](/guides/flowers-for-pollinators/) shows how to fill those gaps.

## What every helper needs

Wherever you garden, pollinators and pest-eaters need four things:

- **Food**: nectar for energy and pollen for protein, from flowers open at different times; caterpillars need the leaves of their own host plants.
- **Water**: clean and shallow, with somewhere to stand.
- **A place to nest or lay eggs**: bare ground, hollow or pithy stems, old wood, the right plants.
- **Shelter**: from wind, cold and heat, and a quiet place for the part of the year they are not active.

Flowers alone are not enough. A bed full of bloom feeds the animals passing through, the author points out, but those that find nowhere to nest will raise their young somewhere else.

Natural enemies need prey, too. Hoverfly and lady beetle larvae live on aphids, so a garden with none keeps none of their hunters. A few aphids and some chewed leaves are part of the bargain.

For food, see [Flowers all season](/guides/flowers-for-pollinators/); for nests, shelter and water, [Nests and shelter](/guides/homes-for-pollinators/); for the lawn, [From lawn to flowers](/guides/lawn-to-flowers/).

## No pesticides, organic ones included

A spray meant for one pest does not choose what it hits. Many pesticides kill bees, and some that do not still harm them, Eierman warns. When something is eating your plants, go through these questions in order before anything goes on a leaf:

1. **Is it a pest at all?** The caterpillar on a chewed leaf may be one you will later see as a butterfly. The author's other example is a leafcutter bee, which cuts pieces from leaves to build her nest. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Watch it for a while first.
2. **Can the garden deal with it?** Aphids are food for the hoverfly and lady beetle larvae drawn in by your flowers, and for the chicks of insect-eating birds. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Give these hunters time to arrive before you act.
3. **Is the plant the real problem?** A plant that keeps falling ill may be in the wrong spot for it, or may have arrived ill. Eierman's answer is plants that are healthy when you buy them and suited to where they grow, and infected leaves picked up before the trouble spreads. For plants that cope with your pests over the years, see [Pests and diseases](/guides/pests-without-sprays/).
4. **Still losing the plant?** Read the box below before you buy anything.

<Safety title="Even organic sprays">
Organic and plant-based pesticides are not harmless: some of them kill bees too. If you still must spray, Eierman rules out synthetic products: her last resort is an organic one aimed at the single pest you are treating, used as little as you can. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Never spray open flowers, where pollinators feed. The plants that some of these sprays are made from carry their own warnings: see [Pesticide plants](/guides/fibre-dye-and-soap/#pesticide-plants).
</Safety>

Watch what you bring into the garden, too. A <Term id="systemic-pesticide">systemic pesticide</Term> is drawn into the whole plant rather than staying on its surface, and bees meet it later in the nectar and pollen. It stays in the plant for months, she warns, and can spread to untreated plants nearby. Before you take a plant home, ask the seller whether it was treated.

Sprays also travel: drift from treated lawns has killed many pollinators, Eierman notes. Keep any lawn you have unsprayed.

## Nectar bats in warm regions

<ForClimate zones={["tropical", "subtropical", "arid"]} source="pollinator-victory-garden">
For warm regions and deserts, where nectar-feeding bats live; the book writes from North America.

Bat flowers open at night. They are large, pale or dull in colour and strongly scented, with plenty of pollen and abundant, watery nectar. Eierman counts bananas, mangoes and guavas among the fruit that bats pollinate. In the deserts of Mexico and the southwestern United States, agaves and tall columnar cacti feed migrating bats, and cutting agave flower stalks for tequila production takes that food away, Eierman notes.
