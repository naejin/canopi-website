Most flowering plants cannot make seed unless an animal carries their pollen, and the animals that do it, mostly insects, are your garden's <Term id="pollinator">pollinators</Term>. Many of the same insects spend their young lives eating aphids and caterpillars: they are <Term id="natural-enemy">natural enemies</Term> of your pests. This guide follows Kim Eierman's *The Pollinator Victory Garden*, written for North American gardens. Wild pollinators are declining, she writes, mainly from lost habitat, lawns, pesticides and a changing climate that puts flowers and insects out of step.

<QuickAnswer>
Grow many kinds of flowers that open one after another through the season, most of them wild plants of your own region. Leave places to nest, such as bare sunny ground, dead stems and old wood; put out water; and use no pesticides, organic ones included. The same garden feeds bees and the hoverflies, wasps and beetles whose young eat aphids and caterpillars.
</QuickAnswer>

In Canopi's [consortium designer](/consortium/), the **Brings helpers** job points to this guide: it marks plants whose flowers feed these animals. Those marks come from Canopi's plant data, not from this guide, so check any plant against your local invasive list before you plant it.

## Why it matters

A bee does not visit a flower to help it. It comes to feed, and pollen rides on its body to the next flower. Without those visits many crops set fewer fruit, or smaller and misshapen ones. In a United Nations figure Eierman cites, 84% of the crops people grow for food need pollinators for a bigger or better harvest, and bees do most of that work.

Some crops need a special touch. Bumble bees and several other wild bees grip the flower and shiver their flight muscles, shaking the pollen loose: this is <Term id="buzz-pollination">buzz pollination</Term>. Tomatoes, peppers and blueberries gain from it; honey bees visit these flowers too, but pollinate them far less well. Wild bees also improve apple crops, and in an estimate the author cites, a few hundred female mason bees can pollinate as much orchard as many thousands of honey bees.

More bees also means more crossing between varieties of one crop: read [Will they cross?](/guides/will-they-cross/) before you save seed, and [Tomatoes that cross](/guides/tomatoes/) for a crop where that crossing is the aim.

<Points>
- **Bees** Most bees live alone, not in hives: the female of a <Term id="solitary-bee">solitary bee</Term> makes and stocks her own nest. Many fly as adults for only a few weeks, and the small ones forage only a short way from home. Bumble bees, which live in small colonies, and the honey bee are the exceptions.
- **Hoverflies** They look like small bees or wasps but have one pair of wings, not two. The adults pollinate; the larvae of most kinds eat aphids.
- **Beetles** The oldest pollinators: slow, fond of pollen, and guided by scent more than colour. Lady beetles, and their larvae even more, eat aphids.
- **Wasps** Poor pollinators, with few hairs to catch pollen, but strong hunters that feed insects to their young. <Term id="parasitoid">Parasitoid</Term> wasps lay their eggs in or on other insects, which the larvae then eat.
- **Butterflies and moths** The adults drink nectar; the caterpillars eat leaves, often of only a few plants. Most moths feed at night; a few, like the hummingbird moths, fly by day.
- **Nectar birds** They differ by continent: hummingbirds in the Americas, sunbirds, honeyeaters, spiderhunters and some parrots elsewhere. Most garden birds also feed insects to their chicks, pests included.
- **Bats** In warm regions only: see [Nectar bats](#nectar-bats-in-warm-regions) below.
</Points>

Most of these visitors are <Term id="generalist-pollinator">generalists</Term> that feed on many kinds of flowers. A <Term id="specialist-pollinator">specialist</Term> depends on one plant or a small group of plants, and the plant may depend on it in turn: the author's example is a North American bee that collects pollen only from two spring beauties. The Brazil nut is another case, setting nuts only where its wild orchid bees thrive: see [Nuts as a staple](/guides/nut-staples/).

<Safety title="Sting allergies">
Most bees are calm and sting only when their nest is threatened, Eierman notes. Social wasps such as paper wasps and yellow jackets live in colonies, in the ground or in nests they build. {/* general tip, not from the book */}If anyone in your household is allergic to stings, ask a doctor what to keep at hand, and keep nest sites and the busiest flowers away from doors, paths and play areas. More on nests: [Nests and shelter](/guides/homes-for-pollinators/).
</Safety>

## Honey bees are one bee among thousands

The honey bee is a single managed, social species among the many thousands of bees in the world. You can help it without keeping a hive: grow flowers it can use, and spray nothing.

Where hives stand nearby, honey bees and wild bees compete for the same flowers. Eierman's answer is to plant more, and to plant flowering trees and shrubs, which carry the most flowers per plant. Watch too for a <Term id="nectar-dearth">nectar dearth</Term>, a hot, dry spell when flowers stop giving nectar: plants that flower through the summer carry bees over it. [Flowers all season](/guides/flowers-for-pollinators/) shows how to fill those gaps.

## What every helper needs

Wherever you garden, pollinators and pest-eaters need four things:

- **Food**: nectar for energy and pollen for protein, from flowers open at different times; caterpillars need the leaves of their own host plants.
- **Water**: clean and shallow, with somewhere to stand.
- **A place to nest or lay eggs**: bare ground, hollow or pithy stems, old wood, the right plants.
- **Shelter**: from wind, cold and heat, and a quiet place for the part of the year they are not active.

Flowers alone are not enough. A bed full of bloom feeds the animals passing through, the author points out, but those that find nowhere to nest will raise their young somewhere else.

Natural enemies need prey, too. Hoverfly and lady beetle larvae live on aphids, so a garden with none keeps none of their hunters. A few aphids and some chewed leaves are part of the bargain.

For food, see [Flowers all season](/guides/flowers-for-pollinators/); for nests, shelter and water, [Nests and shelter](/guides/homes-for-pollinators/); for the lawn, [From lawn to flowers](/guides/lawn-to-flowers/).

## No pesticides, organic ones included

Many pesticides kill bees and other pollinators outright. Others leave them alive but weakened, less able to fly, feed or breed.

The worst are <Term id="systemic-pesticide">systemic pesticides</Term>. The plant takes them up into its tissues, so they end up in its nectar and pollen, can last for months, and can even reach untreated plants nearby. Bought plants may carry them: ask whoever sells you plants whether they were treated.

Before you treat anything, check that there is a problem. A hole in a leaf may be a caterpillar on its host plant, or a leafcutter bee taking pieces for her nest. A healthy garden is not a spotless one.

What Eierman does instead is ordinary gardening: healthy plants in places that suit them, diseased leaves cleared away so the trouble does not spread, natural enemies and insect-eating birds drawn in by what you plant, and a tolerance for some damage. To grow plants that cope with your pests over the years, see [Pests and diseases](/guides/pests-without-sprays/).

<Safety title="Even organic sprays">
Organic and plant-based pesticides are not harmless: some of them kill bees too. If a spray seems unavoidable, the author's fallback is an organic product that acts only on the pest you have, used sparingly. {/* general tip, not from the book */}Never spray open flowers, where pollinators feed. The plants that some of these sprays are made from carry their own warnings: see [Pesticide plants](/guides/fibre-dye-and-soap/#pesticide-plants).
</Safety>

Spray drifts, too: drift from treated lawns has killed many pollinators, she warns. Keep any lawn you have unsprayed.

## Nectar bats in warm regions

<ForClimate zones={["tropical", "subtropical", "arid"]} source="pollinator-victory-garden">
For warm regions and deserts, where nectar-feeding bats live; the book writes from North America.

Bat flowers open at night. They are large, pale or dull in colour and strongly scented, with plenty of pollen and abundant, watery nectar. Eierman counts bananas, mangoes and guavas among the fruit that bats pollinate. In deserts, agaves and tall columnar cacti feed bats on their migrations, and cutting the agaves' flower stalks takes that food away.
