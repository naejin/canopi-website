Can you grow vegetables without digging? Should the tiller go? How do you sow, feed and weed a bed you never turn? Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis leave their established beds undug: they plant through the surface, feed from the top and let soil life do the mixing. This guide is the vegetable-bed side of their method, <Term id="no-dig">no-dig</Term> growing; the bigger picture is in [Living soil](/guides/living-soil/).

<QuickAnswer>
The authors stop digging established beds: tilling shreds fungal threads, wrecks worm tunnels and leaves a fluffy soil that slumps at the first watering. Open only the spot each seed or plant needs, with a dibber or a scratch along the row, and cover it with compost. Feed from the top: compost, then a green mulch such as dried grass clippings or straw, and more organic matter at leaf-fall. Mulch before weeds come up; the weed seeds already in the soil stay in the dark once you stop turning it.
</QuickAnswer>

## Why the authors stopped digging

In their view, tilling and heavy disturbance destroy or badly damage the <Term id="soil-food-web">soil food web</Term>, and an established bed should never be tilled again. What a tiller does, in their account:

<Points>

- **The soil slumps.** The fluffy look does not last: water settles tilled soil, and each soaking packs it tighter.
- **Worms are cut up.** The pieces never grow back into whole worms.
- **Tunnels and pores collapse.** The paths that air and water took are gone.
- **Fungi are shredded.** Fungal <Term id="hyphae">hyphae</Term> run even through soils where bacteria lead, and a tiller chops them up, root partners included ([Mycorrhizal fungi](/guides/mycorrhizal-fungi/)).

</Points>

Vegetables like a soil run mostly by bacteria ([Trees like fungi, vegetables like bacteria](/guides/fungal-or-bacterial/)), but even that soil needs some fungi to hold its crumbs together. The one time the authors accept breaking ground is to turn fungal soil, such as old woodland, into a vegetable bed. Many organic gardeners still dig or double-dig to work in organic matter; the authors call the habit outdated. What they promise instead: no more turning the soil, and less watering and feeding once soil life is back.

## Sowing and planting without digging

- **One hole per plant.** Open it with a trowel, a length of dowel or a <Term id="dibber">dibber</Term>.
- **A scratch for a row.** Run a hoe, or a board's edge, lightly along the line, sow into that shallow groove and cover it with a compost rich in bacteria ([Compost that feeds soil life](/guides/compost-for-soil-life/)).
- **Fewer weeds.** Most weed seeds need light to sprout, and in an undug bed they stay buried.
- **Feet on the paths.** Keep to fixed paths so the soil you grow in is never packed down.

## Feeding from the top

<Points>

- **Compost on top.** Spread bacterial compost around plants and between rows whenever you can, and again before winter. A thin layer is enough: see [Compost that feeds soil life](/guides/compost-for-soil-life/).
- **Green mulch over it.** Fresh, sugary material feeds bacteria, and the finer and damper it is, the faster. Dried grass clippings and straw still count as green, the authors say, because they keep their sugars. Use clippings only from lawns never treated with weedkiller or pesticide and not used by dogs ([Mulch](/guides/mulch/)).
- **Microbe food at planting.** The authors put an organic feed with none of its three NPK numbers above 10 in the hole or along the row, then mulch.
- **An autumn feed.** At leaf-fall they add alfalfa meal, straw or clippings. Bacteria hold on to the nitrogen over winter (<Term id="nitrogen-tie-up">nitrogen tie-up</Term>) and, in the authors' view, release it before spring planting. Then mulch the beds, so soil life keeps working through the winter.

</Points>

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Keep compost on the soil rather than on leaves you will eat, and wash what you harvest.

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Dry meals are dusty: spread them outdoors and do not breathe the dust.

Damp mulch is good; wet mulch is not. If it smells bad, it has turned <Term id="anaerobic">anaerobic</Term>: fluff it up and water less.

The authors also drench and spray their beds with brewed liquids, compost tea and a protozoa brew. This guide gives no recipe, rate or timing: read [Compost tea](/guides/compost-tea/) first.

This site's line: no compost tea on vegetable beds, neither on the leaves nor on the soil.

## Root partners

The authors add root fungi (<Term id="mycorrhiza">mycorrhizae</Term>) to vegetable seed at sowing, or to the roots at transplanting; in undug, unsprayed beds that is their habit, not a need. Skip the cabbage family and the beet and spinach group: they form no partnership.

Peas, beans and other legumes house <Term id="rhizobia">rhizobia</Term>, bacteria that fix nitrogen in nodules on their roots. The authors call a bought legume <Term id="inoculant">inoculant</Term>, put on the seed or roots, a fast and effective tool.

## Weeds without weedkiller

Mulch comes first. The authors lay 5–7.5 cm (2–3 in) of a green, bacteria-feeding mulch before weeds appear, leaving a little bare soil around each stem. In their view it works three ways: no light, a physical barrier, and nutrients locked up by microbes in the thin layer where mulch meets soil, which is where shallow weed roots feed and crop roots do not.

High-nitrogen fertiliser, they say, feeds fast annual weeds and kills the crop's root fungi. Once they gave up high-nitrate feeding and tilling, chickweed, their worst weed, vanished from their beds.

For what still comes up, the authors use careful hoeing, strong vinegar, boiling water, heat such as a flame weeder, and corn gluten, which they say stops weed seedlings from rooting.

<Safety title="Vinegar, scalding water and flame can hurt you">
Strong vinegar burns skin and eyes; scalding water splashes and burns; a flame weeder can set dry mulch, straw, hedges and clothing alight. Protect your skin and eyes, keep children and pets away, and never use a flame near dry mulch.
</Safety>

If a weedkiller is ever used, the authors follow it with compost, mulch and tea to bring soil life back. We leave the tea out: on a vegetable bed it would reach the soil your food grows in.

## Worms

Once chemicals stop, worms come back. A few centimetres of bacterial compost as the leaves start to turn helps draw them. If they still stay away, the authors read it as too few bacteria and protozoa: keep adding compost, then bring in worms to hurry things along. Count them with the [dig-and-sort test](/guides/reading-your-soil/#dig-and-sort).

Never empty leftover fishing worms into woodland: the authors report that worms dumped by anglers now eat through the leaf litter of some forests in the United States and Canada too fast for the trees.

## Bringing a chemical bed back

After years of salt fertilisers, weedkillers or pesticides, the authors say the web may need rebuilding from scratch, which can take a year or more. Beds of annuals respond fast, and gardens long kept organic need only small adjustments. Their plan, by stage:

<Steps>
<Step title="Before planting">

Spread 2.5–5 cm (1–2 in) of bacterial compost.

</Step>
<Step title="At sowing or planting">

Treat seeds and seedlings with root fungi, except the cabbage and beet families.

</Step>
<Step title="After planting">

Lay a green mulch.

</Step>
<Step title="Through the season">

Top up compost and mulch, and keep off the beds.

</Step>
<Step title="After harvest and at leaf-fall">

Compost, the autumn feed and mulch, as in Feeding from the top.

</Step>
</Steps>

Their plan also has regular compost tea on the beds and on the crops. We leave that out: see the box in Feeding from the top and [Compost tea](/guides/compost-tea/).

Most garden insects help, and the authors read a pest outbreak as a sign the soil web is out of balance: see [Pests and diseases](/guides/pests-without-sprays/).
