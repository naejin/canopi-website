A row of beans that returns each year, or a tree whose pods feed you and your goats: this guide follows Eric Toensmeier's *The Carbon Farming Solution* through both. The author does not rank beans among the best perennial staples for most climates: some are poisonous, and many were never properly domesticated.

<QuickAnswer>
Perennial beans exist for most climates: runner and lima beans that die back and resprout, perennial pigeon pea cut back after each harvest, and a few tree beans. Trees such as carob, honey locust <Invasive /> and mesquite <Invasive /> bear sweet, woody pods, ground whole into flour or left for livestock; plant two varieties so some trees crop each year. Honey locust and mesquite are invasive in many places, and several beans in this family are poisonous or need processing: read [the cautions](#beans-that-need-care) first.
</QuickAnswer>

## Beans that come back

Runner and lima beans both have climbing kinds that live for years. When winter or the dry months come, they die back to the ground and resprout from the root. Lima stands drought better; runner lives longer. On a California farm the author saw runner beans still bearing well after decades.

Their weakness is labour: a trellis and hand picking, which is why few people have bred them. Toensmeier floats an untested fix: vines climbing shrubs that are cut back regularly, so the support renews itself.

All of them are <Term id="nitrogen-fixer">nitrogen fixers</Term>. A wild relative from dry highlands, perennial tepary, looks very promising to the author.

For these species as annuals, and how runner beans cross, see [Beans and other legumes](/guides/beans-and-legumes/#many-species-many-seasons).

## Shrub and tree beans

**Perennial pigeon pea** is a short-lived shrub that gives dry peas for a few years. Growers often cut it back hard after each harvest to renew it, a form of <Term id="coppice">coppice</Term>, and early-flowering varieties take the cut best; then you replant. Perennial kinds are hard to find outside India, where they have been garden plants for millennia. It also gives shade, shelter from wind and, cut and carried to animals, a <Term id="fodder-bank">fodder bank</Term>.

**Chachafruto**, an Andean tree, is the only *Erythrina* whose large, soft beans are eaten. It bears through most of the year, so a few trees keep a household supplied almost constantly, according to a paper the book cites. It grows from seed or <Term id="live-stake">live stakes</Term>, and doubles as a <Term id="living-fence">living fence</Term> and fodder. Below its altitude range it fails: a Mexican farm the book follows tried repeatedly.

**Wattleseed acacias** from Australia often bear in the year they are planted. A hard cut back revives them when crops slow, and yields firewood.

## Pods from trees

Some legume trees bear woody pods made to be eaten by big animals. The flesh is mostly sugar, not starch; the seeds hold the protein but are too hard to eat whole, so pods are ground whole, seeds included, into flour or meal.

Most pods go to livestock: carob has fed animals around the Mediterranean since ancient times, and mesquite <Invasive /> pods feed them in dry regions on several continents.

Honey locust <Invasive /> shows why details matter. Cattle were once thought unable to digest its seeds without grinding the pods first; newer research found they digest almost all of them, so pods can drop into the pasture and be grazed where they lie, a form of <Term id="silvopasture">silvopasture</Term>. Sheep cannot break some seed coats, though. See [Feeding animals from trees](/guides/trees-for-livestock/).

Many pod trees swing between heavy and light years: <Term id="alternate-bearing">alternate bearing</Term>. For honey locust, the book suggests complementary varieties, so some trees bear every year; mesquites, by contrast, it counts on to bear annually.

Choose sweet-podded <Term id="graft">grafted</Term> varieties; a grafted honey locust can bear in about three years, Toensmeier reports, though many grafts fail.

Carob and honey locust form no root nodules and fix little or no nitrogen, the book says.

## Thorns, spread and livestock

Mesquite <Invasive /> thrives on poor, dry, salty ground, fixes its own nitrogen, and spreads in the manure of animals that eat its pods. It is a notorious pasture weed, listed as invasive or noxious in many regions, and the mesquites with the best pods are the thorniest and most aggressive.

Honey locust <Invasive /> is a common ornamental, invasive outside its native North America, and wild trees carry fierce spines. So start with the pod trees and beans native to your region, as the book advises, and check your local invasive list before planting either tree.

Where mesquite <Invasive /> already grows wild, the book reports two ways of living with it: farmers in India and Africa thin overgrown stands into grazing land with trees, and US ranchers find that planned rotational grazing stops new seedlings, which cattle trample and eat. In dry places, it adds, mesquite <Invasive /> makes a poor tree between crop rows, competing with them for water.

The book also warns that livestock fed on nothing but mesquite <Invasive /> pods for long periods can fall ill and occasionally die: see [Feeding animals from trees](/guides/trees-for-livestock/) before you rely on them.

<Safety title="Spines">
Mesquites <Invasive /> and wild honey locusts <Invasive /> carry long, hard spines. Wear thick gloves when pruning them, and keep them away from paths and play areas.
</Safety>

## Beans that need care

<Points ruled="accent">
- **Every dry bean here.** Pigeon pea, runner, lima, tepary, thicket bean, chachafruto, wattleseed and food lablab: never eaten raw, like any dry bean. See [Cooking dry beans](/guides/cooking-dry-beans/#why-raw-or-undercooked-seeds-are-dangerous).
- **Sword bean.** Its seeds carry a cell-damaging toxin and are eaten only after careful traditional processing, not described here.
- **Lablab.** Some forms grown for fodder or green manure are poisonous to people. Use only seed sold as a food variety.
- **Other *Erythrina* trees.** Most have small, poisonous beans; chachafruto is the exception. See [Which ones are dangerous](/guides/trees-with-edible-leaves/#which-ones-are-dangerous).
- **Perennial lupins and hairy vetch.** The book flags their seed as poisonous.
- **African locust bean.** Poisonous as harvested; eaten only after traditional processing. Do not try it without someone who knows how.
- **Siberian pea shrub.** Canadian farmers have eaten its bitter seeds as a famine food after processing; the author does not count them as edible, and neither do we.
</Points>

## Pick for your climate

The book sorts climates by winter cold and rainfall, and calls this a rough start. Look first for native beans and pod trees.

<ForClimate zones="tropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Hot, frost-free places, from humid to those with a long dry season.

- **Perennial pigeon pea**: from dry to humid tropics; cut back after each harvest and replace every few years.
- **Climbing lima bean**: for warm places too dry for runner beans; long-lived African strains exist.
- **Lablab**: a vegetable and dry bean across India and Southeast Asia, and fodder under coconut and oil palm. Food varieties only (see above).
- **African locust bean**: a tree of the dry savanna <Term id="parkland">parklands</Term>, whose pods ripen in the dry season when little else does. See the caution above.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="subtropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Mild winters with light frost.

- **Perennial pigeon pea** reaches the subtropics too.
- **Carob**: the book gives it the dry subtropics as well as the Mediterranean.

For the tropical highlands (the book's own category):

- **Chachafruto**: only in humid highlands, between about 1,000 and 3,000 m (3,300 and 9,800 ft) in the book; below that it fails.
- **Runner bean**: at home in the mountains of Mexico and Central America, where it comes from.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="mediterranean" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Winter rain and a dry summer.

- **Carob**: a windbreak and pod tree, traditionally among olives, vines and almonds; it needs at least about 350 mm (14 in) of rain a year to set pods, the book says.
- **Honey locust** <Invasive />: also does well in Mediterranean climates. Choose grafted sweet-podded varieties.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="arid" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Deserts and dry steppe: the book's "arid" climates.

- **Mesquite** <Invasive />: the book's richest pod tree for deserts, bearing every year on very poor, salty soil. Plant it only where it is native.
- **Wattleseed acacias**: Australian dryland shrubs that bear in the driest farmed places and make good windbreaks; one did well in trials in Niger.
- **Perennial tepary**: a wild bean of dry highlands, still to be developed as a crop.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="temperate" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "warm temperate", which takes in cool-summer places such as southern Britain.

- **Runner bean**: the book's perennial bean for mild, cool climates.
- **Honey locust** <Invasive />: let the pods fall into a pasture, and plant two varieties that complement each other.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="continental" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "cold temperate": long, hard frosts.

- **Honey locust** <Invasive />: sweet-podded varieties come through hard winters; the papery-podded ornamentals are not the crop.
- **Thicket bean**: once grown by Native Americans; reliably perennial in the author's cold garden, but not yet a dependable crop.
- **Siberian pea shrub**: a very hardy windbreak and nitrogen fixer for shelter, fodder and soil, not food (see above).
</ForClimate>
