Where winters are hard or summers dry, a tree that feeds you every day is usually a nut tree; in the tropics, the breadnuts join them. This guide follows Eric Toensmeier's *The Carbon Farming Solution* and treats nuts as everyday food rather than a treat.

<QuickAnswer>
Nuts are among the strongest perennial staples, in Toensmeier's view, with candidates for almost every climate: chestnuts, some acorns and breadnuts are starchy like grain; walnuts, hazels and pine nuts are rich in oil and protein. Mix grafted trees from proven local selections with diverse seedlings, favour kinds that crop every year, and grow hazel as a coppiced hedge. Most acorns, ginkgo and the mulberry-family nuts are not eaten as picked, and cashew shells burn: read [the cautions](#nuts-that-need-care) first.
</QuickAnswer>

## Can nuts be a staple?

In the book, a nut is any edible hard-shelled seed of a woody plant. There are three groups:

<Points>
- **Starchy nuts**, eaten the way grain or potatoes are: chestnut, acorns and the breadnuts.
- **Oil-and-protein nuts**: walnut, hazel, pistachio, pine nuts, cashew and Brazil nut.
- **Oily nuts**: pecan and macadamia.
</Points>

Toensmeier ranks nuts among the strongest groups of perennial <Term id="staple-crop">staples</Term>; in cold climates, where few other tree crops fill that role, they are most of what there is. For the other groups, see [Staple foods from perennials](/guides/perennial-staples/).

## Seedlings or grafts

A <Term id="graft">grafted</Term> tree is a copy of one proven tree, so you know what it will bear. A seedling is new, and no two are alike.

Grafts give you predictable nuts and, in some crops, an earlier start: the newer pecan varieties, and walnuts that bear along their side shoots, come into crop sooner, the book reports. Farmers graft their own best trees too, from Spain's sweet-acorn oaks to a Mexican farm's cloud-forest oaks. Oak grafts do not always take, though, the book warns.

Seedlings cost less and vary. A mixed planting can adjust to your ground, and no single disease is likely to take every tree. Phil Rutter, a breeder the book profiles, raises chestnuts, hazels and hickories from seed as a <Term id="hybrid-swarm">hybrid swarm</Term> crossed from several species, culls hard in every generation, and warns that planting only <Term id="clone">clones</Term> is a trap.

A middle road, from Roger Leakey's work in the tropics: locals can nearly always point to the best tree nearby. Copy that <Term id="plus-tree">plus tree</Term> by cuttings or grafts, village by village, and each place keeps its own diversity.

In practice: plant a few grafted, named trees beside some seedlings, and watch the best nut tree near you.

## When they bear

Big nut trees take many years to give a first crop. Choose early-bearing varieties where they exist, and grow annual crops between the young trees in the meantime. [Carbon farming](/guides/carbon-farming/) sets out this and the other limits.

**Every year, or every other year?** Chestnuts crop every year, which many nuts from cold places do not, and so do good hazel varieties. Pistachio and most pecans swing between heavy and light years: <Term id="alternate-bearing">alternate bearing</Term>. Nut pines give a good crop only every few years, and in cold climates so do most oaks.

**Pollination.** Ken Asmus, an oak breeder the book quotes, blames many thin acorn crops on poor pollination and plants several oaks together. Plant more than one variety of any nut.

**Storage.** Starchy nuts keep poorly. Chestnut growers once kept the harvest as flour or candied nuts, or fattened pigs on it.

## Nuts as a hedge

Hazels grow naturally as many-stemmed shrubs. At Badgersett, a research farm in the United States, they are planted as close hedgerows and cut to the ground about every ten years; the stumps sprout again and the hedge returns to bearing.

## Where nut trees fit

<Points>
- **Natives first.** Almost every region has nut trees of its own; the book notes there is an oak for nearly every place outside the tropics. Before planting one from elsewhere, check your local invasive-plant list.
- **Among field crops.** Chestnuts, walnuts and pecans are grown with annual crops between their rows until the shade closes over. See [Trees among field crops](/guides/trees-in-the-field/).
- **With animals.** In the oak pastures of Spain and Portugal, a form of <Term id="silvopasture">silvopasture</Term>, pigs feed on fallen acorns. See [Feeding animals from trees](/guides/trees-for-livestock/). The book warns that some growers who graze under nut trees find manure in with the gathered nuts, a real risk to food safety.
- **Next to other plants.** Some walnuts hold back what grows near them; the book's example is black walnut, which releases a natural weedkiller (<Term id="allelopathy">allelopathy</Term>).
- **In the far north.** In high boreal places, the author argues, dark evergreen pines may warm the climate more than they cool it, soaking up sunlight that snow would reflect (<Term id="albedo">albedo</Term>). See [Carbon farming](/guides/carbon-farming/).
</Points>

## Nuts that need care

<Points ruled="accent">
- **Acorns.** Most are full of bitter tannins, which have to come out before acorns are food. Factories do this in Korea and China; elsewhere it is slow hand work this guide does not describe. A few oaks are low in tannin, the book says, such as encina, burgambel and Emory oak; treat every acorn with the same caution.
- **Ginkgo.** Sources the book cites call the nuts harmful eaten in quantity, and poisonous raw even in small amounts. Being common food in Asia does not make them safe: see the caution below.
- **Mulberry-family nuts.** Ramon, breadnut, African breadnut and jackfruit seeds are not eaten as picked, in the book's account.
- **Cashew.** The shell holds an oil that burns the skin and, the book adds, can cause stomach illness and jaw paralysis. Shelling is careful work the book does not teach. Never crack raw cashews at home.
- **Allergy.** The book notes that allergies come with all nuts. If anyone in your household reacts to nuts, ask a doctor before making them a daily food. {/* general tip, not from the book: the doctor clause */}
</Points>

<FoodCaution risk={["needs-processing", "cashew"]} />

<Safety title="Spiny burrs">
Chestnuts fall in spiny burrs. Gather them in thick gloves and sturdy shoes.
</Safety>

## Pick nut trees for your climate

The book sorts climates by winter cold and calls this only a rough start. Begin with your own region's nut trees.

<ForClimate zones="tropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Hot, frost-free places, from humid to those with a long dry season.

- **Ramon (Mayan breadnut)**: a lowland tree that copes with long dry seasons and sea spray. See the caution above.
- **Breadnut** (*Artocarpus camansi*): a seeded relative of breadfruit for humid lowlands, among the book's heaviest-bearing nuts, grown as tall shade over other crops. See the caution above.
- **African breadnut**: for the humid tropics, in sun or shade; its huge fruit is packed with small nuts, and the author thinks it may out-yield every other protein crop. See the caution above.
- **Brazil nut**: only in big, forest-like plantings. It sets nuts only where its wild orchid bees thrive, and plantations of Brazil nut alone have failed.
- **Cashew**: for tropics with long dry seasons; the author reports a first crop within eighteen months, and it coppices. See the cashew caution above.

<Safety title="Falling fruit">
An African breadnut fruit can be half a metre across, and a falling one can kill; Brazil nuts drop from the tops of forest giants. Plant both well away from houses, paths and play areas, and keep people out from under them while the fruit is falling.
</Safety>
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="subtropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Mild winters with light frost.

- **Bunya**: a large Australian tree whose huge cones hold chestnut-sized starchy nuts.

For the tropical highlands (the book's own category):

- **Macadamia**: only where frost never comes; its dense evergreen shade suits coffee.
- **Andean walnut**: a nut and timber tree of the high Andes.
- **Chicalaba oak**: a cloud-forest oak with perhaps the biggest acorns of any oak, bearing fairly regularly at a Mexican farm the book cites; they need processing like any acorn.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="mediterranean" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Winter rain and a dry summer.

- **Encina (holm oak)**: grafted sweet selections bear heavily most years and are the backbone of the Iberian oak pastures. The book calls good varieties largely free of bitterness; the acorn caution above still applies.
- **Italian stone pine**: the highest-yielding nut pine in the book, though it rests for a few years after a heavy crop.
- **European chestnut**: the book places it from warm temperate to Mediterranean climates; it bears every year.
- **Pistachio**: needs long, dry summers and some winter chill; heavy and light years alternate.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="temperate" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "warm temperate", which takes in cool-summer places such as southern Britain.

- **Persian walnut**: choose varieties that bear along their side shoots; they bear years before the old tip-bearing kinds and stay smaller.
- **Pecan**: newer grafted varieties bear early, though most crop every other year.
- **Turkish tree hazel**: the hazel that grows as a tree rather than a shrub; good varieties bear every year.
- **Ginkgo**: an old Asian nut tree that the book places from cold temperate to subtropical climates. See the ginkgo caution above.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="continental" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "cold temperate": long, hard frosts.

- **Chestnut**: often the author's first suggestion for cold, humid places; the hardiest kinds reach the cold end of this band.
- **Hybrid and neohybrid hazels**: bred for cold and disease resistance. Grow them as a hedge.
- **Heartnut**: the book's pick among walnuts for cold-winter places, with kernels that come out of the shell easily.
- **Burgambel oak**: a cross of two tough oaks, with less tannin than most acorns, and early to bear for an oak.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="boreal" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Very cold winters, including some mountain places. The pines here are evergreen: see [the albedo point](#where-nut-trees-fit).

- **Siberian stone pine**: perhaps the hardiest staple anywhere, the author suggests; heavy crops come only every few years.
- **Korean pine**: improved varieties are grown on Korean hillsides among other crops; good crops come every other year.
- **Siberian and Manchurian hazels**: shrubs hardy into the boreal zone.
- **Butternut**: a North American walnut hardy into the boreal zone, on trial in Estonia.
</ForClimate>
