Compost tea is the liquid tool in Jeff Lowenfels and Wayne Lewis's way of caring for soil, beside compost and mulch. It can go wrong in ways a heap of compost cannot, so this guide starts with the risks.

<Safety title="Never on anything you will eat">
Compost tea can carry bacteria such as E. coli that make people ill, especially a brew that has lost its air or contains manure or added sugars. Keep it off vegetables, fruit, herbs and the soil they grow in, and keep it away from children's hands and mouths.
</Safety>

Lowenfels and Lewis themselves list keeping tea off food crops as one way to avoid E. coli; this guide makes it the rule.

<FoodCaution risk="compost-tea" />

<QuickAnswer>
Compost tea is good compost bubbled in water with an air pump, so its microbes pass into the liquid, which is poured on soil or sprayed on leaves. The authors credit it with protecting plants from disease; those are their claims. A brew that loses its air, or holds manure, can grow bacteria such as E. coli, so this guide keeps tea off anything you will eat. Compost and mulch carry the same life, only more slowly.
</QuickAnswer>

## What compost tea is, and what it is not

<Term id="actively-aerated-compost-tea">Actively aerated compost tea</Term> is finished compost held in water while air bubbles through it without a break. The bubbles work microbes loose; in the liquid they multiply, and the constant air keeps the brew <Term id="aerobic">aerobic</Term>.

Why not just use compost? In the authors' view, compost and mulch are heavy, slow to reach roots, and never stay on a leaf. Tea can be sprayed on foliage, where, they say, its microbes take the room on the leaf surface, the <Term id="phyllosphere">phyllosphere</Term>, before disease organisms can.

Several liquids are called tea. Two sit in water with no air and turn <Term id="anaerobic">anaerobic</Term>: that is where the danger lies.

| Liquid | How it is made | What the authors say |
|---|---|---|
| Leachate | Liquid that drains out of compost | Some colour and nutrients, hardly any life: the microbes stay stuck to the compost. |
| Still-water extract | Compost left in water with no air | Anaerobic, can carry disease organisms and alcohols; unsafe in their judgement. |
| Manure tea | A bag of manure hung in water | Anaerobic; by their account it all but guarantees E. coli. |
| Aerated compost tea | Compost in water with constant bubbling | The only one they make; safe, in their view, only while it stays aerobic. |

<Safety title="Don't make manure tea or still-water extracts">
Manure hung in water, or compost left in still water without air, turns anaerobic: the authors say manure tea all but guarantees E. coli and call extracts unsafe. Don't make them, and don't use one someone gives you.
</Safety>

{/* general tip, not from the book */}
Older garden traditions call the risky liquids "tea" too: when a source says compost tea, check which one it means.

## How a brewer works

The authors brew in a plain bucket. This is how it works, so you can judge a set-up; it is not a recipe.

- **Air.** An aquarium-type pump outside the bucket feeds a tube to a bubbler on the bottom: an air stone, or a coil of drip-irrigation soaker hose for wider bubbling. For the authors, air is the whole method: they prefer two outlets or two pumps to one, and warn that extremely fine bubbles can shred microbes.
- **Compost.** It hangs in a porous bag, so the tea needs no straining. It must be finished, smell good, hold no chemical residues and never have overheated: poor compost, poor tea. Worm compost gives a strongly bacterial tea. See [Compost that feeds soil life](/guides/compost-for-soil-life/).
- **Water.** Chlorine and preservatives harm microbes. The authors bubble chlorinated tap water on its own first so the chlorine escapes, and filter out chloramines with carbon or reverse osmosis.
- **Place.** Mild, steady warmth, out of direct sun (it kills microbes), where foam and spills do no harm. The authors use an aquarium heater in cold spots and ice in hot ones.

<Safety title="Mains power next to water">
The pump, and any heater, run on mains power beside a bucket that foams and spills. Keep the pump, plugs and cables dry and above any spill, and unplug everything before you touch the water or the bubbler.
</Safety>

For a bought brewer, the authors want the maker's laboratory test showing it extracts fungi as well as bacteria, and buy nothing without one.

## Additives, and why to leave them out

In the authors' system, food added to a brew sets its character: sugars such as molasses or fruit juice feed bacteria; seaweed, humic acids, rock dust and some fruit pulps feed fungi; fish products are on their list too.

Yet their own advice against E. coli is to leave out manure and the added foods and brew compost alone. This guide puts that first: **if you brew, brew compost and water only.**

For a more fungal tea, the authors give fungi a head start: moist compost mixed with an oat- or soy-based food, kept warm and dark until white threads, <Term id="hyphae">hyphae</Term>, cover it.

<Safety title="Mould spores and dusts">
Warm, moist compost fed to grow fungi also grows moulds, and rock dust and powdered additives are dusty. Open and handle them outdoors, keep your face away, and skip this step if you have asthma or a weak immune system.
</Safety>

Root fungi, if used, go in only as a brew ends, the authors say, since brewing destroys them: see [Mycorrhizal fungi](/guides/mycorrhizal-fungi/).

## Good brew, bad brew

<Points ruled="accent">
- **A good brew.** Coffee-brown, a touch warmer than at the start, smelling sweet and earthy. Some foam is normal: worm proteins from the compost.
- **A bad brew.** A sour, rotten or unpleasant smell means it has lost its air. Pour it away from plants, and far from anything you grow to eat.
- **Use it fresh.** Once the bubbling stops, the authors say, microbes soon use up the air and food, so tea is used at once; bubbling or cold storage only slows the decline. Never keep it in a fridge or container used for food. {/* general tip, not from the book */}
- **Bought tea.** Ask for test results and smell it first; it can go bad on the shelf.
</Points>

## Where the authors use it, and where this guide stops

The authors drench soil and spray leaves on lawns, trees, shrubs and flowers: just after leaf-fall, so fallen leaves keep rotting through winter, again just before leaf-out, and as soon as disease shows. Unlike soluble fertiliser, they say, it does not burn roots or leaves.

This guide draws a firmer line: only on plants nothing will be eaten from, such as lawns, ornamental beds, and shrubs and trees that bear no food you harvest. Never on vegetable beds, fruit trees, berry bushes, herbs or any edible perennial, neither their leaves nor their soil. A drench under a fruit tree counts as a food crop.

Apply it gently: the authors find forceful spraying kills the microbes, so they use low pressure and big drops. Work out of strong sun, use plastic cans (they avoid metal), and a sprayer that has never held weedkiller or pesticide.

<Safety title="Don't breathe the spray">
Spraying tea makes a fine mist that can carry its bacteria. Keep people and pets away while you spray, don't spray in wind or toward food plants, neighbours or open windows, and don't breathe the mist.
</Safety>

Keep children and pets off a treated lawn until it has dried. {/* general tip, not from the book */}

## What the authors claim

The authors report fungal teas used against mildews, rusts, damping off and some root rots, and bacterial teas against several leaf spots. They also report effects on some soil grubs and on weeds: clover and couch grass, and chickweed and plantain, which they tie to excess nitrate. The book gives no trial data with these lists: read them as the authors' experience, not as cures. Another route to healthy plants: [Pests and diseases: selecting plants that cope](/guides/pests-without-sprays/).

## Cleaning up

Clean the bucket, bubbler and tubing as soon as the tea is out. Bacterial slime, a <Term id="biofilm">biofilm</Term>, clogs bubblers and gathers in the bucket's bottom seam. Wet, it hoses off; dried, it needs a household hydrogen peroxide product or a baking-soda solution.

<Safety title="Clean with gloves, away from the kitchen">
Slime in a brewer can hold the bacteria you don't want. Wear gloves, clean it outside rather than in a kitchen sink, and never use kitchen utensils or food containers for tea.
</Safety>

## Is it worth it?

Compost and mulch carry the same life as tea, the authors' own point, only more slowly and without the brewing risks. Tea adds equipment, cleaning and the hazards above; many gardens never need it. If you do brew: compost and water only, plenty of air, smell every batch, use it at once, and only where nothing is eaten. Otherwise, start with [Compost that feeds soil life](/guides/compost-for-soil-life/) and [Mulch](/guides/mulch/).
