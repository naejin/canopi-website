You have a seed packet in your hand, or a squash you loved, and you want to know whether its seed is worth keeping. This guide sorts seed into three kinds, tells you what the saved seed of each grows into, and shows the one check that matters most: does the plant make pollen?

<QuickAnswer>
For many crops, yes. Seed from an F1 hybrid grows well, but its plants are not all alike: they mix traits from the hybrid's two parents, which makes them a good start for a landrace. In Lofthouse's experience, hybrids of tomato, cucumber, squash, corn, melon, watermelon and spinach are fine to save from. He leaves out shop-bought hybrids of carrot, onion, beet, radish, sunflower and the cabbage family, which often pass a pollen defect to their seed, and he looks at the flowers before saving.
</QuickAnswer>

## Three kinds of seed

**Heirloom and open-pollinated.** An <Term id="heirloom">heirloom</Term> is an old variety that seed savers have kept the same for decades. <Term id="open-pollinated">Open-pollinated</Term> is the wider label: any variety pollinated by wind, insects or itself. Keep either from crossing with other varieties, and the plants grown from its seed look like this year's.

Lofthouse reads both words more critically. For him they describe varieties held apart from all others for fifty years or more. That long isolation, he argues, is what keeps them the same, and also what makes them slow to fit a new garden.

**F1 hybrid.** An <Term id="f1-hybrid">F1 hybrid</Term> comes from a seed company crossing two chosen parents, usually lines inbred for a long time. Every plant from the packet is much like the next. The packet says "F1".

**Landrace.** A <Term id="landrace">landrace</Term> is mixed on purpose: many types growing together, crossing freely, saved year after year in one place until the crop suits that place and its growers. Its seed grows into more of the same mix, never a copy of one plant. The [landrace gardening guide](/guides/landrace-gardening/) explains how to start one.

<Note>Lofthouse does not call his own crops heirlooms. He calls them heritage crops: grown the way crops were grown before anyone kept seed pure, crossing and changing as they go.</Note>

## What the saved seed grows into

Sow seed saved from an F1 and you grow the <Term id="f2">F2</Term>. The grandparents' traits are dealt out again, at random, so each plant gets its own hand: the colour of one grandparent, the shape of the other, a ripening time somewhere between. A few plants look like the hybrid you bought; most do not. That is all people mean when they say hybrid seed "doesn't come true".

For a seed saver this is good news. A seed company picked a hybrid's parents because they give fine offspring, so the grandchildren are often worth growing too. Grow them out, keep the plants you like, and you have a start. Lofthouse adds two cautions. Shop-bought hybrids hold less diversity than their F2 suggests, because both parents come from narrow, inbred lines. And a hybrid can pass on a harmful trait, such as the pollen problem below.

**What about hybrid vigour?** Lofthouse sees <Term id="hybrid-vigour">hybrid vigour</Term> as a partial recovery from the <Term id="inbreeding-depression">inbreeding depression</Term> of the parents. A hybrid outgrows its inbred parents, he points out, but that does not make it stronger than a population that was never inbred.

## The pollen problem: male sterility

Some shop-bought hybrids are <Term id="male-sterile">male sterile</Term>: they set seed but make no working pollen. Seed firms find this handy, because a plant that cannot pollinate itself is a cheap way to make sure every seed it bears is a cross.

The usual kind is <Term id="cytoplasmic-male-sterility">cytoplasmic male sterility</Term> (CMS). The fault does not sit in the genes that pollen carries. It sits in parts of the cell that only the mother passes on, so it does not dilute away: seed saved from a CMS plant stays sterile, generation after generation. A <Term id="restorer-gene">restorer gene</Term> can sometimes bring the pollen back, but you cannot tell from the packet, and Lofthouse finds it simpler to keep only plants that make pollen.

His lists come from his own garden. Some cabbage-family hybrids are made with <Term id="self-incompatible">self-incompatibility</Term> instead of CMS; he will use those once he has seen their flowers shed normal pollen. Find your crop in the index below.

## Check the flowers

A male-sterile plant looks perfectly healthy, so the only way to know is to look when it blooms.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Wait for flowers">Check plants grown from hybrid seed, from saved hybrid seed, or from any new seed you add to your patch.</Step>
  <Step title="Find the anthers">The <Term id="anther">anthers</Term> are the small parts that hold pollen. On a fertile flower they are plump and often dusty.</Step>
  <Step title="Look for pollen">Fresh pollen shows as a fine powder on the anthers. A hand lens helps with small flowers.</Step>
  <Step title="Pull the faulty ones">Remove plants with missing, shrivelled or misshapen anthers before they set seed.</Step>
  <Step title="Repeat each year">New seed can bring sterility back, so make the check part of every flowering season.</Step>
</Steps>

In carrots the sign is easy to see: on a male-sterile plant, the tiny flowers of the umbel usually have no working anthers.

## Using hybrids to start a landrace

Lofthouse finds hybrid spinach, melon, squash and tomato useful starting stock. Sow the saved seed beside other varieties, expect a wide range of plants, and expect a few male-sterile ones in crops prone to it. There is no hurry: you can choose the plants you want over the next seasons. [Will they cross?](/guides/will-they-cross/) tells you which of your varieties will mix.

If you love a hybrid or an heirloom exactly as it is, keep a packet of the original seed aside. Your saved seed can wander; the packet will not.
