Every crop ripens its seed in one of two places: in dry pods, husks and heads, or inside juicy fruit. Once you know which, the rest is a short routine. This guide takes you from picking to the moment the seed is dry enough to put away, crop by crop.

<QuickAnswer>
If the seed sits in dry pods or heads (beans, peas, corn, grains, lettuce, the cabbage family), let the plants dry, break them up, then sieve and winnow. Tomato and cucumber seed stays in its own juice in a jar until the jelly around each seed falls apart, which takes about three days for tomatoes in Lofthouse's routine; then add water and keep what sinks. Melon and watermelon seed only needs a rinse, and squash seed needs no jar at all. Whatever the crop, finish by drying the seed fast, spread out thin.
</QuickAnswer>

## Dry seed or wet seed?

Look at where the seed is when the plant is done.

- **Dry:** the seed rattles in a pod, a husk or a head that turns brown and brittle on the plant. Beans, peas, fava beans, corn, wheat, barley, rye, oats, lettuce, spinach, sunflowers, kale and the rest of the cabbage family, mustard, flax.
- **Wet:** the seed is buried in flesh or juice. Tomatoes, cucumbers, melons, watermelons, squash.

Dry seed is cleaned with your hands, your feet and the wind. Wet seed is cleaned with water. Both end the same way: spread out, dried hard, then stored.

## Dry seed

<Steps>
  <Step title="Dry">Leave the plants standing until pods and heads are fully dry. Everything that follows works best on brittle, crackling material.</Step>
  <Step title="Thresh">Break the pods or heads so the seed falls free. This is <Term id="thresh">threshing</Term>.</Step>
  <Step title="Screen">Pass the seed through a sieve or a piece of mesh to hold back stems and big bits, which is <Term id="screen">screening</Term>.</Step>
  <Step title="Winnow">Pour the seed slowly through a breeze or in front of a fan so the light <Term id="chaff">chaff</Term> blows off. That last step is <Term id="winnow">winnowing</Term>.</Step>
</Steps>

### Picking at the right time

Some crops drop their seed before the whole plant has dried, which is called <Term id="shattering">shattering</Term>. When Lofthouse sees it starting, he pulls the plants early and lays them on a tarp out of reach of rain and dew, then threshes once they are dry.

Seed drying on the plant can take a passing shower. A long wet spell is different: if weeks of rain are forecast, bring the plants in first and keep them somewhere dry and airy until you thresh. Damp spoils dry seed through mould.

Cut the plants with shears a little above the soil instead of pulling them up. Soil that comes in on the roots ends up among the seed, and it is hard to get out again.

### How hard to hit

Match the force to the pod. The tougher the pod, the harder you can hit.

For the bin, hold a bunch of dry plants and knock them against the inside wall until no more seed drops.

### Weeds out, chaff kept

A screen with the right mesh, or careful winnowing, also separates many weed seeds from your crop, because they differ in size or weight. Lofthouse's example is foxtail grass, which comes away easily from dry bush beans either way. Every weed seed you leave behind now is one you won't sow next spring.

Chaff that still holds a little seed is not waste: he feeds it to animals or scatters it over a patch of garden or wild ground.

### Grains and sunflowers

Wheat, barley, rye and oats follow the same four steps on a bigger scale.

For sunflowers, Lofthouse keeps plants whose seed comes loose easily, so he can rub it out of the head in the field with a gloved hand. He used to dry whole heads and lost them to mould in cool, damp autumns; loose seed spread on a sheet dries far faster.

## Wet seed

Wet seed often comes out while you are preparing the fruit for the table, so keep a jar or a colander next to the chopping board.

Some wet seeds wear a <Term id="gel-coat">gel coat</Term>, a jelly that keeps them from sprouting inside the fruit. For those crops you let the jelly rot off in a jar: that is <Term id="seed-fermentation">seed fermentation</Term>. Across the crops Lofthouse grows, the jar stage runs from nothing at all to about five days.

<Note>The jar is seed, not food. Label it, move it out of the kitchen, and make sure no one mistakes it for a drink or a sauce.</Note>

### Cucumber

Cucumber seed has a gel coat too. Treat it like tomato seed: a few days in the jar and the jelly comes away. Then rinse, keep what sinks, and dry.

### Melon

Melons and watermelons have hardly any jelly on their seed. Scoop the seed into a colander and rinse it straight away until it is clean. No jar needed.

### Squash

Some squash seed has a little jelly, but Lofthouse doesn't bother with a jar. He puts the seed in a colander and strips the pulp off with a jet of water. Whatever jelly stays on dries and blows away when he winnows.

### Finish: dry fast, then winnow

Wet seed must dry quickly and all the way through, or it moulds. Spread it in a single layer and stir it now and then. Once it is dry, winnow it: empty seed coats are light and fly off, leaving the good seed.

## When is seed ripe enough?

Earlier than you might think. In Lofthouse's experience, seed can grow well before its fruit is fully ripe, though it may make a less vigorous plant, and seed in a picked fruit keeps ripening for a while.

<HisGarden
  his="In his first years of growing muskmelons and moschata squash in a short Utah season, the fruit was still very immature when summer ended. He picked it anyway, left it to finish ripening inside for some months, then took the seed out, and it grew."
  you="With a long, warm season you can usually let fruit ripen fully on the plant. Where the season is short or frost comes early, pick what you have before the cold and let it finish indoors."
/>

The real danger is frost. Wet seed that freezes inside the fruit can be badly damaged, so bring in every fruit you want seed from before the first hard frost, ripe or not. Fully dried seed of temperate crops is not harmed by freezing.

{/* General tip, not from the book (which mentions germination tests without a method): no counts, times or rates. */}
Unsure whether a batch is any good? Run a <Term id="germination-test">germination test</Term>: sprout a counted handful on a damp cloth and see how many come up.

## How clean is clean enough?

Your seed doesn't need to look like the seed in a shop packet. Lofthouse's view is that seed is tough and the exact cleaning method matters little: a few bits of chaff sown along with it do no harm. What counts is that it is dry, free of mould, and mostly free of weed seed.
