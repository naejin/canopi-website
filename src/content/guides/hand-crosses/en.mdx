Want seed of a cross you chose yourself, say between two squash you love, or your own hybrid sweet corn? A hand cross means getting one plant's pollen onto another plant's flower before any other pollen gets there. This guide covers squash, corn and spinach, how to pick the two parents, and how to tell next year whether it worked. Each crop section stands on its own.

<QuickAnswer>
Keep stray pollen off the mother, then give her the father's. In squash, tape a male and a female bud shut the evening before they open, rub the pollen on next morning, close the female again and tie a ribbon to her stem. In corn, sow mother rows beside a father row and pull the mothers' tassels before they shed. In spinach, pull the mother variety's male plants before they flower. Squash and corn are the easiest crops to start with.
</QuickAnswer>

## Which crop to start with

Squash is the classic first cross. Its flowers are big enough to handle without tools, each one is either male or female, and one pollinated flower gives a fruit full of seed. Corn is just as rewarding: male and female flowers grow apart on the same plant, so both crops are <Term id="monoecious">monoecious</Term>, and one cob carries hundreds of kernels.

The hard end is a crop like chickpea. Its tiny flowers hold the male and female parts close together, and each try gives a seed or two at most.

The cabbage family has a shortcut. Its plants are <Term id="self-incompatible">self-incompatible</Term>: their own pollen can't fertilise them. Lofthouse's trick is to plant exactly one plant of each of the two varieties, side by side. Each can then only set seed with the other's pollen, with no handwork at all.

## Pick the two parents

Start by writing mother × father on a label. The mother is the plant you will take seed from; the father only gives pollen. Then choose the pair for what you want:

- **One new trait in a crop you like:** pick two parents alike in everything else. Looking back on a pea cross he made for pod colour, Lofthouse says he would now cross two snow peas, to avoid importing a heap of other traits.
- **A wide-open project:** pick two very different parents. He likes Hubbard × banana squash. The first generation is the <Term id="f1-hybrid">F1 hybrid</Term>; in the next, the <Term id="f2">F2</Term>, the grandparents' traits come back in every mix, and you keep what you like.
- **Not a cross you'll regret:** think about what the father could bring in. His popcorn began as a chance cross with a flour corn, and getting it to pop well again took him years. He would not make that cross on purpose.

If you can, give the father a trait the mother lacks and you can see, such as flower colour or a climbing habit. It will tell you later which offspring are true hybrids.

The female is easy to spot: a baby fruit sits right under the flower. In the male, the <Term id="anther">anthers</Term> carry the pollen; in the female, it has to land on the <Term id="stigma">stigma</Term>.

<Steps>
  <Step title="The evening before">Find a male and a female bud that will open next morning, one on each parent. Tape or clip each shut so no insect can get in.</Step>
  <Step title="Next morning">Bees often sleep inside squash flowers; check before you pick one. Pick the male flower, peel back its petals, open the female and rub the pollen over her stigma.</Step>
  <Step title="Close her again">Tape the female flower shut once more, so no bee adds other pollen.</Step>
  <Step title="Tie a ribbon">Loop a ribbon loosely round the fruit's stalk. At harvest, take seed only from ribboned fruit.</Step>
</Steps>

**No taping at all.** If any father will do, as long as it isn't the plant itself, keep removing one plant's male flowers. Every seed it sets then comes from another plant's pollen.

With corn you don't carry pollen yourself. The wind does that; your job is to take the mother's own pollen away, which is called <Term id="detasseling">detasseling</Term>.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Sow in a block">Sow two to four rows of the mother variety for each row of the father, side by side.</Step>
  <Step title="Pull the mothers' tassels">As each tassel appears on a mother plant, pull it out before it starts shedding pollen.</Step>
  <Step title="Check, and check again">Walk the mother rows often; tassels hide.</Step>
  <Step title="Harvest the mothers">Seed from the mother rows is your hybrid. Keep the father rows' seed apart.</Step>
</Steps>

One corn plant gives about 600 seeds in Lofthouse's count, so a small block of mothers can sow a big patch. He finds the hybrid ripens about halfway between its parents, so choose an earlier or later father to move it.

## Spinach

Spinach is <Term id="dioecious">dioecious</Term>: each plant is male or female, and the wind carries the pollen. So you cross it by taking males away.

<Steps>
  <Step title="Sow side by side">Sow variety A next to variety B.</Step>
  <Step title="Pull A's males">Before they flower, take out every male plant of A.</Step>
  <Step title="Save from A">Take seed only from A's plants: it is your A × B cross.</Step>
</Steps>

Some plants fit neither drawing. If you can't tell, pull it.

## Did it work?

A marker trait answers that: something the father shows and the mother doesn't, that turns up in her offspring. Beans have several good ones, from flower colour to where the seed leaves sit; the table in [Beans and legumes](/guides/beans-and-legumes/#spotting-a-natural-cross) pairs each with what a hybrid looks like and when to look.

In <Term id="self-pollinating">self-pollinating</Term> crops, Lofthouse finds one cross keeps throwing up new combinations for two to four generations. In his garden, a handful of hand-made tepary bean hybrids from a friend widened the seed colours across his whole tepary population.

## Record it

Write mother × father and the year on the seed jar. Lofthouse also publishes the parents of any hybrid he releases, so anyone can make it again.
