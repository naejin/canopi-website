Cloth, rope, baskets, dyes and soap can come from long-lived plants rather than from oil or yearly crops. Eric Toensmeier treats these non-food harvests as part of a carbon-storing farm (see [Carbon farming](/guides/carbon-farming/)), and at the simplest scale he pictures a household with its own firewood, a little fibre, some oil and soap from soapnut trees. Grow a few fibre, dye and soap plants among your other perennials, and learn the processing from local makers. Several of these plants are poisonous or burn the skin: keep them apart from food, children, animals and water.

## Where the fibre is

Plant fibre sits in five main places, and where it sits decides how you get it out.

<Figure>
<WhereTheFibreIs labels={["Seed floss", "Stem (bast)", "Leaf", "Trunk sheath", "Husk"]} alt="Five plant parts that give fibre: the fluffy floss around some seeds, the bast layer inside a stem, long fibres running along a leaf, the layered sheaths of a banana-like trunk, and the husk of a coconut." />
</Figure>

Perennial cotton and kapok wrap their seeds in soft floss. Ramie, nettle and dogbane carry a <Term id="bast-fibre">bast fibre</Term> under the bark of their stems. Sisal, New Zealand flax and yuccas run tough fibres along their leaves. Abacá, a banana relative, gives strong fibre from its trunk, and the husk of a coconut gives coir as a by-product of the nuts.

Bast fibre is freed by <Term id="retting">retting</Term>, letting the stems partly rot until the fibre comes away. Perennial cotton is cut back every year and grows again; in Brazil and Cameroon it is grown this way.

The hard part is the processing. Small farmers grow most of these crops, the author notes, and they often lack machines and separate the fibre by hand; ramie's gummy stems make home extraction especially hard. Find a local spinner or weaver before you plant a field.

<HisGarden book="carbon-farming-solution" his="His Japanese fibre bananas are killed to the ground by frost every winter and sprout again each spring." you="A hardy fibre banana can give you leaves and fibre even where winters freeze." />

<Safety title="Stinging nettle">
Stinging nettle <Invasive /> has hairs on its green leaves and stems that sting painfully. The book also calls it weedy: it spreads readily into edges and orchards. {/* general safety, not from the book */}Wear gloves and long sleeves to harvest it.
</Safety>

## Baskets and bark

Willow rods from a coppice are the classic basket material: see [Willow and poplar](/guides/coppice-and-bamboo/#willow-and-poplar). Some trees give a bark harvest without being felled, cork oak of the Mediterranean above all; other barks give tannins or fibre.

## Dyes and tannins

Hundreds of plants give dyes, and synthetic dyes are among the worst polluters, the author writes. The book names dye plants but gives no dye methods, and neither does this guide: learn from a dyer.

Indigo, the most famous dye plant, is a nitrogen-fixing shrub cut back every year; one kind is grown as a <Term id="contour-hedgerow">contour hedgerow</Term> (see [Keep rain and soil on your land](/guides/water-and-slopes/)). Tannins, used for leather and for dyeing, often come cheaply as leftovers: alder cones, pecan shells, walnut husks, and the waste from processing acorns. Black walnut husks give a dye too, but the tree's roots hold back some plants near it, an effect called <Term id="allelopathy">allelopathy</Term>.

Some shrubs and palms also give wax for candles and polish.

## Soap from trees

Soapnut trees, also called soapberries, are grown for washing: their fruits are full of natural soap, and shops now sell them as a green laundry detergent. They need no special tools to use. Toensmeier washes his own laundry with them and would like every farm and village to keep a few trees.

<Safety title="Soapnut and fish">
The same fruits poison fish and insects. Keep the fruits and their wash water out of ponds, streams and fish tanks. {/* general safety, not from the book */}Store them out of reach of children, who may take them for food.
</Safety>

Some oil trees give soap and candles as well, such as pongam <Invasive /> and Chinese tallow <Invasive />.

## Pesticide plants

Neem, tephrosia, pyrethrum and tree tobacco are among the plants grown for natural pesticides.

<Safety title="Plant pesticides are poisons">
The book gives no safe way to prepare these plants, and neither does this guide. It also lists contraceptive effects among neem's uses. {/* general safety, not from the book */}Many of them are poisonous to people, animals or fish as well as to insects. Keep neem products away from anyone who is or may become pregnant. Do not make sprays without training, and follow local rules. For pest control without poisons, see [Pests and diseases](/guides/pests-without-sprays/).
</Safety>

## Oils and resins that burn the skin

Some industrial crops are poisonous to eat, and a few to touch. The author describes a farm, Las Cañadas in Mexico, where castor <Invasive /> grows in a small planting mixed with food plants, perennial cotton and alder; such crops, he notes, have long been grown among food. His advice is to keep the harvest and processing of toxic crops separate from food.

<Safety title="Poisonous oils and saps">
- Castor <Invasive /> seeds contain ricin, a deadly poison.
- Tung and candlenut <Invasive /> nuts and oils are poisonous.
- The lacquer tree's raw sap brings on severe rashes, like poison ivy; harvesters often suffer badly.
- Petroleum plant <Invasive /> burns the skin and eyes, is toxic to eat, and runoff from it can foul well water; the author advises caution in planting it outside sub-Saharan Africa.
- Plant resins catch fire easily.

{/* general safety, not from the book */}At home, go further than a managed farm like Las Cañadas: plant these away from children, animals and food crops, never where their seeds could be mistaken for food, and wear protective gear to handle them. Store resins away from heat and flames.
</Safety>

## For your climate

The principles hold everywhere; the plants do not. Start with plants native to your region, and check every newcomer against your local invasive list. A few the book names:

<ForClimate zones="tropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
- **Perennial cotton**: cut back each year and regrown.
- **Abacá**: trunk fibre, for the humid tropics at middle elevations.
- **Indigo**: a nitrogen-fixing dye shrub.
- **Soapnut**: for washing; heed the fish warning.
- **Neem**: only with the pesticide warning.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="subtropical" source="carbon-farming-solution">
Mild winters with light frost.

- **Sisal**: leaf fibre; new plants come from its suckers.
- **Ramie**: a stem fibre whose leaves also feed pigs.
- **Soapnut**: soap from the fruit.
- **Tung**: an oil tree for wood finish, only with the warning above.

**For the tropical highlands:** New Zealand flax, for leaf fibre.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="mediterranean" source="carbon-farming-solution">
- **Cork oak**: its bark is stripped from the living tree about once a decade.
- **Spanish broom**: a stem fibre.
- **New Zealand flax**: leaf fibre.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="temperate" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "warm temperate", which includes cool-summer places such as southern Britain.

- **Stinging nettle** <Invasive />: a fine, linen-like fibre from a plant that takes partial shade.
- **Bayberry**: wax for candles.
- **Black walnut**: husks for dye; mind its effect on nearby plants.
- **Willow**: rods for baskets.
</ForClimate>
<ForClimate zones="continental" source="carbon-farming-solution">
The book's "cold temperate".

- **Dogbane hemp**: a North American stem fibre.
- **Common milkweed** <Invasive />: stem fibre, and floss that once filled life vests.
- **Western soapberry**: a hardy soapnut, for soap in dry or humid places.
- **Willow**: rods for baskets.
</ForClimate>
