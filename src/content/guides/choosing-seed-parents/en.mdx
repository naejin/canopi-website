Which plants should you keep seed from, and how many? Each jar of seed you fill picks the parents of next year's crop. This guide helps you pick them on purpose: which plants, how much seed from each, and how to keep what you love about a crop while it settles into your garden.

<QuickAnswer>
Keep seed from plants that did well with the care you normally give, and from many of them rather than one star plant. Take more from the best, less from the merely good. Decide on one or two traits that make the crop what it is for you, hold those steady, and let the rest vary. And know that your way of gardening is choosing parents too, whether you mean it to or not.
</QuickAnswer>

## Start with the plants that did well

In Lofthouse's experience, the plants most likely to do well in your garden next year are the children of the ones that did well there this year. That is the whole case for choosing <Term id="seed-parent">seed parents</Term> from your own patch.

How strict to be depends on how long you have grown the crop.

- **In the first years, be generous.** Lofthouse lets almost any plant that manages to ripen seed into the jar at this stage.
- **Once the crop is settled, be choosier.** Now you can favour the plants with the best harvest.

Pull a plant early if disease or pests hit it badly. Gone before it flowers, it sheds no pollen onto its neighbours. This is <Term id="culling">culling</Term>.

A bad year is still worth saving from. In his experience, fruit cut short by frost often holds seed that grows, though the plants may start slower. Lofthouse's moschata squash began that way, from green fruit he finished indoors. If you doubt a batch, run a <Term id="germination-test">germination test</Term> before you sow it.

## Take seed from many plants

It is tempting to save seed from the one finest plant. Don't. A single plant holds a small slice of the crop's diversity, and every plant next year would be its child.

Spread the choice instead. Keep seed from plants of different sizes, shapes, colours and ripening dates; this is <Term id="mass-selection">mass selection</Term>. Then weight it:

- **Strong plants with a great harvest:** lots of seed.
- **Good, not outstanding:** some.
- **Plants that struggled:** a little, or none.

Watch for plants that look nothing like the rest. In a <Term id="self-pollinating">self-pollinating</Term> crop such as beans or lettuce, an <Term id="off-type">off-type</Term> is often a natural cross, and Lofthouse finds its children tend to inherit the difference. Keep its seed apart and give it its own spot next year.

## Keep what defines the crop

A varied crop can still keep its character. Pick one or two traits that matter to you and select hard on those; let the rest wander.

- **Muskmelon:** Lofthouse holds his to a netted skin and orange flesh, and grows his green-fleshed melons in another field so the two stay apart.
- **Zucchini:** long, slim fruit, in whatever skin colour turns up.
- **Crookneck:** a yellow skin and a bent neck. Leaf shape, and whether the plant bushes or trails, he leaves to chance.

### Sister lines

Want two types of one crop? Grow them as <Term id="sister-lines">sister lines</Term>, a little apart, and choose seed for each on its own. Lofthouse's corn once went through one big mixing; now a new flour corn joins only his flour corn. With acorn and Delicata squash, he notes, one row would do: one type at each end.

### Lean on the ratio

You can tilt a crop without losing anything. When his butternut-type squash scattered into every shape, his market customers took to the long-necked ones. He now sows roughly nine long-necked seeds for each round one: long necks stay the norm, and the round types stay in the pool.

Beans offer the same lever: equal shares of each seed type keep them all, while sowing the jar as it comes lets the most productive types take over. See [Beans and legumes](/guides/beans-and-legumes/).

## Judge what you harvest

Choose by what the plant gave you. Look at the whole harvest: how the plant grew, the size, shape and colour of the fruit, how easily the knife or peeler goes through it, and how long it keeps. Keep seed only from fruit that was good on every count.

<Safety title="Never judge a plant by mouth in the garden">Don't put seeds, raw plant parts, or anything you can't name as food in your mouth to judge a plant. Some crops and their wild relatives carry natural toxins.</Safety>

If you also choose by flavour, do it at the table, never in the garden. Only judge fruit of a crop you already eat, such as squash, melons, tomatoes or cucumbers, prepared the way you always prepare it. First scoop each fruit's seed into its own labelled cup, then keep the cups of the fruit you enjoyed. Don't taste anything raw that you would normally cook, unripe fruit, leaves from plants going to seed, or seeds. If a fruit is bitter, stop eating it and throw its seed out too.

<FoodCaution risk="bitter-cucurbits" />

For crops whose seed is the food, such as beans, corn and grain, choose by growth and looks.

Ask others to help. Lofthouse gets seed back from cooks, friends and customers who kept the best of what they ate, so the crop comes to suit many people, not one.

Expect trade-offs:

- **Very small fruit can mean weak seed.** He once kept seed only from his smallest butternuts. The fruit kept badly, the seed grew weak plants, and he never shared that seed.
- **Fragrance can cost you fruit.** His muskmelons grew so soft and aromatic that he loses about a fifth before market. He takes the loss; you might not.
- **Some faults show.** Lofthouse finds that lettuce plants with thick, milky sap are the bitter ones, so he culls by looking. Judge seed lettuce by eye; don't taste plants that are going to seed.

## Your habits choose too

In Lofthouse's view, whatever you do to a crop every year becomes part of what its seed expects: selection happens whether you intend it or not. So settle how you want to garden first, then let the crop adapt to that.

The same pull works for you when you don't expect it. He never kept seed from tomatoes lying in mud, and years later found his vines arching to hold their fruit off the ground. Squash too hard to cut never reached his seed jar, and his squash became easy to slice.

## Things you can select for

If you can see it, you can select for it. A few aims from his garden:

- **Corn out of reach:** pheasants took the cobs on short plants, so he saved from tall ones. Later, with raccoons and skunks about, he saved only the cobs they left, and his plants grew stiffer with cobs set higher.
- **Sunflowers that hang their heads:** birds can't perch on a head facing the ground, and seed that rubs out easily dries fast.
- **Grain that stands:** he never saves seed from plants that fell over.
- **Earliness:** three short, frost-ended seasons in a row pushed his moschata squash to ripen sooner.

## Keep records light

Lofthouse puts all the seed of one crop in one jar, labelled with the crop and the year, and takes plenty of photos as the season goes. A planting map only pays off when look-alike sister lines grow side by side.
