Want a leafy vegetable you plant once and pick for years? In much of the world it grows on trees. This guide follows Eric Toensmeier's free manual *Trees with Edible Leaves* (with Erica Klopf): which trees suit your climate, how to keep them small and productive, and which ones can hurt you.

<QuickAnswer>
Dozens of trees and shrubs are grown for their young leaves and shoots, eaten as greens. Wherever they are grown, the method is the same: cut them back hard and often, as a coppice, a pollard or a hedge, so the leaves stay within reach and keep coming tender for months. Choose species for your climate, natives first. Most leaves must be cooked, and a few, such as chaya and cassava, are poisonous raw or undercooked: read [the warnings](#which-ones-are-dangerous) before you eat any.
</QuickAnswer>

## What counts as a leaf tree

The manual counts any woody plant that would grow taller than a person if left alone and whose leaves or tender shoots are eaten as a vegetable, not as a herb. Shrubs count, and so do cactus pads. It profiles about a hundred cultivated species, and far more grow wild.

Toensmeier finds leaf trees among the most nutritious vegetables there are. Once established they need little work and no digging, and their deep roots keep them leafy through dry spells.

Assume every leaf needs cooking unless a reliable source says otherwise for that species. That is the manual's own rule.

## Keep them low

Left alone, most of these trees give tender leaves briefly, then toughen. Cut hard, they push vigorous shoots that stay soft much longer. The manual reports months of picking from linden, Chinese toon and mulberry in cold gardens, against a few weeks from an unpruned tree, and, in the tropics, leaves long after the rains stop. A low tree is also safer: climbing a tall one to pick is how people get hurt.

- **Coppice** suits dense beds and the smallest gardens. The manual expects a yearly coppice to keep producing for decades.
- **Pollard** lifts the regrowth above grazing animals. Some pollards are a single knob on the trunk; others are a candelabra of several branches cut at the same height.
- **Hedge** is the easiest to pick: you take shoots every week or two and the hedge stays dense.

A fourth form, lopping, harvests the tips of a tall tree's branches, which means climbing.

In the subtropics, frost can do the cutting for you: moringa and baobab, among others, die back in a cold snap and regrow leafy from the base.

## Pick a handful for your climate

Start with natives if any are grown for leaves in your region. Before planting anything from elsewhere, check your local list of invasive plants: the manual flags several of its species as invasive or weedy, white mulberry and nopale among them. A few examples:

| Climate | Examples | Usual form |
|---|---|---|
| Cold winters | Linden (*Tilia cordata*), Chinese toon, white mulberry, edible-leaf goji, European beech | Coppice; beech and goji also as a hedge |
| Dry tropics and subtropics | Moringa, African moringa, nopale cactus, chaya | Coppice or pollard blocks; nopale in hedges or rows |
| Humid tropics and subtropics | Katuk, aibika, lettuce tree, sweet bitterleaf, hedge panax | Hedges and coppice, often in partial shade |

Chaya and katuk carry warnings: see [Which ones are dangerous](#which-ones-are-dangerous). The full catalogue is in the manual, linked under Sources.

## Fit them into your design

All of these can be drawn in a Canopi design:

- **Here and there.** In a small garden, single trees tucked between other plants, picked as you pass.
- **A block.** A dense bed of coppiced or pollarded trees, like a livestock fodder bank but for the kitchen. Spacings vary widely; one grower the manual quotes keeps plants at least about 60 cm (2 ft) apart in double, staggered rows, with paths of 1.5 to 1.8 m (5 to 6 ft).
- **A hedge.** By the door for daily picking, along a garden edge, or across a slope on the contour, where it also holds the soil.
- **Rows between beds.** Lines of leaf trees alternating with annual crops. Keep them low so they shade the beds less.
- **Under taller trees.** In a food forest or tropical home garden, shade-tolerant species fill the understorey: katuk, tea, linden, beech and hedge panax. The manual notes that leaf crops often grow bigger, softer leaves in some shade.

## Propagate them

- **Seed** is the most common route, and every seedling is a little different. Many species from cold climates need a cold spell first: sow in pots and leave them outside over winter. Some have <Term id="hard-seed">hard seed</Term> whose coat must be broken before it takes up water. The manual gives each species' treatment; some use acid or very hot water, so follow its notes with care.
- **Cuttings** come next: soft green shoots kept humid, or dormant hardwood. Each is a <Term id="clone">clone</Term> of the parent, the way to keep a good form.
- **Live stakes**, thick branches pushed straight into the ground, are the usual way in the tropics, for cassava and chaya.
- **Cactus pads, suckers and layers**: a nopale pad is left to dry in the shade, then set half into the soil; a rooted sucker can be dug up; a low branch pinned to the soil roots in place.

Some species have poisonous seed (see the table below). Label it, keep it with your seed stock, never in the kitchen.

## Care and harvest

1. **Prepare the site.** Match each species to your sun, soil and rainfall, and clear perennial weeds, grasses above all, before you plant.
2. **Protect young trees.** Fence or guard them against livestock and deer. A coppice always needs protection; a pollard only until its head is out of reach.
3. **Shape before you harvest.** In the manual's temperate experience, wait until the second year for a real harvest; spend the first on setting the coppice or pollard height. Tropical hedges can be picked sooner.
4. **Pick often, cut back less often.** Take young shoots as they come. In cold gardens, one hard cut each winter is enough, with an optional cut in midsummer for a fresh flush. Now and then, let a tree grow taller for a season to rebuild its reserves.
5. **Feed the heavy croppers** such as mulberry and moringa; many growers never feed at all.

With tree purslane, cut back each branch you pick from rather than leaving bare twigs, which invite disease.

## Which ones are dangerous

<FoodCaution risk="toxic-leaves" />

The manual lists species because people grow them, not because they are safe, and it flags the dangers below. Its cooking instructions are not repeated here: learn how a leaf is prepared from your food-safety authority and from people who eat it traditionally.

| Species | What the manual warns |
|---|---|
| Chaya | Leaves hold cyanide: poisonous raw or undercooked. Some varieties have stinging hairs whose sting lasts for days. |
| Cassava | Leaves hold cyanide and are eaten only after thorough cooking. |
| Tree cassava (nkweso) | Presumed to contain cyanide, like cassava. |
| Black plum (*Vitex doniana*) | Traces of cyanide; the leaves are cooked. |
| Potato tree (*Solanum erianthum*) | Leaves contain solanine and perhaps other alkaloids; reports that they can cause miscarriage. Berries reported toxic. |
| Spiral nightshade (*Solanum spirale*) | Its genus often has toxic solanine in the leaves. |
| Katuk | Drinking too much katuk juice has killed at least one person; the manual urges caution with heavy eating. |
| Tree purslane | Very salty and high in oxalic acid; builds up toxic nitrates under heavy chemical fertiliser. |
| Bush apple (*Heinsia crinita*) | Its scent may point to toxic coumarins. |
| Hoja santa | Contains safrole, a compound that has caused cancer in rodents. |
| Sicklepod | A strong laxative; older leaves are reported highly toxic to grazing animals. |
| Salang (*Claoxylon*) | Laxative; start with very little if it is new to you. |
| Chipilín | Seeds very toxic, and many relatives toxic. Leaves said to cause mild drowsiness, as are those of pito (*Erythrina berteroana*). |
| Coral bean, kumbha, *Pterocarpus santalinoides* | Seeds poisonous. |
| Balanites | The bark is used as a poison against the creatures that carry bilharzia and Guinea worm. |
| Cow-itch (*Urera baccifera*) | Stings far worse than a nettle, for days. Leaves eaten only thoroughly cooked. |
| Ora pro nobis | Spines break off in the skin and inflame it. |
| Tea (as the vegetable laphet) | Contains caffeine. |
| Lettuce tree | Sticky fruits can trap and kill birds. |
